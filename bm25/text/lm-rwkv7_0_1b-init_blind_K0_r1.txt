The cell is the basic unit of structure and function of all living things.
All living organisms are made of one or more cells; cell theory says new cells come from existing cells.
Unicellular organisms such as bacteria, amoeba, paramecium and euglena are made of a single cell.
Multicellular organisms such as plants, animals and humans are made of many cells.
Levels of organization: cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, organ systems form an organism.
A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together to perform a function.
An organ is made of different tissues working together, such as the heart, stomach, lungs, liver, leaf or root.
An organ system is a group of organs working together to perform a function.
The nucleus is the control center of the cell; it contains DNA, chromosomes and genetic information that directs cell activities.
The cell membrane surrounds the cell and controls what substances enter and leave the cell.
The cell membrane is selectively permeable and lets nutrients, water and oxygen in and wastes out.
The cell wall is a rigid outer layer that gives plant cells support, shape, structure and protection; it is made of cellulose.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole; animal cells do not.
Chloroplasts contain green chlorophyll and capture sunlight energy to make food (glucose) by photosynthesis.
Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell; they release energy from food (glucose) during cellular respiration.
Vacuoles store water, food, nutrients and wastes in the cell.
Cytoplasm is the jelly-like fluid inside the cell membrane where organelles are found.
Ribosomes make proteins in the cell.
Organelles are small structures inside cells that perform specific jobs.
Prokaryotic cells such as bacteria have no nucleus; eukaryotic cells have a nucleus.
Cells divide by mitosis to grow, repair damaged tissue and replace old cells.
Meiosis produces sex cells (gametes) such as sperm and egg cells with half the number of chromosomes.
Cells need energy, water, oxygen and nutrients and they produce waste such as carbon dioxide.
Specialized cells: nerve cells carry messages (impulses), muscle cells contract, red blood cells carry oxygen.
White blood cells fight infection, germs, bacteria and viruses.
Root hair cells absorb water and minerals; guard cells open and close stomata in leaves.
Nerve cells (neurons) carry electrical signals and messages between the brain and the body.
Skin cells protect the body; bone cells support the body.
Diffusion is the movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration.
Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a cell membrane.
A microscope magnifies very small objects such as cells, bacteria and microorganisms that cannot be seen with the eye.
A compound light microscope uses lenses and light to magnify cells; the objective lens and eyepiece magnify the specimen.
Robert Hooke first observed and named cells by looking at cork through a microscope.
Anton van Leeuwenhoek observed bacteria and microorganisms with his early microscope.
A hand lens or magnifying glass makes small objects such as insects, rocks and leaves look larger.
Microscope slides hold a specimen; a stain makes cell parts easier to see.
Photosynthesis: plants use sunlight energy, carbon dioxide and water to make glucose (sugar, food) and release oxygen.
Photosynthesis takes place in the leaves, in chloroplasts that contain chlorophyll.
Chlorophyll is the green pigment that absorbs light energy and makes leaves green.
Plants are producers because they make their own food from sunlight.
Plants take in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny openings called stomata in their leaves.
Plants release oxygen into the air as a product of photosynthesis.
Light energy from the Sun is changed into chemical energy stored in sugar by photosynthesis.
Plants need sunlight, water, air (carbon dioxide), nutrients and space to grow.
Plants do cellular respiration too, using oxygen to break down sugar for energy.
Roots anchor the plant in the soil and absorb water and minerals (nutrients) from the soil.
Roots hold soil in place and help prevent soil erosion.
The stem supports the plant and carries water and nutrients between the roots and the leaves.
Xylem carries water and minerals up from the roots; phloem carries sugar (food) from the leaves to the rest of the plant.
Leaves are the main organ where the plant makes food; they capture sunlight.
Flowers are the reproductive parts of a plant; they make seeds.
Flowers attract pollinators such as bees, butterflies, hummingbirds and bats with bright colors, scent and nectar.
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the stamen to the pistil of a flower.
The stamen is the male part of a flower; the anther makes pollen.
The pistil is the female part of a flower; it contains the stigma, style and ovary with ovules.
After fertilization the ovary develops into a fruit and the ovules develop into seeds.
A fruit protects the seeds and helps disperse (spread) them.
A seed contains a plant embryo and stored food (cotyledon) protected by a seed coat.
Seeds are dispersed by wind, water, animals and by exploding pods; burrs stick to animal fur.
Seeds with wings or fluffy parachutes like dandelions and maple seeds are spread by the wind.
Animals eat fruits and spread the seeds in their droppings.
Germination is when a seed begins to sprout and grow; seeds need water, warmth (temperature) and oxygen to germinate.
Plant life cycle: seed, germination, seedling, adult plant, flower, pollination, fruit and seeds.
Some plants reproduce asexually from bulbs, tubers, runners, cuttings or spores.
Ferns and mosses reproduce with spores instead of seeds.
Mosses are nonvascular plants without true roots, stems or leaves; they live in moist shady places.
Vascular plants have tubes (xylem and phloem) that carry water and food.
Conifers such as pine trees make seeds in cones and keep needles all year; they are evergreen.
Flowering plants (angiosperms) make seeds inside fruits; gymnosperms have naked seeds in cones.
Deciduous trees lose their leaves in autumn (fall) and are dormant in winter.
Leaves change color in autumn because chlorophyll breaks down when days get shorter and cooler.
Phototropism: plants grow or bend toward light.
Gravitropism (geotropism): roots grow down in response to gravity and stems grow up.
Thigmotropism: vines and tendrils respond to touch by wrapping around objects.
A tropism is a plant's growth response to a stimulus such as light, gravity, water or touch.
Transpiration is the loss of water vapor from the leaves of plants through stomata.
Wilting happens when a plant loses more water than it takes in; the plant needs water.
Cactus plants survive in the desert with thick waxy stems that store water, spines instead of leaves and shallow wide roots.
Spines on a cactus protect it from animals and reduce water loss.
Thorns protect plants such as roses from being eaten by animals.
Plants in shady rainforests have large broad leaves to capture more sunlight.
Water lilies have air spaces and floating leaves; aquatic plants live in water.
Fertilizer adds nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium to soil to help plants grow.
Plants grown without light become pale or yellow and weak.
Algae are simple producers that make food by photosynthesis in water; phytoplankton produce much of Earth's oxygen.
Trees provide shade, wood, fruit, oxygen and habitats for animals.
Bulbs such as onions and tulips store food underground; potatoes are tubers that store starch.
We eat plant parts: carrots and beets are roots, celery is a stem, lettuce and spinach are leaves, broccoli is a flower.
Apples, tomatoes, peppers, pumpkins and cucumbers are fruits because they contain seeds.
Grafting and cuttings make new plants identical to the parent plant.
Vertebrates are animals with a backbone (spine): fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.
Invertebrates are animals without a backbone: insects, spiders, worms, jellyfish, snails, clams, crabs, sponges, starfish, octopus.
Mammals have hair or fur, are warm-blooded, breathe with lungs, give birth to live young and feed their babies milk.
Mammals: dog, cat, cow, horse, human, whale, dolphin, bat, mouse, bear, elephant, rabbit, deer, squirrel, lion, wolf, seal, monkey.
Whales, dolphins and porpoises are mammals that live in the ocean and breathe air through blowholes.
Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly.
Birds have feathers, wings, beaks and hollow bones, are warm-blooded and lay hard-shelled eggs.
Birds: eagle, hawk, owl, robin, penguin, ostrich, duck, sparrow, crow, pigeon, chicken, parrot, hummingbird.
Reptiles have dry scaly skin, are cold-blooded, breathe with lungs and lay leathery eggs on land.
Reptiles: snake, lizard, turtle, tortoise, alligator, crocodile, iguana, gecko.
Amphibians have moist smooth skin, are cold-blooded, live in water when young and on land as adults.
Amphibians: frog, toad, salamander, newt; they lay jelly-like eggs in water.
Fish live in water, breathe with gills, have scales and fins and are cold-blooded.
Fish: shark, salmon, trout, tuna, goldfish, catfish, bass, cod.
Gills take in dissolved oxygen from water; lungs take in oxygen from air.
Insects have six legs, three body parts (head, thorax, abdomen), antennae and usually wings; an exoskeleton.
Insects: ant, bee, butterfly, moth, beetle, grasshopper, fly, mosquito, ladybug, dragonfly, cricket, termite.
Spiders (arachnids) have eight legs and two body parts; scorpions and ticks are arachnids.
Arthropods have jointed legs and an exoskeleton: insects, spiders, crabs, lobsters, shrimp.
An exoskeleton is a hard outer covering that protects and supports the body; it is shed by molting.
Cold-blooded (ectothermic) animals have body temperature that changes with the environment.
Warm-blooded (endothermic) animals keep a constant body temperature.
Complete metamorphosis: egg, larva, pupa, adult; a caterpillar (larva) forms a chrysalis or cocoon (pupa) and becomes a butterfly or moth.
Incomplete metamorphosis: egg, nymph, adult; grasshoppers, dragonflies and crickets.
Frog life cycle: egg, tadpole with gills and tail, froglet, adult frog with lungs and legs.
Life cycles: animals are born, grow, develop, reproduce and die.
A larva such as a caterpillar or maggot looks different from the adult.
Mammal babies develop inside the mother; birds and reptiles hatch from eggs.
Instinct (inherited, innate behavior) is behavior an animal is born knowing, such as spiders spinning webs, birds building nests, migration and hibernation.
Learned behavior is acquired through experience or by being taught, such as a dog doing tricks or a child reading.
Hibernation is a deep sleep in winter that helps animals such as bears, bats and groundhogs survive cold weather and lack of food.
Migration is moving long distances with the seasons to find food, warmer weather or places to breed; birds, whales, butterflies.
Monarch butterflies and geese migrate south in the fall for warmer weather and food.
Camouflage helps an animal blend in with its surroundings (environment) to hide from predators or prey.
Animals that change color, such as chameleons and octopuses, use camouflage to hide from predators.
White fur helps polar bears and arctic foxes hide in snow; brown fur matches the forest.
Mimicry is when a harmless animal looks like a dangerous or poisonous one to protect itself.
Warning coloration: bright colors on poison dart frogs and some insects warn predators that they are poisonous.
Thick fur, blubber and fat keep animals warm in cold arctic and polar climates; insulation.
Blubber is a thick layer of fat that insulates whales, seals and walruses in cold water.
Desert animals such as camels and kangaroo rats conserve water; camels store fat in their humps.
Large ears help desert animals such as fennec foxes and jackrabbits release body heat and stay cool.
Many desert animals are nocturnal and active at night when it is cooler.
Nocturnal animals such as owls and bats are active at night; owls have large eyes to see in dim light.
Webbed feet help ducks, frogs and otters swim.
Sharp claws, sharp teeth and keen eyesight help predators such as hawks, eagles, lions and wolves catch prey.
Sharp teeth (canines) tear meat; flat teeth (molars) grind plants.
Beak shape is an adaptation to the food a bird eats: hooked beaks tear meat, long thin beaks reach nectar, strong short beaks crack seeds.
Long necks help giraffes reach leaves high in trees.
Streamlined bodies and fins help fish and dolphins swim fast.
Shells protect turtles, snails and clams; quills protect porcupines; skunks spray a bad smell.
Living in herds, schools or flocks helps protect animals from predators.
Hollow bones and feathers help birds fly.
Adaptations are inherited traits or behaviors that help an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
Structural (physical) adaptations are body parts; behavioral adaptations are actions such as migration.
Animals need food, water, air (oxygen), shelter and space to survive.
Earthworms loosen and aerate the soil and add nutrients; they help plants grow.
Animals get energy by eating plants or other animals.
Bees make honey from nectar and pollinate flowers.
Ants and bees live in colonies and work together.
Animal senses: smell, hearing, sight, taste and touch help animals find food and avoid danger.
Whiskers help cats feel their way in the dark.
Echolocation: bats and dolphins use echoes of sound to find food and navigate in the dark.
Antlers, horns and bright feathers help male animals compete for mates.
Fish lay many eggs because many are eaten; mammals have few young and care for them.
Snakes shed their skin as they grow; they smell with their tongues.
Animals with a backbone have an internal skeleton (endoskeleton).
Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called coral polyps.
Animal classification uses kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
A species is a group of organisms that can mate and produce fertile offspring.
Scientific names use genus and species, such as Homo sapiens.
A dichotomous key is used to identify and classify organisms by their characteristics.
Organisms are classified into kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi, protists and bacteria (monerans).
Fungi such as mushrooms, molds and yeast cannot make their own food; they absorb nutrients and are decomposers.
Yeast is a fungus used to make bread rise; it produces carbon dioxide gas.
An ecosystem is all the living (biotic) and nonliving (abiotic) things in an area interacting.
Biotic factors are living things such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are nonliving things such as sunlight, water, air, soil, rocks, temperature and climate.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives and finds food, water and shelter.
A niche is the role or job of an organism in its ecosystem.
A population is all the organisms of one species living in an area.
A community is all the populations of different species living together in an area.
The Sun is the source of energy for almost all food chains and ecosystems.
A food chain shows how energy passes from one organism to another when it is eaten.
Food chain order: sun, producer (plant, grass), primary consumer (herbivore), secondary consumer (carnivore), decomposer.
Example food chain: grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, hawk.
Example food chain: algae, small fish, large fish, bird; phytoplankton, zooplankton, fish, seal, shark.
A food web is many overlapping food chains in an ecosystem.
Arrows in a food chain show the direction energy flows, from the food to the eater.
Producers such as plants, grass, trees and algae make their own food using energy from sunlight.
Consumers cannot make their own food; they eat other organisms for energy.
Herbivores eat only plants: rabbits, deer, cows, horses, grasshoppers, caterpillars, giraffes, elephants.
Carnivores eat only other animals (meat): lions, wolves, hawks, eagles, sharks, snakes, owls, tigers.
Omnivores eat both plants and animals: humans, bears, raccoons, pigs, chickens.
Predators hunt and kill other animals for food; the animals they eat are called prey.
Predator and prey: owl and mouse, hawk and rabbit, wolf and deer, lion and zebra, snake and mouse, fox and rabbit.
When a predator population decreases, the prey population increases; more prey means more predators.
Scavengers such as vultures, hyenas and crows eat dead animals.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi, mushrooms and worms break down dead plants and animals and wastes.
Decomposers return nutrients to the soil so plants can use them again; they recycle matter.
Without decomposers, dead organisms and wastes would pile up and nutrients would not be recycled.
Energy pyramid: producers have the most energy; only about ten percent of energy passes to the next level.
Most energy is lost as heat at each level of a food chain, so there are fewer top predators.
Matter cycles through ecosystems, but energy flows through and is lost as heat.
Competition happens when organisms need the same limited resources such as food, water, space, light or mates.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space, shelter, disease and predators limit population size.
Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support.
Symbiosis is a close relationship between two different species.
Mutualism: both organisms benefit, such as bees and flowers, or clownfish and sea anemones.
Commensalism: one organism benefits and the other is not helped or harmed, such as barnacles on a whale.
Parasitism: a parasite benefits and the host is harmed, such as ticks, fleas, lice, tapeworms and mosquitoes.
Invasive (non-native) species are introduced to a new area, have no natural predators and outcompete native species.
Extinction happens when all members of a species die out; dinosaurs and dodo birds are extinct.
Endangered species have very small populations and are at risk of extinction.
Habitat loss from cutting forests, building cities and farming is the main threat to many species.
Deforestation is the cutting down of forests; it destroys habitats and increases erosion and carbon dioxide.
Biodiversity is the variety of different species in an ecosystem; high biodiversity makes ecosystems stable.
Ecological succession is the gradual change of a community over time, such as after a forest fire or on bare rock.
Pioneer species such as lichens and mosses are the first to grow on bare rock and help form soil.
Lichens are a partnership of fungi and algae that break down rock.
Biomes are large regions with similar climate, plants and animals.
Desert biome: very little rainfall, hot days, cold nights, cactus, camels, lizards, snakes, sand.
Tundra biome: very cold, frozen ground (permafrost), few trees, mosses, lichens, caribou, arctic foxes.
Tropical rainforest biome: hot, wet, heavy rainfall all year, tall trees, greatest biodiversity, monkeys, parrots.
Temperate deciduous forest: four seasons, trees lose their leaves in fall, deer, squirrels, bears.
Grassland (prairie, savanna): grasses, few trees, moderate rainfall, bison, zebras, prairie dogs.
Taiga (boreal forest): cold winters, coniferous evergreen trees, moose, wolves.
Ocean (marine) biome: salt water, fish, whales, coral reefs, plankton, kelp.
Freshwater ecosystems: lakes, ponds, rivers, streams, wetlands, marshes, swamps.
Wetlands filter water, prevent floods and provide habitats for birds, frogs and fish.
Estuaries are where fresh river water meets salt ocean water.
Coral reefs are found in warm shallow clear ocean water.
Deep ocean has no sunlight, so there are no plants; animals depend on food falling from above.
Changes in ecosystems: floods, fires, droughts, volcanoes, disease and human activity affect populations.
A drought is a long period with little rain; plants die and animals must move or die.
Organisms that cannot adapt to changes in the environment may move away or die out.
Overpopulation of deer can happen when predators such as wolves are removed.
Cycles in nature: water cycle, carbon cycle, nitrogen cycle, rock cycle.
Carbon cycle: plants take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis; animals release carbon dioxide during respiration; burning fuels releases carbon dioxide.
Nitrogen cycle: bacteria in soil and on plant roots fix nitrogen gas into forms plants can use.
Oxygen cycle: plants release oxygen; animals breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide.
The circulatory system (heart, blood, blood vessels) carries oxygen, nutrients and wastes throughout the body.
The heart is a muscle that pumps blood through the body.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart; capillaries are tiny blood vessels.
Red blood cells carry oxygen; white blood cells fight infection; platelets help blood clot.
Blood carries oxygen from the lungs and nutrients from the small intestine to the cells.
The respiratory system (lungs, trachea, bronchi, diaphragm, nose) takes in oxygen and removes carbon dioxide.
In the lungs, oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out to be exhaled; alveoli are tiny air sacs.
Breathing: the diaphragm muscle contracts to pull air into the lungs; we inhale oxygen and exhale carbon dioxide.
The digestive system (mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver) breaks down food into nutrients.
Digestion begins in the mouth where teeth chew food and saliva starts to break it down.
The stomach uses acid and enzymes to break down food; the esophagus carries food from the mouth to the stomach.
Nutrients are absorbed into the blood in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs water.
The liver makes bile to digest fats and filters the blood; the pancreas makes digestive enzymes and insulin.
The nervous system (brain, spinal cord, nerves) controls the body, carries messages and responds to stimuli.
The brain is the control center of the body; it processes information from the senses, thinking and memory.
The spinal cord carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body.
Reflexes are fast automatic responses controlled by the spinal cord, such as pulling a hand away from a hot stove.
The five senses: eyes see (sight), ears hear (hearing), nose smells, tongue tastes, skin feels touch.
The eye: light enters through the pupil, the lens focuses it on the retina, and the optic nerve sends signals to the brain.
The skeletal system (bones) supports the body, protects organs, makes blood cells and allows movement with muscles.
The skull protects the brain; the ribs protect the heart and lungs; the spine protects the spinal cord.
Joints are where bones meet; ligaments connect bones to bones; tendons connect muscles to bones; cartilage cushions joints.
Calcium from milk and dairy foods makes bones and teeth strong.
The muscular system: muscles contract and pull on bones to move the body; muscles work in pairs.
Three types of muscle: skeletal (voluntary), smooth (organs), cardiac (heart).
The excretory (urinary) system (kidneys, bladder) removes liquid wastes; the kidneys filter blood and make urine.
Sweat glands in the skin remove waste and cool the body; the lungs excrete carbon dioxide.
The immune system (white blood cells, antibodies) defends the body against germs, bacteria, viruses and disease.
Skin is the largest organ; it protects the body from germs, keeps water in and helps control temperature (integumentary system).
The endocrine system uses glands and hormones as chemical messengers to control growth, development and body processes.
Hormones such as insulin and adrenaline are chemical messages (signals) carried by the blood from glands to cells.
The reproductive system produces offspring; egg and sperm cells join during fertilization.
Puberty, growth and development are controlled by hormones.
Body systems work together: the respiratory and circulatory systems deliver oxygen to cells.
The digestive and circulatory systems work together to deliver nutrients to cells.
The nervous and muscular systems work together to move the body.
Homeostasis is keeping stable internal conditions such as body temperature; sweating cools the body and shivering warms it.
Normal human body temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius (98.6 degrees Fahrenheit).
Nutrition: a balanced diet includes proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, minerals and water.
Carbohydrates such as bread, rice, pasta and sugar give the body quick energy.
Proteins from meat, fish, eggs, beans and nuts build and repair muscles and tissues.
Fats store energy and insulate the body; too much fat can cause heart disease.
Vitamins and minerals from fruits and vegetables keep the body healthy; vitamin C prevents scurvy.
Fiber from fruits, vegetables and whole grains helps digestion.
Calories measure the energy in food.
Exercise makes the heart and muscles strong and increases heart rate and breathing rate.
During exercise the heart beats faster to deliver more oxygen to muscles; breathing rate increases.
Pulse (heart rate) can be measured at the wrist or neck in beats per minute.
Infectious diseases are caused by germs (pathogens) such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites and can spread from person to person.
Viruses cause colds, influenza (flu), chickenpox, measles, polio and AIDS (HIV); antibiotics do not work on viruses.
Bacteria cause strep throat, tuberculosis, food poisoning and infections; antibiotics kill bacteria.
Vaccines help the body make antibodies so it can fight a disease and become immune; they prevent diseases.
Washing hands with soap helps prevent the spread of germs and disease.
Covering coughs and sneezes prevents spreading germs.
Some bacteria are helpful: they help digest food, make yogurt and cheese and decompose dead matter.
Noninfectious diseases such as diabetes, cancer, heart disease and asthma are not spread by germs.
Diabetes is a disease in which the body cannot control blood sugar; insulin is used to treat it.
Smoking damages the lungs and can cause lung cancer and heart disease.
Alcohol and drugs damage the brain, liver and body.
Sunscreen protects the skin from ultraviolet rays that can cause sunburn and skin cancer.
Sleep and rest help the body grow and repair itself.
Medicines are tested in clinical trials to make sure they are safe and effective.
Louis Pasteur showed that germs cause disease and invented pasteurization; Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin.
Edward Jenner developed the first vaccine for smallpox.
Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to offspring through genes.
Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring in genes: eye color, hair color, height, skin color, blood type, freckles, dimples.
Acquired traits are learned or develop during life from the environment and are not inherited: scars, a broken arm, riding a bike, speaking a language, strong muscles from exercise, short hair from a haircut.
Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes that carry the instructions (code) for traits.
DNA is the genetic material in the nucleus that carries hereditary information; it has a double helix shape.
Chromosomes are made of DNA; humans have 23 pairs (46) of chromosomes.
Offspring get half of their genes from the mother and half from the father.
Dominant traits show up even if only one dominant allele is inherited; recessive traits show only with two recessive alleles.
A Punnett square predicts the probability of offspring traits from a cross of parents.
Genotype is the gene combination (alleles) such as Tt; phenotype is the physical trait that appears, such as tall.
Homozygous means two identical alleles (TT or tt); heterozygous (hybrid) means two different alleles (Tt).
Gregor Mendel studied pea plants and discovered the basic laws of heredity; he is the father of genetics.
Sexual reproduction needs two parents; offspring get a mix of genes and show variation, which helps a species survive changes.
Asexual reproduction needs only one parent; offspring are identical (clones) to the parent: budding, binary fission, runners, cuttings, spores.
Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission (splitting in two); yeast and hydra reproduce by budding.
Fertilization is the joining of a sperm cell and an egg cell to form a zygote.
A mutation is a change in DNA (a gene) that can be harmful, helpful or have no effect; mutations can be inherited.
Mutations can be caused by radiation, ultraviolet light and some chemicals.
Selective breeding (artificial selection) is when humans breed plants or animals with desired traits, such as dog breeds or crops.
Genetic engineering changes the DNA of an organism to give it new traits, such as crops that resist insects.
Cloning makes a genetically identical copy of an organism.
Variation: individuals of the same species have differences in traits.
Natural selection: organisms with helpful traits (adaptations) are more likely to survive and reproduce and pass those traits to offspring.
Evolution is the gradual change in species over many generations (long periods of time) through natural selection.
Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection after studying finches on the Galapagos Islands.
Darwin's finches have different beak shapes adapted to the different foods on each island.
Evidence for evolution: fossils, similar body structures (homologous structures), embryos and DNA.
The peppered moth became darker during the industrial revolution when soot darkened trees, because dark moths were hidden from birds.
Bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics through natural selection.
Organisms that are closely related share more similar DNA and body structures.
Traits can be influenced by both genes and the environment, such as height affected by nutrition.
Identical twins have the same DNA; fraternal twins do not.
Purebred organisms produce offspring with the same trait; hybrids come from parents with different traits.
Gametes (sex cells) have half the number of chromosomes made by meiosis.
Igneous rock forms when magma or lava cools and hardens (solidifies).
Granite, basalt, obsidian and pumice are igneous rocks.
Igneous rocks that cool slowly underground (intrusive) have large crystals, such as granite.
Igneous rocks that cool quickly on the surface (extrusive) have small crystals or are glassy, such as obsidian and basalt.
Pumice is a light igneous rock full of holes (gas bubbles) that can float on water.
Sedimentary rock forms when layers of sediment (sand, mud, clay, pebbles, shells) are compacted and cemented together.
Sandstone, limestone, shale, conglomerate and coal are sedimentary rocks.
Fossils are usually found in sedimentary rock layers.
Limestone forms from shells and skeletons of ocean organisms; it fizzes with acid (calcium carbonate).
Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by heat and pressure deep underground.
Marble (from limestone), slate (from shale), quartzite (from sandstone) and gneiss are metamorphic rocks.
The rock cycle shows how rocks change from one type to another through melting, cooling, weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction, heat and pressure.
Magma is melted rock below the surface; lava is melted rock that reaches the surface.
Minerals are naturally occurring nonliving solids with a crystal structure; rocks are made of minerals.
Mineral properties used to identify minerals: hardness, luster, streak, color, cleavage, fracture, density, crystal shape.
Hardness is a mineral's resistance to being scratched; the Mohs hardness scale goes from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond).
Diamond is the hardest mineral; talc is the softest; a fingernail scratches gypsum; quartz scratches glass.
Streak is the color of a mineral's powder on a streak plate; streak is more reliable than color.
Luster describes how a mineral reflects light: metallic, glassy, dull, pearly.
Cleavage is when a mineral breaks along flat smooth surfaces; fracture is breaking unevenly.
Quartz, feldspar, mica, calcite, halite (salt), gypsum and talc are common minerals.
Magnetite is magnetic; calcite fizzes in acid; halite tastes salty.
Ores are rocks with useful metals such as iron, copper, aluminum and gold that are mined.
Weathering is the breaking down of rocks into smaller pieces (sediment) by water, ice, wind, plants and chemicals.
Mechanical (physical) weathering breaks rock without changing its composition: frost wedging (ice), plant roots, abrasion.
Frost wedging: water seeps into cracks, freezes and expands, and breaks the rock apart.
Plant roots grow into cracks and split rocks apart.
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock: acid rain dissolves limestone, oxygen rusts iron in rocks.
Caves and sinkholes form when acidic groundwater dissolves limestone.
Erosion is the movement (carrying away, transport) of weathered rock and soil by wind, water, ice (glaciers) and gravity.
Deposition is when wind, water or ice drops (deposits) sediment in a new place.
Rivers erode valleys and canyons and deposit sediment at their mouths forming deltas.
The Grand Canyon was carved by erosion from the Colorado River over millions of years.
Fast moving water carries larger rocks; slow water deposits sediment; floods move a lot of soil.
Glaciers are large sheets of moving ice that carve U-shaped valleys, scratch rocks and deposit moraines and boulders.
Wind erosion forms sand dunes and wears down rocks in deserts; sandblasting is abrasion by wind.
Ocean waves erode beaches and cliffs and deposit sand; sea walls and jetties reduce beach erosion.
Gravity causes landslides, mudslides and rockfalls (mass movement) down steep slopes.
Planting trees, grass and cover crops holds soil in place and reduces erosion; terraces and contour plowing also help.
Soil is made of weathered rock (sand, silt, clay), humus (decayed organic matter), water and air.
Humus is dark decomposed plant and animal material that makes soil fertile.
Soil layers (horizons): topsoil (humus), subsoil, and bedrock.
Sand has the largest particles and drains water quickly; clay has the smallest particles and holds water.
Loam is a mix of sand, silt, clay and humus that is best for growing plants.
Soil forms very slowly from weathering of rock over a long time.
Fossils are the preserved remains or traces of organisms that lived long ago: bones, shells, footprints, imprints, molds, casts.
Fossils form when organisms are buried quickly by sediment and harden into rock over a long time.
Amber preserves insects; tar pits and ice preserve whole animals; petrified wood is wood turned to stone.
Fossils tell us about past life, environments and climates; fossils of sea animals on mountains mean the area was once under ocean.
Fossil ferns or palm trees in cold places show that the climate there was once warm.
The law of superposition: in undisturbed rock layers, the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest are at the top.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a wide area and help date rock layers.
Relative dating compares the ages of rock layers; absolute (radiometric) dating uses radioactive decay and half-life.
Geologic time scale divides Earth's 4.6 billion year history into eras and periods; dinosaurs lived in the Mesozoic era.
Dinosaurs became extinct about 65 million years ago, possibly due to an asteroid impact.
Paleontologists study fossils; geologists study rocks and Earth.
Coal formed from ancient swamp plants buried and compressed over millions of years.
Oil and natural gas formed from tiny ocean organisms buried long ago.
Earth's layers: crust, mantle, outer core, inner core.
The crust is the thin rocky outer layer of Earth; oceanic crust is dense basalt and continental crust is granite.
The mantle is the thickest layer, made of hot rock that flows slowly; convection currents in the mantle move the plates.
The outer core is liquid iron and nickel; the inner core is solid iron and nickel and is the hottest layer.
Temperature and pressure increase with depth inside Earth.
The lithosphere is the crust and upper mantle broken into tectonic plates.
Plate tectonics: Earth's crust is broken into large plates that move slowly a few centimeters per year.
Continental drift: Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents were once joined in a supercontinent called Pangaea.
Evidence for continental drift: continents fit like puzzle pieces, matching fossils and rock layers on different continents.
Divergent boundaries: plates move apart, magma rises, and new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys.
Seafloor spreading forms new ocean floor at mid-ocean ridges.
Convergent boundaries: plates collide; one plate sinks under another (subduction) forming trenches and volcanoes, or mountains are pushed up.
The Himalaya mountains formed when the India plate collided with the Asia plate.
Transform boundaries: plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes, such as the San Andreas fault.
Earthquakes are sudden shaking of the ground caused by plates moving along faults; energy is released as seismic waves.
Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate boundaries, such as the Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean.
A seismograph (seismometer) measures and records earthquake waves (seismic waves).
The Richter scale and magnitude measure the strength (energy) of an earthquake.
The epicenter is the point on the surface above where the earthquake starts (focus).
Tsunamis are huge ocean waves caused by underwater earthquakes or landslides.
Volcanoes are openings in the crust where magma, lava, ash and gases erupt.
Volcanic eruptions build mountains and islands, such as the Hawaiian Islands, which formed over a hot spot.
Volcanic ash can block sunlight and lower temperatures; lava flows destroy forests and buildings.
Mountains form by plates colliding (folding), faulting and volcanic activity; they are worn down by weathering and erosion.
Landforms: mountains, valleys, plains, plateaus, canyons, deltas, islands, peninsulas, dunes, hills.
Constructive forces build up land: volcanoes, deposition, crust deformation; destructive forces wear down land: weathering, erosion.
A delta is a landform made of sediment deposited where a river enters an ocean or lake.
A canyon is a deep narrow valley with steep sides carved by a river.
Topographic maps use contour lines to show elevation; close contour lines mean a steep slope.
Maps use a scale, a legend (key) and a compass rose; latitude and longitude lines locate places.
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.
Earth's surface is about 71 percent water (oceans) and 29 percent land.
About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans; most fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.
Groundwater is water stored underground in soil and rock (aquifers); wells pump groundwater.
Ocean water is salty because rivers carry dissolved salts and minerals to the sea.
Ocean currents move water around the world and carry warm or cold water that affects climate; the Gulf Stream is warm.
Ocean waves are caused mostly by wind blowing over the water surface.
Tides are the daily rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull (gravity) of the Moon, and also the Sun.
There are about two high tides and two low tides each day.
Spring tides (largest tides) happen at new moon and full moon when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up; neap tides happen at quarter moons.
The ocean floor has continental shelves, slopes, abyssal plains, mid-ocean ridges and deep trenches.
Salt water is denser than fresh water; cold water is denser than warm water.
The hydrosphere is all of Earth's water; the atmosphere is the air; the geosphere (lithosphere) is rock; the biosphere is all living things.
Earth's magnetic field comes from the moving liquid iron in the outer core; compasses point to magnetic north.
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere.
Water cycle steps: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection (runoff, infiltration).
The Sun provides the energy that drives the water cycle and causes evaporation.
Evaporation: heat from the Sun changes liquid water from oceans, lakes and puddles into water vapor (gas).
Puddles dry up and wet clothes dry because water evaporates; evaporation is faster when it is warm, sunny and windy.
Condensation: water vapor cools and changes into liquid water droplets, forming clouds, fog and dew.
Water droplets form on the outside of a cold glass because water vapor in the air condenses.
Dew forms on grass on cool mornings when water vapor condenses.
Precipitation is water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
Snow forms when water vapor freezes into ice crystals in cold clouds; hail forms in thunderstorms.
Runoff is precipitation that flows over the ground into streams, rivers, lakes and oceans.
Infiltration is water soaking into the ground to become groundwater.
Transpiration is water vapor released by plants through their leaves into the air.
Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals.
Cumulus clouds are puffy white clouds seen in fair weather.
Cumulonimbus clouds are tall dark storm clouds that bring thunderstorms, lightning, heavy rain and hail.
Stratus clouds are flat gray layers that cover the sky and may bring drizzle; fog is a stratus cloud at ground level.
Cirrus clouds are thin wispy high clouds made of ice crystals; they often mean fair weather or a change in weather.
Nimbus or nimbostratus clouds bring steady rain.
Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a certain place and time: temperature, precipitation, wind, humidity, air pressure, clouds.
Climate is the average weather of a region over many years (a long period of time).
Meteorologists study and forecast (predict) weather using instruments, weather maps, satellites and radar.
A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
A barometer measures air pressure; falling air pressure usually means stormy weather is coming; rising pressure means fair weather.
An anemometer measures wind speed; a wind vane (weather vane) shows wind direction.
A rain gauge measures the amount of rainfall (precipitation).
A hygrometer (psychrometer) measures humidity, the amount of water vapor in the air.
Weather satellites and Doppler radar track clouds, storms and hurricanes.
Air pressure is the weight of the air pushing down; it decreases as altitude (elevation) increases.
Wind is the movement of air from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure, caused by uneven heating of Earth's surface by the Sun.
Sea breeze: during the day, land heats faster than water, so cool air from the sea blows toward land; at night a land breeze blows toward the sea.
Land heats up and cools down faster than water; water has a high specific heat.
Air masses are large bodies of air with similar temperature and humidity.
A front is the boundary between two air masses.
A cold front happens when cold air pushes under warm air; it brings thunderstorms, then cooler clear weather.
A warm front brings steady rain or drizzle followed by warmer weather.
High pressure systems bring clear sunny fair weather; low pressure systems bring clouds, rain and storms.
Thunderstorms produce lightning (static electricity discharge) and thunder (sound of rapidly heated expanding air).
We see lightning before we hear thunder because light travels faster than sound.
Tornadoes are violent rotating funnel clouds of wind that form from severe thunderstorms; common in tornado alley.
Hurricanes are large tropical storms with strong winds and heavy rain that form over warm ocean water; they weaken over land.
Hurricanes are also called typhoons or cyclones; the calm center is the eye.
Blizzards are snowstorms with strong winds and low visibility.
Floods happen when heavy rain causes rivers to overflow; droughts are long periods without rain.
Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air; relative humidity of 100 percent means the air is saturated.
Dew point is the temperature at which water vapor condenses.
The atmosphere is the layer of gases around Earth: about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, plus argon, carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Atmosphere layers: troposphere (weather happens here), stratosphere (ozone layer), mesosphere (meteors burn up), thermosphere, exosphere.
The ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun.
Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) damage the ozone layer.
Temperature decreases with altitude in the troposphere; mountain tops are colder.
The greenhouse effect: gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat (infrared) in the atmosphere and keep Earth warm.
Global warming (climate change) is the increase in Earth's average temperature caused by more greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels.
Global warming melts glaciers and polar ice caps and causes sea level to rise.
Climate is affected by latitude, elevation, nearness to oceans, ocean currents and mountains.
Places near the equator are warm because they receive more direct sunlight; the poles are cold.
Coastal areas have milder climates than inland areas because water heats and cools slowly.
The side of a mountain facing the wind gets more rain; the other side (rain shadow) is dry.
El Nino is a warming of Pacific ocean water that changes weather patterns.
The Sun heats Earth unevenly, causing winds, ocean currents and weather.
The Sun is a star at the center of our solar system; it is a ball of hot glowing gas (hydrogen and helium).
The Sun produces energy (light and heat) by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth; it looks bigger and brighter than other stars because it is much closer.
Other stars look small and dim because they are very far away.
The Sun is a medium-sized yellow star; there are larger and smaller stars.
The solar system is the Sun and all objects that orbit it: planets, moons, asteroids, comets and dwarf planets.
Order of planets from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun and the smallest planet; it has almost no atmosphere.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat (greenhouse effect).
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only planet known to support life, with liquid water and oxygen.
Mars is the red planet; it is red because of iron oxide (rust) in its soil; it has polar ice caps.
Jupiter is the largest planet; it is a gas giant with a Great Red Spot storm and many moons.
Saturn is a gas giant known for its large bright rings made of ice and rock.
Uranus and Neptune are icy gas giants far from the Sun; Uranus rotates on its side.
Inner (terrestrial) planets Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are small and rocky.
Outer planets Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are large gas giants with rings and many moons.
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit and are colder.
The asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter contains many rocky asteroids.
Comets are balls of ice and dust that orbit the Sun; they form a glowing tail pointing away from the Sun when near it.
Meteors (shooting stars) are rocks burning up in the atmosphere; meteorites are pieces that hit the ground.
Craters on the Moon were made by meteorite and asteroid impacts.
Rotation is spinning on an axis; Earth rotates once every 24 hours (one day), causing day and night.
Earth rotates toward the east; the Sun appears to rise in the east; the Sun appears to set in the west.
The Sun appears to move across the sky during the day because Earth rotates.
Shadows are longest in the morning and evening when the Sun is low; shadows are shortest at noon when the Sun is highest in the sky.
Revolution is moving in an orbit around another object; Earth revolves around the Sun once every 365 days (one year).
The Moon revolves around Earth about once a month (27 to 29.5 days) and rotates once in the same time.
We always see the same side of the Moon because it rotates once each time it orbits Earth.
Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis (23.5 degrees) as Earth revolves around the Sun.
Summer happens when a hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun and receives more direct sunlight and longer days.
Winter happens when a hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun; sunlight is less direct and days are shorter.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere, it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
The summer solstice is the longest day of the year; the winter solstice is the shortest day of the year; equinoxes have equal day and night.
The Moon does not make its own light; moonlight is sunlight reflected off the Moon.
Moon phases are caused by how much of the Moon's sunlit half we see from Earth as the Moon orbits Earth.
Moon phases: new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent.
The cycle of moon phases takes about one month (29.5 days).
A full moon happens when Earth is between the Sun and the Moon; a new moon happens when the Moon is between Earth and the Sun.
A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and blocks sunlight, casting a shadow on Earth.
A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
The Moon has no atmosphere, no liquid water, no wind and no weather; footprints stay for a long time.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's; an astronaut weighs less on the Moon but has the same mass.
Gravity keeps the planets in orbit around the Sun and the Moon in orbit around Earth.
The Sun's gravity holds the solar system together; more mass means more gravity.
Stars are huge balls of hot gas that give off their own light; star color shows temperature: blue stars are hottest, red stars are coolest.
A star's brightness depends on its size, temperature and distance from Earth.
Star life cycle: nebula, main sequence star, red giant, white dwarf; massive stars become supernovas, neutron stars or black holes.
A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust where stars form.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars held together by gravity; the Milky Way is our spiral galaxy.
The universe contains billions of galaxies; the big bang theory explains its beginning.
A light-year is the distance light travels in one year; it measures distances between stars.
Constellations are patterns of stars in the sky such as the Big Dipper, Orion and Ursa Major.
Stars appear to move across the night sky because Earth rotates; different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves.
Polaris (the North Star) stays almost fixed in the sky above the North Pole.
Telescopes collect light and magnify distant objects such as planets, stars and galaxies; Galileo used a telescope to see Jupiter's moons.
The Hubble Space Telescope orbits above the atmosphere to get clearer images.
Satellites orbit Earth; the Moon is Earth's natural satellite; artificial satellites are used for communication, GPS and weather.
Astronauts in space need oxygen, water, food and spacesuits; the International Space Station orbits Earth.
Rockets use thrust from burning fuel pushing gases out to launch into space (action and reaction).
Copernicus proposed that Earth and planets revolve around the Sun (heliocentric model) instead of an Earth-centered (geocentric) model.
Earth is tilted on its axis, which runs through the North Pole and South Pole.
The Sun is about 150 million kilometers (93 million miles) from Earth; sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.
Solar flares and sunspots are activity on the Sun's surface.
Natural resources are materials from nature that people use: air, water, soil, minerals, plants, animals, fossil fuels.
Renewable resources can be replaced naturally in a short time: sunlight (solar), wind, water, trees (wood), plants, crops, biomass, geothermal.
Nonrenewable resources cannot be replaced in a human lifetime once they are used up: coal, oil (petroleum), natural gas, minerals, uranium.
Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) formed from the remains of ancient plants and animals over millions of years.
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, and causes air pollution and acid rain.
Gasoline, diesel, kerosene and propane come from petroleum (crude oil).
Solar energy from sunlight is collected by solar panels (solar cells) that change light into electricity.
Wind turbines (windmills) change the kinetic energy of wind into electricity.
Hydroelectric power plants at dams use moving (flowing, falling) water to spin turbines and generate electricity.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth, from hot springs and geysers.
Biomass energy comes from burning wood, plant material, crop waste or making ethanol from corn.
Nuclear power plants use uranium in fission to produce energy; they make radioactive waste but no air pollution.
Tidal energy uses the rise and fall of ocean tides.
Solar and wind energy are clean and renewable but depend on weather; the Sun does not shine at night and the wind does not always blow.
Electric power plants burn coal or gas to boil water into steam that turns turbines connected to generators.
Conservation means using resources wisely and protecting them so they last longer.
Reduce, reuse, recycle: ways to conserve resources and reduce waste in landfills.
Recycling aluminum cans, paper, glass and plastic saves energy, natural resources and landfill space.
Ways to conserve energy: turn off lights, use energy-efficient light bulbs (LED, fluorescent), insulate homes, walk, ride bikes, carpool, use public transportation.
Ways to conserve water: turn off the faucet while brushing teeth, take shorter showers, fix leaks.
Composting turns food scraps and yard waste into fertile soil (humus).
Air pollution comes from burning fossil fuels in cars, factories and power plants; smog, smoke, soot.
Acid rain forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning coal mix with water vapor; it harms lakes, forests, statues and buildings.
Water pollution comes from sewage, oil spills, fertilizer runoff, pesticides, factories and trash.
Fertilizer runoff into lakes causes algae to grow too much (algal bloom), which uses up oxygen and kills fish.
Oil spills harm ocean animals and birds by coating feathers and fur.
Pesticides kill insects but can harm birds and other animals through the food chain; DDT thinned eagle eggshells.
Litter and plastic in the ocean can be eaten by or entangle sea turtles, birds and fish.
Landfills store trash; some trash takes hundreds of years to decompose; plastic does not decompose easily.
Biodegradable materials such as paper, food and wood are broken down by decomposers.
Soil conservation: planting trees, windbreaks, cover crops, terracing, contour plowing and crop rotation prevent erosion.
Overgrazing, deforestation and poor farming cause soil erosion and loss of topsoil.
Building dams changes river ecosystems and floods habitats upstream but provides water and electricity.
Urbanization (building cities, roads, parking lots) destroys habitats and increases runoff.
Protecting habitats, wildlife refuges, national parks and laws help endangered species.
Reforestation is planting trees to replace forests that were cut down.
Human population growth increases the use of resources, land, water and energy.
Hybrid and electric cars use less gasoline and produce less pollution.
Insulation in walls and attics keeps heat in during winter and reduces energy use.
Irrigation brings water to crops; drip irrigation saves water.
Water treatment plants clean and filter water so it is safe to drink; chlorine kills germs.
Clean drinking water is a limited resource; only a small amount of Earth's water is fresh and available.
Mining removes minerals and ores from Earth but can damage land and pollute water.
Natural gas is used to heat homes and cook; it is a fossil fuel.
Wood is a renewable resource if new trees are planted; paper is made from trees.
Cotton, wool and silk are natural fibers; nylon and polyester are synthetic materials made from petroleum.
Plastic is made from petroleum (oil).
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space (volume).
States of matter: solid, liquid, gas (and plasma).
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume; its particles are packed tightly and vibrate in place.
A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container; its particles slide past each other.
A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume; it spreads out to fill its container; its particles move fast and far apart.
Plasma is a state of matter found in stars, lightning and neon signs.
Melting is a solid changing into a liquid when heat is added; ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius (32 degrees Fahrenheit).
Freezing is a liquid changing into a solid when heat is removed (cooling); water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius (32 degrees Fahrenheit).
Boiling: water boils at 100 degrees Celsius (212 degrees Fahrenheit) at sea level and changes into steam (water vapor).
Evaporation is a liquid changing slowly into a gas at its surface; boiling is rapid evaporation throughout the liquid.
Condensation is a gas changing into a liquid when it is cooled.
Sublimation is a solid changing directly into a gas, such as dry ice (solid carbon dioxide).
Deposition (in chemistry) is a gas changing directly into a solid, such as frost.
Adding heat makes particles move faster and spread apart; removing heat makes particles move slower.
Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy (motion) of the particles in a substance.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled; water expands when it freezes, so ice floats.
Water is the only substance commonly found in all three states on Earth: ice, liquid water and water vapor.
Changes of state are physical changes; the substance stays the same.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons with a spring scale; weight changes on the Moon but mass does not.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, measured in liters, milliliters or cubic centimeters.
The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder; the volume of an irregular solid (rock) can be found by water displacement.
The volume of a rectangular box equals length times width times height.
Density is mass divided by volume (mass per unit volume); D = m / V, in grams per cubic centimeter.
Objects less dense than water float; objects more dense than water sink; the density of water is 1 gram per milliliter.
Oil floats on water because oil is less dense than water.
Physical properties can be observed without changing the substance: color, shape, size, mass, volume, density, texture, hardness, odor, melting point, boiling point, magnetism, solubility, conductivity.
Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts and changes into new substances: flammability, reactivity with acid or oxygen, ability to rust or burn.
A physical change changes the size, shape or state of a substance but does not make a new substance: cutting, tearing, folding, crushing, melting, freezing, boiling, dissolving, breaking glass.
A chemical change (chemical reaction) makes new substances with new properties: burning, rusting, cooking an egg, baking a cake, digestion, rotting, tarnishing, fireworks, photosynthesis.
Signs of a chemical change: gas bubbles produced, color change, temperature change (heat or light given off), new smell, precipitate (solid) forms.
Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water; rusting is a chemical change (oxidation).
Burning (combustion) is a chemical reaction of fuel with oxygen that gives off heat and light and produces carbon dioxide and water.
Mixing baking soda and vinegar produces bubbles of carbon dioxide gas, a chemical reaction.
The law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction or physical change matter is not created or destroyed; total mass stays the same.
In a chemical equation, reactants are on the left and products on the right; the number of atoms of each element stays the same.
Endothermic reactions absorb heat and feel cold; exothermic reactions release heat and feel warm.
An atom is the smallest particle of an element that keeps the properties of that element.
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the center of the atom; electrons move around the nucleus in energy levels (shells or clouds).
The atomic number is the number of protons; it identifies the element.
The mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons; isotopes have different numbers of neutrons.
An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom; elements cannot be broken down into simpler substances.
Examples of elements: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, helium, iron, gold, silver, copper, aluminum, sodium, chlorine, calcium, neon.
The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number into rows (periods) and columns (groups or families) with similar properties.
Dmitri Mendeleev created the periodic table.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity; most are solids at room temperature; mercury is a liquid metal.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors; many are gases, such as oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine.
Noble gases such as helium, neon and argon are unreactive (inert).
A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together, such as O2, H2O or CO2.
A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined (bonded) in a fixed ratio.
Water (H2O) is a compound of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Carbon dioxide (CO2) has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms; table salt is sodium chloride (NaCl).
Compounds have different properties from the elements that form them: sodium and chlorine form salt.
Chemical bonds hold atoms together; covalent bonds share electrons; ionic bonds transfer electrons between atoms.
Oxygen gas (O2) is made of two oxygen atoms that share electrons in a covalent double bond.
Chemical formulas show the elements and number of atoms in a compound; subscripts show the number of atoms.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined; each keeps its own properties and they can be separated by physical means.
Examples of mixtures: salad, trail mix, sand and water, salt water, air, soil, concrete.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance (solute) dissolves evenly in another (solvent), such as salt water or sugar water.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
Dissolving is faster with stirring, heating (higher temperature) and smaller pieces (crushing, more surface area).
Solubility is how much of a substance can dissolve in a solvent.
Mixtures can be separated by filtering (filtration), evaporation, magnets, sifting (screens), decanting, distillation and picking by hand.
A magnet separates iron filings from sand; filtering separates sand from water; evaporation separates salt from water.
A suspension is a mixture in which particles settle out, such as muddy water.
Acids have a pH less than 7, taste sour and turn blue litmus paper red: lemon juice, vinegar, stomach acid.
Bases have a pH greater than 7, taste bitter, feel slippery and turn red litmus paper blue: soap, baking soda, ammonia, bleach.
A pH of 7 is neutral, such as pure water; acids and bases neutralize each other to form water and salt.
Indicators such as litmus paper and phenolphthalein show whether a substance is an acid or base.
Conductivity: metals such as copper conduct electricity and heat.
Physical properties of materials are chosen for their uses: metal pans conduct heat, plastic handles insulate, glass is transparent, rubber is flexible.
Malleable means a material can be hammered into thin sheets; ductile means it can be drawn into wires.
Brittle materials such as glass break easily; flexible materials bend.
Texture describes how a surface feels: rough, smooth, bumpy, soft.
Carbon is found in all living things; organic compounds contain carbon.
Radioactive elements such as uranium give off radiation as they decay.
Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; faster and heavier (more massive) objects have more kinetic energy.
Potential energy is stored energy due to position or condition; an object higher up has more gravitational potential energy.
A stretched rubber band, a compressed spring, a drawn bow and a ball held high have potential energy.
As a ball falls, potential energy changes into kinetic energy; at the top of a hill a roller coaster has the most potential energy.
Mechanical energy is the total of kinetic and potential energy of an object.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds of substances such as food, fuel, gasoline, wood and batteries.
Electrical energy is the energy of moving electric charges (electrons) in a current.
Thermal energy (heat) is the total kinetic energy of particles; it flows from warmer objects to cooler objects.
Light (radiant) energy travels as electromagnetic waves and can travel through empty space (a vacuum).
Sound energy is produced by vibrations and travels as waves through matter.
Nuclear energy is stored in the nucleus of atoms and released by fission or fusion.
The law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed (transformed) from one form to another.
Energy transformations: a battery changes chemical energy into electrical energy; a light bulb changes electrical energy into light and heat.
A toaster or electric stove changes electrical energy into thermal energy (heat).
A fan or motor changes electrical energy into mechanical energy (motion, kinetic energy).
A speaker or radio changes electrical energy into sound energy; a microphone changes sound into electrical energy.
A generator changes mechanical energy (motion) into electrical energy.
Solar cells change light energy into electrical energy; plants change light energy into chemical energy.
Burning wood or fuel changes chemical energy into heat and light (thermal and radiant energy).
A car engine changes chemical energy in gasoline into mechanical energy (motion) and heat.
In most energy transformations some energy is lost as heat (thermal energy), often due to friction.
Rubbing hands together changes kinetic energy into heat by friction.
Heat transfer: conduction, convection and radiation.
Conduction is the transfer of heat by direct contact (touching), such as a metal spoon getting hot in soup or a pan on a stove.
Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement (currents) of fluids, liquids or gases; warm air or water rises and cool air sinks.
Radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves without matter, such as heat from the Sun or a fire reaching you.
Conductors such as metals (copper, aluminum, iron, silver) let heat and electricity flow easily.
Insulators such as plastic, rubber, wood, glass, cloth, wool, foam, styrofoam and air slow the flow of heat or electricity.
Wearing a wool coat or blanket keeps you warm because it insulates and traps air, slowing heat loss.
A thermos (vacuum flask) keeps drinks hot or cold by reducing heat transfer.
Dark colors (black) absorb more light and heat (sunlight) than light colors; white and light colors reflect light and stay cooler.
Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius, Fahrenheit or kelvin.
Heat makes objects expand; bridges have expansion joints; thermometer liquid expands and rises when heated.
Electricity is the flow of electric charges (electrons) through a conductor; this flow is called electric current.
An electric circuit is a complete closed path (loop) through which electric current flows: a power source (battery), wires, a load (bulb) and a switch.
In a closed circuit current flows and the bulb lights; in an open (broken) circuit there is a gap and current stops.
A switch opens and closes a circuit.
In a series circuit there is only one path; if one bulb burns out, all the bulbs go out.
In a parallel circuit there is more than one path; if one bulb goes out, the others stay lit; homes are wired in parallel.
Batteries (cells) store chemical energy and provide voltage to push current; they have positive and negative terminals.
Voltage is measured in volts, current in amperes (amps), resistance in ohms; Ohm's law: voltage equals current times resistance.
Resistance opposes the flow of current; thin long wires have more resistance; resistance makes heat.
A short circuit lets too much current flow and can cause a fire; fuses and circuit breakers protect circuits.
Electrical power is measured in watts; energy use in kilowatt-hours.
Static electricity is a buildup of electric charge on an object, such as rubbing a balloon on hair; lightning is a large static discharge.
Like charges repel each other; opposite charges attract each other.
Rubbing materials together transfers electrons, making one object negatively charged and the other positively charged.
Electrical safety: do not touch outlets or wires with wet hands; water conducts electricity.
Magnets attract iron, nickel and cobalt and objects made of steel; magnets do not attract plastic, wood, glass, paper, aluminum or copper.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole; like magnetic poles repel; opposite (unlike) magnetic poles attract.

A compass needle is a small magnet that points north because it lines up with Earth's magnetic field.
An electromagnet is made by wrapping a coil of wire around an iron core (nail) and running electric current through it; it can be turned on and off.
More coils of wire or more current (more batteries) make an electromagnet stronger.
Electric motors use electromagnets to change electrical energy into motion; generators use magnets and coils to make electricity.
Moving a magnet through a coil of wire produces (induces) an electric current.
Magnets can lose magnetism if dropped or heated; a magnet can be broken into smaller magnets each with two poles.
A force is a push or a pull that can change an object's motion, speed, direction or shape.
Force is measured in newtons with a spring scale (force meter).
Gravity is a force of attraction between all objects with mass; it pulls objects toward the center of Earth.
Gravity makes objects fall to the ground and gives them weight; objects with more mass have more gravity.
The farther apart two objects are, the weaker the force of gravity between them.
In a vacuum, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass; air resistance slows falling objects such as feathers and paper.
Air resistance (drag) is friction from air that slows moving objects; a parachute increases air resistance.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces that are touching; it slows things down and produces heat.
Rough surfaces (sandpaper, carpet, grass, gravel) produce more friction than smooth surfaces (ice, glass, polished wood).
Lubricants such as oil and grease reduce friction between moving parts; wheels and ball bearings reduce friction.
Tires with treads, sand on icy roads and rubber soles increase friction to prevent slipping.
Balanced forces are equal and opposite and do not change an object's motion; unbalanced forces cause a change in motion (acceleration).
Net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
Newton's first law (inertia): an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in motion; more mass means more inertia; seat belts protect passengers.
Newton's second law: force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma); more force makes more acceleration; more mass means less acceleration.
Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction, such as a rocket pushing gas out, or a swimmer pushing water back.
Speed is distance divided by time (speed = distance / time), measured in meters per second, kilometers per hour or miles per hour.
Velocity is speed in a given direction; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity (speeding up, slowing down or changing direction).
Motion is a change in position over time compared to a reference point.
A distance-time graph with a steeper line shows faster speed; a flat line shows the object is not moving.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
Work is done when a force moves an object a distance: work = force times distance, measured in joules.
Power is the rate of doing work: work divided by time, measured in watts.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force: lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge, screw.
A lever is a bar that pivots on a fulcrum; seesaws, crowbars, scissors, wheelbarrows and bottle openers are levers.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope or chain that changes the direction of force; used to raise a flag or lift heavy loads with a crane.
A wheel and axle: doorknobs, steering wheels, bicycles and screwdrivers.
An inclined plane is a ramp or slope that makes it easier to lift objects by moving them a longer distance.
A wedge is two inclined planes that split or cut things apart: axes, knives, chisels, doorstops, teeth.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder: jar lids, bolts, light bulbs.
Compound machines are made of two or more simple machines, such as scissors, bicycles and can openers.
Mechanical advantage tells how much a machine multiplies force; machines do not reduce the amount of work.
Magnets exert magnetic force without touching; gravity and magnetism act at a distance.
Buoyancy is the upward force of a fluid that makes objects float.
Pressure is force divided by area; sharp knives have a small area and high pressure; snowshoes spread weight over a large area.
Centripetal force keeps objects moving in a circle, such as a ball on a string or planets in orbit.
A heavier (more massive) object needs more force to move or stop.
Pushing a cart with more force makes it go faster; a heavier cart needs more force for the same acceleration.

Waves transfer energy from one place to another without transferring matter.
Transverse waves move the medium perpendicular (at right angles, up and down) to the direction the wave travels, such as light and water waves.
Longitudinal (compression) waves move the medium back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels, such as sound waves.
Wave parts: crest (top), trough (bottom), wavelength (distance between crests), amplitude (height), frequency (waves per second).
Frequency is measured in hertz; higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
The amplitude of a wave is related to its energy; larger amplitude means more energy.
A medium is the matter (solid, liquid or gas) that a wave travels through.
Mechanical waves such as sound need a medium; electromagnetic waves such as light can travel through a vacuum (empty space).
Sound is produced by vibrations (objects vibrating back and forth).
Sound travels as waves through solids, liquids and gases but cannot travel through a vacuum or outer space.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids and slowest through gases (air).
The speed of sound in air is about 340 meters per second; light is much faster.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is; it depends on frequency; faster vibrations give a higher pitch.
Short, thin, tight strings and short air columns make higher pitch sounds; long, thick, loose strings make lower pitch.
Loudness (volume) depends on the amplitude (energy) of the sound wave; it is measured in decibels.
An echo is a reflected sound wave bouncing off a surface.
Soft materials such as carpet, curtains and foam absorb sound and reduce echoes.
Ears receive sound vibrations; the eardrum vibrates and the cochlea sends signals to the brain.
Musical instruments make sound by vibrating strings, air columns, or drum skins (membranes).
Sonar uses reflected sound waves (echoes) to find objects underwater and measure ocean depth.
Ultrasound is sound with frequency too high for humans to hear; dogs and bats hear higher frequencies.
Light is a form of energy that travels in straight lines as electromagnetic waves.
Light travels at about 300,000 kilometers per second; it is the fastest thing in the universe.
Light from the Sun is white light made of all the colors of the rainbow (visible spectrum): red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
A prism separates (refracts) white light into the colors of the spectrum; a rainbow forms when sunlight is refracted by raindrops.
Red light has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency; violet light has the shortest wavelength and highest frequency.
Reflection is light bouncing off a surface; mirrors reflect light and form images; the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
Smooth shiny surfaces such as mirrors reflect light well.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium to another, such as from air into water or glass; a straw looks bent in water.
Lenses refract (bend) light; convex lenses bring light together (converge) and magnify; concave lenses spread light out (diverge).
Eyeglasses, magnifying glasses, cameras, microscopes and telescopes use lenses.
Absorption is when light energy is taken in by a material and changed into heat; black objects absorb all colors.
We see an object's color because it reflects that color of light and absorbs the others; a red apple reflects red light.
White objects reflect all colors of light; black objects absorb all colors.
Transparent materials such as clear glass, water and air let almost all light pass through.
Translucent materials such as wax paper, frosted glass and tissue paper let some light through but scatter it.
Opaque materials such as wood, metal, cardboard and bricks block light and cast shadows.
A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light; shadows change length and direction as the light source moves.
We see objects when light reflects off them and enters our eyes.
The electromagnetic spectrum from longest to shortest wavelength: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat; ultraviolet rays cause sunburn; X-rays pass through soft tissue to show bones.
Radio waves and microwaves are used for communication, cell phones, television and cooking (microwave ovens).
Lasers produce a narrow intense beam of light of one color.
Light bulbs: incandescent bulbs give off much heat; LED and fluorescent bulbs are more energy efficient.
Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or through openings.
Interference happens when two waves meet and combine.
Seismic waves are waves of energy that travel through Earth during an earthquake.
The scientific method: ask a question, research, form a hypothesis, test with an experiment, record and analyze data, draw a conclusion, communicate results.
A hypothesis is a testable prediction or possible explanation, often written as an if-then statement.
A theory is a well-tested explanation supported by much evidence; a law describes what happens in nature.
An experiment tests a hypothesis by changing one variable and measuring the result.
The independent (manipulated) variable is the one factor the scientist changes on purpose.
The dependent (responding) variable is what is measured or observed in response to the change.
Controlled variables (constants) are factors kept the same in all groups so that the test is fair.
A control group does not receive the treatment and is used for comparison.
A fair test changes only one variable at a time and keeps all other conditions the same.
Repeating trials (repeated trials) and using a large sample size makes results more reliable and accurate.
Scientists repeat experiments to verify results; other scientists must be able to replicate the results.
Qualitative observations describe qualities using the senses (color, texture, smell); quantitative observations use numbers and measurements.
An inference is a conclusion or explanation based on observations.
A prediction is a statement about what will happen in the future based on evidence or patterns.
Data are recorded in data tables, charts and science notebooks (journals) and shown in graphs.
Bar graphs compare amounts in different categories; line graphs show changes over time (continuous data); pie charts (circle graphs) show parts of a whole.
On a graph the independent variable goes on the x-axis and the dependent variable goes on the y-axis.
A model represents an object or process that is too large, too small, too slow or too dangerous to study directly, such as a globe or a model of the solar system.
Scientists communicate results by publishing reports and sharing data so others can review them.
Bias can affect results; scientists should report data honestly, even when it does not support the hypothesis.
Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other.
Metric (SI) units: length in meters (m), centimeters, millimeters, kilometers; mass in grams (g) and kilograms; volume in liters (L) and milliliters (mL); temperature in degrees Celsius.
A metric ruler or meter stick measures length (distance) in centimeters and meters; a tape measure measures longer lengths.
A balance (triple beam balance, pan balance) measures mass in grams.
A spring scale measures force or weight in newtons.

A beaker and a flask hold and mix liquids; a test tube holds small samples.
A thermometer measures temperature.
A stopwatch or clock measures time in seconds.
A microscope magnifies tiny objects such as cells; a hand lens magnifies small objects; a telescope views distant objects.
Binoculars help observe birds and distant animals.
A compass shows direction (north, south, east, west).
A calculator and computer help analyze data.
A pH meter or litmus paper measures acidity.
