The periodic table lists all known elements, about 118, organized by atomic number.
Each element has a chemical symbol: H hydrogen, He helium, C carbon, N nitrogen, O oxygen, Na sodium, Cl chlorine, Fe iron, Cu copper, Au gold, Ag silver, Ca calcium, K potassium, Mg magnesium, Al aluminum, S sulfur, P phosphorus, Ne neon, Pb lead, Hg mercury.
Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature.
Gold and silver are unreactive metals that do not easily rust or tarnish, so they are used in jewelry and coins.
Iron rusts; aluminum resists corrosion and is lightweight, used in cans and airplanes.
Sodium is a soft, very reactive metal; chlorine is a poisonous green gas; together they form harmless table salt.
Elements in group 1 (alkali metals) have one valence electron and react strongly.
Elements in group 18 (noble gases) have full outer shells and rarely react.
Atoms of elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells.
Atoms are neutral when the number of protons equals the number of electrons.
Atoms gain or lose electrons to form ions; metals lose electrons to form positive ions.
Electrons in the outermost shell are involved in chemical bonds.
Atoms bond to become more stable with full outer shells.
A chemical formula such as H2O shows two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; the subscript shows the number of atoms.
Glucose has the formula C6H12O6, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
Methane (CH4) is natural gas, made of carbon and hydrogen.
Ammonia (NH3) is made of nitrogen and hydrogen.
Ozone (O3) is a molecule of three oxygen atoms.
Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) breaks down into water and oxygen gas.
In a chemical equation the reactants are on the left of the arrow and the products are on the right.
Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction; the number of each type of atom is the same before and after.
The total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
When wood burns, its mass seems to decrease because gases such as carbon dioxide and water vapor escape into the air.
In a closed container the mass stays the same during a reaction.
Rate of reaction increases with higher temperature, greater concentration, smaller particle size (more surface area) and stirring or a catalyst.
Powdered sugar dissolves faster than a sugar cube because it has more surface area.
Food spoils faster at warm temperatures because reactions and bacteria are faster.
Enzymes in the body are catalysts that speed up reactions such as digestion.
Combustion of hydrocarbons such as gasoline, propane and methane produces carbon dioxide and water.
Photosynthesis is a chemical reaction: carbon dioxide plus water with light energy produces glucose and oxygen.
Cellular respiration is a chemical reaction: glucose plus oxygen produces carbon dioxide, water and energy.
Rusting is a slow oxidation reaction of iron with oxygen and water to form iron oxide.
Painting, oiling or galvanizing iron prevents rust by keeping out water and oxygen.
Tarnish on silver is a chemical reaction with sulfur in the air.
A precipitate is a solid that forms when two liquid solutions are mixed.
An endothermic reaction absorbs heat, so the container feels cold, as in an instant cold pack.
An exothermic reaction releases heat, as in burning, hand warmers and fireworks.
Cooking an egg changes the proteins, a chemical change that cannot be reversed.
Physical changes such as melting and dissolving can often be reversed; most chemical changes cannot be reversed easily.
Dissolving salt in water is a physical change because the salt can be recovered by evaporating the water.
Mixtures can be heterogeneous (different parts visible, like salad or sand and water) or homogeneous (the same throughout, like salt water or air).
Solutions are homogeneous mixtures; the solute dissolves in the solvent.
Concentration is how much solute is dissolved in a solvent; adding more water dilutes a solution.
A saturated solution cannot dissolve any more solute.
More sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold water.
Gases such as carbon dioxide dissolve better in cold water; soda goes flat faster when warm.
Distillation separates liquids by boiling and condensing them.
Chromatography separates the colors in ink.
Evaporation separates dissolved salt from water.
Filtration separates insoluble solids such as sand from a liquid.
A centrifuge spins mixtures to separate parts by density.
Pure substances (elements and compounds) have fixed melting and boiling points.
Physical properties can be observed without changing the substance: color, odor, density, hardness, melting point, boiling point, conductivity, solubility, magnetism.
Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts: flammability, reactivity with acid, ability to rust or burn.
Flammable materials such as gasoline, paper and wood catch fire easily.
Acids react with metals such as zinc to produce hydrogen gas.
Acids react with limestone and baking soda to produce carbon dioxide bubbles.
Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) helps digest food; antacids are bases that neutralize it.
Vinegar contains acetic acid; citrus fruits contain citric acid.
Soap, bleach, ammonia and drain cleaner are bases.
The pH scale goes from 0 to 14: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic (alkaline).
Acid rain has a pH below 5.6.
Plastics are polymers, long chains of repeating molecules, made from petroleum.
Organic compounds contain carbon and are found in living things: carbohydrates, proteins, fats and nucleic acids.
Carbon atoms can form four bonds and long chains.
Salt (sodium chloride) forms cubic crystals.
Crystals form when a solution evaporates slowly or a melted substance cools slowly.
Metals are malleable (can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (can be drawn into wires).
Nonmetals such as sulfur and carbon (graphite) are brittle.
Graphite in pencils is soft; diamond is hard; both are made of carbon atoms arranged differently.
The density of water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter (1 gram per milliliter).
Substances denser than water, such as iron (7.9 grams per cubic centimeter), sink.
Liquids of different densities form layers: honey at the bottom, then water, then oil on top.
Density of a substance stays the same no matter the size of the sample.
Cutting an object in half does not change its density.
Temperature changes can change density: hot water is less dense than cold water.
Salt water is denser than fresh water, so objects float more easily in the ocean.
A substance's state at room temperature depends on its melting point and boiling point.
Heating a substance at its melting point keeps the temperature constant until all of it melts.
Radioactive elements such as uranium and radium give off radiation as their nuclei break down.
Half life is the time for half of the atoms of a radioactive element to decay.
Nuclear fission splits a large nucleus; nuclear fusion joins small nuclei, as in the Sun.
Rotation is spinning on an axis; Earth rotates once a day.
Revolution is moving in an orbit around another object; Earth revolves around the Sun once a year.
Weather is the short term condition of the atmosphere, today or this week.
Climate is the long term average weather of a region over decades.
Mass is the amount of matter and stays the same everywhere.
Weight is the pull of gravity and changes on the Moon or other planets.
Speed is how fast; velocity is speed with direction.
A physical change makes no new substance: melting, cutting, dissolving.
A chemical change makes a new substance: burning, rusting, cooking.
Plant cells have cell walls, chloroplasts and large vacuoles.
Animal cells have a cell membrane, nucleus and cytoplasm, with small vacuoles.
Both plant and animal cells have a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm and mitochondria.

Cellular respiration uses oxygen and sugar and gives off carbon dioxide and water; it releases energy.

Predators hunt; prey are hunted.
Inherited traits come from parents through genes.
Learned behaviors come from experience and teaching.
Vertebrates have backbones; invertebrates do not have backbones.
Warm blooded animals keep a steady body temperature: mammals and birds.
Cold blooded animals have body temperatures that change with the environment: reptiles, amphibians and fish.

Incomplete metamorphosis has a nymph stage: egg, nymph, adult.
Sexual reproduction has two parents and offspring with varied traits.
Asexual reproduction has one parent and identical offspring.

Erosion carries the pieces away.
Deposition drops the pieces in a new place.

Sedimentary rock forms from compacted and cemented sediment.
Metamorphic rock forms from heat and pressure changing other rock.

A star makes its own light; a planet reflects light from a star.
A planet orbits a star; a moon orbits a planet.
A solar eclipse darkens the Sun during the day; a lunar eclipse darkens the Moon at night.

Convection moves heat through flowing liquids and gases.
Radiation moves heat through space as waves.
Conductors let heat and electricity pass easily; metals are conductors.
Insulators block heat and electricity; rubber, plastic and wood are insulators.
Transparent objects let all light through; translucent let some light through; opaque let no light through.
Reflection bounces light back; refraction bends light; absorption takes light in.
Series circuits have one path; parallel circuits have many paths.
Renewable resources are replaced naturally; nonrenewable resources run out.
Kinetic energy is energy of motion; potential energy is stored energy.
Elements have one kind of atom; compounds have two or more elements chemically joined; mixtures are physically combined.
Solutions are evenly mixed; suspensions settle out.
Acids have pH below 7; bases have pH above 7.
Atoms are the smallest units of elements; molecules are groups of bonded atoms.
Protons are positive; electrons are negative; neutrons are neutral.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins return blood to the heart.
The small intestine absorbs nutrients; the large intestine absorbs water.
Bacteria are living cells; viruses are not cells and need a host.
Antibiotics treat bacterial infections; vaccines prevent diseases.
Mitosis makes body cells for growth; meiosis makes sex cells.
Dominant traits show with one allele; recessive traits need two alleles.
Genotype is the genes; phenotype is the appearance.
Hypothesis is a testable prediction; theory is a well supported explanation; law describes a pattern.
Observation is what you sense; inference is what you conclude.
Independent variable is changed; dependent variable is measured; controlled variables stay the same.
Qualitative data describe; quantitative data count and measure.
The inner planets are rocky and small; the outer planets are gas giants.
High pressure brings fair weather; low pressure brings storms.
Cold fronts bring short heavy storms; warm fronts bring long light rain.
Cumulus clouds are puffy; stratus clouds are flat layers; cirrus clouds are thin and wispy.
Hurricanes form over warm oceans and are huge; tornadoes form over land and are small but violent.
Continental crust is thick and less dense; oceanic crust is thin and more dense.
Divergent boundaries move apart; convergent boundaries collide; transform boundaries slide past.
Fresh water is in lakes, rivers, groundwater and ice; salt water is in oceans.
Day has sunlight; night is dark because that side of Earth faces away from the Sun.
Summer has long days and direct sunlight; winter has short days and slanted sunlight.
Gravity pulls objects together; friction resists motion; magnetism attracts or repels.
Balanced forces cause no change in motion; unbalanced forces change motion.
Sound needs a medium; light does not need a medium.
Sound travels faster in solids than in gases; light travels fastest in a vacuum.
Earth has layers: the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.
The crust is the thin, solid, rocky outer layer of Earth that we live on.
The mantle is the thickest layer of Earth, made of hot, slowly flowing rock (magma) below the crust.
The outer core is liquid iron and nickel; the inner core is solid iron and nickel and is the hottest layer.
The lithosphere is the crust and upper mantle broken into tectonic plates.
Tectonic plates are large pieces of Earth's crust that move slowly on the mantle, a few centimeters per year.
Convection currents in the mantle move the tectonic plates.
Continental drift: the continents were once joined in one supercontinent called Pangaea and have slowly moved apart.
Evidence for continental drift includes matching fossils, matching rock layers and coastlines of continents that fit together like puzzle pieces, such as South America and Africa.
At divergent plate boundaries plates move apart, forming mid ocean ridges, rift valleys and new crust (sea floor spreading).
At convergent plate boundaries plates collide (push together), forming mountains, volcanoes and deep ocean trenches.
Subduction is when a dense oceanic plate sinks under another plate at a convergent boundary.
The Himalaya mountains formed when the India plate collided with the Asia plate.
At transform boundaries plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes, like the San Andreas fault in California.
A fault is a crack or break in Earth's crust where rocks move.
Earthquakes are sudden shaking of the ground caused by movement of rocks along faults at plate boundaries.
Earthquakes are measured with a seismograph (seismometer); magnitude is rated on the Richter scale.
The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above where an earthquake starts (focus).
Earthquakes under the ocean can cause tsunamis, huge ocean waves.
Volcanoes form where magma (molten rock) rises through openings in the crust, often near plate boundaries and the Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean.
Lava is magma that flows out onto Earth's surface from a volcano.
Volcanic eruptions release lava, ash and gases such as sulfur dioxide and carbon dioxide; ash can block sunlight and cool climate.
Hot spots such as Hawaii form volcanic islands in the middle of a plate.
Mountains form by plates colliding, folding, faulting and volcanoes.
Constructive forces build up land: volcanoes, deposition, mountain building, crustal uplift.
Destructive forces wear down land: weathering, erosion, earthquakes, landslides.
Rocks are made of one or more minerals.
A mineral is a naturally occurring, nonliving (inorganic) solid with a definite crystal structure and chemical composition.
Minerals are identified by properties: hardness, streak, luster, color, cleavage, fracture, crystal shape and density.
Streak is the color of a mineral's powder when rubbed on a streak plate (unglazed porcelain tile).
Luster describes how a mineral reflects light: metallic, glassy, dull or pearly.
The Mohs hardness scale rates minerals from 1 (talc, softest) to 10 (diamond, hardest).
A harder mineral scratches a softer mineral; a scratch test compares hardness.
Diamond is the hardest mineral; talc is the softest mineral.
Quartz is a common hard mineral (hardness 7) found in granite and sand.
Calcite fizzes (bubbles) when acid such as vinegar is dropped on it; limestone and marble contain calcite.
Igneous rocks form when molten rock (magma or lava) cools and hardens (solidifies).
Granite is an intrusive igneous rock with large crystals that cooled slowly underground.
Basalt and obsidian are extrusive igneous rocks that cooled quickly on the surface from lava; obsidian is volcanic glass.
Pumice is a light igneous rock full of holes (gas bubbles) that can float on water.
Slow cooling forms large crystals; fast cooling forms small crystals or glass.
Sedimentary rocks form when layers of sediments (sand, mud, clay, pebbles, shells) are deposited, compacted and cemented together.
Sandstone, shale, limestone and conglomerate are sedimentary rocks.
Sedimentary rocks form in layers and often contain fossils.
Limestone forms from shells and skeletons of ocean organisms (calcium carbonate).
Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by great heat and pressure deep underground without melting.
Marble is metamorphic rock formed from limestone; slate forms from shale; quartzite forms from sandstone; gneiss forms from granite.
The rock cycle: rocks change from one type to another through melting, cooling, weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction, heat and pressure.
Weathering is the breaking down of rocks into smaller pieces (sediments) at or near Earth's surface.
Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock without changing its composition: ice wedging, plant roots, abrasion, temperature changes.
Ice wedging (frost wedging): water seeps into cracks, freezes, expands and breaks rocks apart; it also cracks pavement and causes potholes.
Plant roots grow into cracks in rocks and break them apart.
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock: acid rain dissolving limestone, oxidation (rusting) of iron in rocks, carbonic acid forming caves.
Acid rain dissolves limestone and marble statues and buildings.
Warm, wet climates have the most chemical weathering.
Erosion is the movement (carrying away) of weathered rock, sediment and soil by water, wind, ice (glaciers) and gravity.
Running water in rivers and streams is the main agent of erosion; rivers carve valleys and canyons.
The Grand Canyon was carved by erosion from the Colorado River over millions of years.
Wind erosion moves sand and forms sand dunes in deserts; wind blows sand against rocks (abrasion).
Glaciers are huge, slowly moving masses of ice that erode U-shaped valleys and carry boulders, rocks and sediment (till).
Glaciers deposit moraines and leave lakes and scratches on rocks when they melt and retreat.
Gravity causes landslides, mudslides and rockfalls that move soil and rock downhill.
Ocean waves erode beaches, cliffs and shorelines.
Deposition is when sediment carried by water, wind or ice is dropped in a new place.
A delta forms where a river deposits sediment as it slows and enters an ocean or lake, like the Mississippi River delta.
Sand bars, beaches, flood plains, alluvial fans and sand dunes are formed by deposition.
Floods deposit fertile soil (silt) on flood plains.
Plants, grass, trees, cover crops, terraces and windbreaks reduce soil erosion by holding soil in place with their roots.
Contour plowing and terracing on hillsides reduce soil erosion.
Removing trees (deforestation) and overgrazing increase soil erosion.
Soil is a mixture of weathered rock particles, minerals, humus (decayed organic matter), water and air.
Humus is dark organic material from decomposed (decayed) plants and animals that makes soil fertile.
Topsoil is the top layer of soil, rich in humus and nutrients, where plants grow.
Sand particles are the largest soil particles and drain water quickly.
Clay particles are the smallest soil particles, hold water and feel sticky.
Silt particles are medium sized soil particles.
Loam is a soil mixture of sand, silt, clay and humus that is best for growing plants.
Earthworms loosen (aerate) soil, mix it and add nutrients.
Soil forms very slowly, over hundreds to thousands of years, from weathering of bedrock and decomposition.
Landforms include mountains, valleys, plains, plateaus, canyons, hills, islands, peninsulas, deltas and dunes.
A plateau is a high, flat area of land.
A peninsula is land surrounded by water on three sides; an island is surrounded by water on all sides.
A canyon is a deep narrow valley with steep sides carved by a river.
Topographic maps use contour lines to show elevation and the shape of land; close lines mean steep slopes.
Fossils are preserved remains or traces of organisms that lived long ago, such as bones, shells, footprints, imprints and molds.
Most fossils form in sedimentary rock when organisms are buried quickly by sediment such as mud or sand.
Hard parts like bones, teeth and shells are most likely to become fossils.
Petrified wood forms when minerals replace the wood of buried trees.
Insects can be preserved whole in amber (tree sap); mammoths have been preserved frozen in ice.
Fossils show how living things and environments have changed over time and that many species are extinct.
Fossils of ocean organisms (fish, shells, coral) found on land or mountains show the area was once covered by an ocean or sea.
Fossils of tropical plants (ferns, palm trees) in cold places show the climate was once warm and tropical.
Coal forms from the fossil remains of ancient swamp plants buried and compressed over millions of years.
The law of superposition: in undisturbed sedimentary rock layers, the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest layers are on top.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a wide area, used to date rock layers.
Relative dating compares rock layers; radioactive (absolute) dating uses half life of radioactive elements to find the age of rocks.
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.
The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into eras and periods based on fossils.
Dinosaurs lived during the Mesozoic Era and became extinct about 66 million years ago, probably after an asteroid impact.
Earth's surface is about 71 percent water, mostly salt water in oceans.
About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans; only about 3 percent is fresh water.
Most of Earth's fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps; some is groundwater.
Groundwater is water stored underground in soil and rock layers called aquifers; wells pump groundwater.
Permeable soil and rock such as sand and gravel let water pass through; impermeable clay and rock do not.
A watershed (drainage basin) is the land area that drains into a river, lake or stream.
Ocean water is salty because rivers carry dissolved minerals and salts into the ocean.
Ocean currents move water around Earth and carry heat, affecting climate; the Gulf Stream is a warm current.
Tides are the regular rise and fall of ocean water levels, about twice a day, caused mostly by the Moon's gravity.
The deepest parts of the ocean are trenches, like the Mariana Trench.
The continental shelf is the shallow part of the ocean floor near the coast.
Estuaries are where fresh river water mixes with salt ocean water.
Wetlands such as marshes and swamps filter water, reduce flooding and provide habitats for many organisms.
Caves form when acidic groundwater dissolves limestone; stalactites and stalagmites form in caves.
Geysers and hot springs are heated by magma underground, as in Yellowstone.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth.
Earth's surface changes slowly through weathering, erosion and deposition and quickly through earthquakes, volcanoes, landslides and floods.
Slow changes take thousands to millions of years, such as forming canyons, mountains and soil.
Fast changes happen in minutes to days, such as earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides, floods and hurricanes.
Mountains wear down over millions of years by weathering and erosion; old mountains such as the Appalachians are rounded and low.
Young mountains such as the Rocky Mountains and Himalayas are tall and jagged.

Sediments settle in layers with the largest, heaviest particles on the bottom.

Waves and currents move sand along beaches; jetties and sea walls protect shorelines.
Barrier islands protect the mainland coast from storms.
Mass movement includes landslides, mudflows, rockfalls, creep and slumps caused by gravity.
Heavy rain, earthquakes and removing vegetation make landslides more likely on steep slopes.
Sinkholes form when underground limestone dissolves and the ground collapses.
Karst landscapes with caves and sinkholes form in limestone regions.
The ocean floor has mountains (mid ocean ridges), trenches, plains and seamounts.
New oceanic crust forms at mid ocean ridges and is destroyed at trenches.
Rocks closer to a mid ocean ridge are younger; rocks farther away are older.
The Atlantic Ocean is getting wider as plates move apart at the Mid Atlantic Ridge.
Iceland sits on the Mid Atlantic Ridge and has many volcanoes.
The Andes mountains formed where an oceanic plate sinks under the South American plate.
Japan, Indonesia, Alaska and the west coast of the United States are on the Ring of Fire and have many earthquakes and volcanoes.

Volcanic ash makes fertile soil.
Seismographs at three stations are used to locate an earthquake's epicenter.
Earthquake damage depends on magnitude, distance from the epicenter, ground type and building design.
Engineers design earthquake resistant buildings that can sway with flexible frames.
Earth's interior is hot because of heat left from its formation and from radioactive decay.
Temperature and pressure increase with depth inside Earth.

Oceanic crust is made of dense basalt; continental crust is mostly lighter granite.
Most of Earth's crust is made of oxygen and silicon in silicate minerals such as quartz and feldspar.
Ores are rocks with valuable minerals such as iron, copper and aluminum that are mined.
Gems such as diamonds, rubies and emeralds are rare, hard minerals.

Trilobites are index fossils of ancient ocean invertebrates.
The oldest fossils are of simple single celled organisms such as bacteria.
Life on Earth began in the oceans; plants and animals later moved onto land.
Mass extinctions wiped out many species at once, such as at the end of the age of dinosaurs.
The fossil record shows that simple organisms appeared before complex organisms.
Carbon dating is used to find the age of once living things up to about 50,000 years.
Rocks containing fossils of the same species on different continents show the continents were once joined.
Glacial deposits found in tropical areas show continents have moved.
Coal found in Antarctica shows it once had a warm climate with swamps.
Earth's magnetic field has reversed many times, recorded in sea floor rocks.
Groundwater flows slowly through pores in rocks and soil.
The water table is the top of the zone where the ground is saturated with water.
Porous rocks such as sandstone hold water; impermeable rocks such as shale and granite block water.
Springs form where groundwater flows out at the surface.
Wells must be drilled below the water table.
Pollution from landfills, septic tanks and fertilizers can seep into groundwater.
Rivers flow downhill from high elevations to the ocean.
The Continental Divide separates rivers flowing to the Pacific from rivers flowing to the Atlantic.
Lakes form in low areas, behind dams, in glacial basins and in volcanic craters.
Ocean water contains dissolved salts, mostly sodium chloride.
The ocean has layers: a warm surface layer, a thermocline and cold deep water.
Surface ocean currents are driven by wind; deep currents are driven by differences in temperature and salinity (density).
Upwelling brings cold, nutrient rich water to the surface, supporting fish.
El Nino is a warming of the Pacific Ocean that changes weather patterns around the world.
The ocean absorbs heat and carbon dioxide and affects climate.
Sea level rises when glaciers and ice sheets melt and when ocean water warms and expands.
The Sun heats Earth unevenly: the equator gets more direct energy than the poles.
Heat moves from the equator toward the poles through winds and ocean currents.
Prevailing winds (trade winds, westerlies) move weather across Earth; in the United States weather usually moves from west to east.
The jet stream is a fast, high altitude wind that steers storms.
The Coriolis effect, caused by Earth's rotation, curves winds and ocean currents.
Mountain ranges block moist air, causing rain on the windward side and deserts on the leeward side.
Lake effect snow forms when cold air passes over warm lake water.
Monsoons are seasonal winds that bring heavy rain.
Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water vapor condenses.
Warm air can hold more water vapor than cold air.
Rising air cools, and water vapor condenses to form clouds and precipitation.
Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals, not water vapor.
Raindrops form when cloud droplets join together and become heavy enough to fall.
Hurricanes get their energy from warm ocean water; they weaken over land or cold water.

Tornadoes are most common in the central United States (Tornado Alley) in spring.
A tornado watch means conditions are right for tornadoes; a warning means one has been seen.
Weather maps show high and low pressure systems, fronts, temperature and precipitation.
Isobars on weather maps connect points of equal air pressure.
Climate is affected by latitude, elevation, nearness to water, ocean currents and prevailing winds.
Coastal cities have milder climates than inland cities at the same latitude.
Climate change evidence includes rising temperatures, melting glaciers, rising sea levels and changes in plant and animal ranges.
Scientists study ice cores, tree rings, fossils and sediment to learn about past climates.
Volcanic eruptions can cool global climate for a year or two by blocking sunlight with ash and sulfur gases.
Natural changes in Earth's orbit cause ice ages over long time periods.
Humans increase greenhouse gases by burning fossil fuels and cutting forests.
Reducing fossil fuel use and planting trees can slow global warming.
Earth's atmosphere is made of about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, and small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Early Earth's atmosphere had little oxygen; oxygen increased after photosynthetic organisms evolved.
The atmosphere becomes thinner (less dense) with altitude; climbers on high mountains need extra oxygen.
Air pressure is greatest at sea level.
An ecosystem is all the living organisms (biotic factors) and nonliving things (abiotic factors) interacting in an area.
Abiotic (nonliving) factors include sunlight, water, temperature, air, soil, rocks and minerals.
Biotic (living) factors include plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives and gets food, water, shelter and space.
A niche is the role of an organism in its ecosystem, what it eats and how it lives.
A population is all the members of one species living in an area.
A community is all the populations of different species living together in an area.
The biosphere is all the parts of Earth where life exists.
A food chain shows how energy passes from one organism to another when it is eaten.
A food chain starts with the Sun and a producer, such as grass, then herbivores, then carnivores: grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, hawk.
The arrows in a food chain or food web show the direction energy flows, from the food to the eater.
A food web is many connected food chains in an ecosystem.
Producers (plants, algae, phytoplankton) make their own food from sunlight by photosynthesis and are the base of food chains.
Consumers cannot make their own food and must eat other organisms.
Herbivores are primary consumers that eat only plants, such as rabbits, deer, cows, horses, grasshoppers and caterpillars.
Carnivores eat only other animals, such as lions, wolves, hawks, eagles, snakes, sharks and owls.
Omnivores eat both plants and animals, such as humans, bears, raccoons, pigs and many birds.
Predators hunt and eat other animals called prey; owls are predators of mice.
When the number of predators increases, the prey population decreases; when prey decreases, predators decrease.
Scavengers such as vultures, hyenas and crabs eat dead animals.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi, mushrooms and some worms break down dead plants and animals and wastes, returning nutrients to the soil.
Decomposers recycle nutrients so producers can use them again; all organisms in an ecosystem depend on decomposers and soil microorganisms.
Without decomposers, dead organisms and wastes would pile up and nutrients would not be recycled.
An energy pyramid shows that energy decreases at each level of a food chain; only about 10 percent of energy is passed to the next level.
Most energy at each level is used for life processes or lost as heat.
Producers are at the bottom of the energy pyramid and have the most energy; top predators have the least energy.
There are more producers than consumers in an ecosystem.
Plankton are tiny organisms floating in water; phytoplankton are producers and zooplankton are consumers.
Phytoplankton and algae produce most of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.
Competition happens when organisms need the same limited resources, such as food, water, space, light or mates.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space, shelter, predators and disease limit the size of a population.
Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support.
Symbiosis is a close relationship between two different species.
Mutualism: both organisms benefit, like bees and flowers, or clownfish and sea anemones.
Commensalism: one organism benefits and the other is not affected, like barnacles on a whale or birds nesting in trees.
Parasitism: a parasite benefits and harms its host, like ticks, fleas, lice, tapeworms and mosquitoes.
Biomes are large regions with similar climate, plants and animals.
Deserts are very dry with little rainfall; cactus, lizards, snakes and camels live there.
Tropical rainforests near the equator are hot and wet with the most biodiversity (most species).
The tundra is a cold, treeless biome with permafrost (frozen soil), mosses, lichens, caribou and arctic foxes.
Grasslands (prairies, savannas) have grasses, few trees, and grazing animals such as bison, zebras and antelope.
Deciduous forests have four seasons and trees that lose their leaves in fall.
Coniferous forests (taiga) have evergreen trees with needles, cold winters and moose.
Oceans (marine biomes) cover most of Earth and are salt water; coral reefs have great biodiversity.
Freshwater ecosystems include ponds, lakes, rivers and streams.
Ecological succession is the gradual change of an ecosystem over time, such as after a forest fire or on bare rock.
Pioneer species such as lichens and mosses are the first to grow on bare rock and help make soil.
Lichens are a partnership of fungi and algae that break down rock.
After a fire, grasses grow first, then shrubs, then trees.
Biodiversity is the variety of different species in an ecosystem; high biodiversity makes ecosystems more stable.
Invasive (nonnative) species introduced to a new ecosystem compete with native species and can harm them because they have no natural predators.
Natural resources are materials from nature that people use: air, water, soil, minerals, plants, animals, fossil fuels.
Renewable resources can be replaced naturally in a short time, such as sunlight, wind, water, trees, plants and animals.
Renewable energy sources include solar, wind, hydroelectric (moving water), geothermal, tidal and biomass energy.
Wind, solar and moving water are renewable sources of energy that do not run out.
Nonrenewable resources cannot be replaced in a human lifetime and have limited supplies: coal, oil (petroleum), natural gas, gasoline, uranium and minerals.
Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) formed from remains of ancient plants and animals over millions of years and are nonrenewable.
Burning fossil fuels causes air pollution, acid rain and releases carbon dioxide.
Solar and wind energy do not pollute the air but depend on weather and sunlight.

Reusing containers and bags reduces waste.

Ways to conserve energy: turn off lights, use energy efficient bulbs, carpool, walk, ride a bike, use public transportation, insulate homes.

Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; a moving object has kinetic energy.
A faster or more massive moving object has more kinetic energy.
Potential energy is stored energy due to position or height, like a rock at the top of a hill.
Gravitational potential energy increases with height and mass.
A stretched rubber band or compressed spring stores elastic potential energy.
As a ball falls, potential energy changes into kinetic energy.
A ball thrown upward gains potential energy and loses kinetic energy as it rises.
A roller coaster has the most potential energy at the top of the highest hill and the most kinetic energy at the bottom.
A pendulum swings, changing potential energy to kinetic energy and back.
Mechanical energy is the total kinetic plus potential energy of an object.
Thermal energy (heat) is the total kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
Chemical energy is stored in food, fuels such as gasoline, wood and coal, and batteries.
Electrical energy is the energy of moving electric charges (electric current).
Light energy (radiant energy) travels as electromagnetic waves, like sunlight.
Sound energy is produced by vibrations and travels as waves.
Nuclear energy is released by splitting atoms (fission) or joining atoms (fusion) in the nucleus.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
Nuclear power plants use fission of uranium to produce electricity.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another (law of conservation of energy).
Energy transformations: a battery changes chemical energy into electrical energy.
A flashlight changes chemical energy in batteries to electrical energy and then light energy.
A light bulb changes electrical energy into light energy and heat (thermal) energy.
An electric motor changes electrical energy into mechanical energy (motion).
A generator changes mechanical energy (motion) into electrical energy.
A wind turbine changes kinetic energy of wind into electrical energy.
Solar panels (solar cells, photovoltaic cells) change light energy from the Sun into electrical energy.
A toaster or electric stove changes electrical energy into heat (thermal) energy.
A fan changes electrical energy into kinetic (mechanical) energy.
A speaker or radio changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A microphone changes sound energy into electrical energy.
Plants change light energy from the Sun into chemical energy stored in food (glucose) by photosynthesis.
Animals change chemical energy from food into kinetic energy (motion) and heat in their bodies.
Burning fuel changes chemical energy into heat and light energy.
A car engine changes chemical energy in gasoline into mechanical energy (motion) and heat.
Rubbing hands together changes mechanical energy into heat through friction.
In energy transformations some energy is always lost as heat to the surroundings.
Most energy on Earth comes from the Sun.
Heat is transferred by conduction, convection and radiation.
Conduction is heat transfer by direct contact between touching objects, like a metal spoon in hot soup getting hot.
Convection is heat transfer by the movement of fluids (liquids or gases) in currents; warm air rises and cool air sinks.
Convection currents in boiling water, in the atmosphere and in the mantle move heat.
Radiation is heat transfer by electromagnetic waves through space, like heat from the Sun or a campfire reaching you.
Conductors such as metals (copper, aluminum, iron, silver) transfer heat and electricity easily.
Insulators such as wood, plastic, rubber, foam, glass, wool, cloth and air slow the transfer of heat and electricity.
A metal pan gets hot quickly; a wooden or plastic handle stays cool because it is an insulator.
Thermos bottles, foam cups, blankets, coats and home insulation reduce heat transfer.
Wearing a wool coat or mittens keeps body heat in during cold weather.
Dark colors such as black absorb more light and heat energy from the Sun; dark clothing gets hotter.
Light colors such as white reflect more sunlight and stay cooler.
Shiny surfaces reflect heat and light.
A force is a push or a pull on an object.
Forces can change an object's speed, direction or shape.
Gravity is the force of attraction between all objects with mass; Earth's gravity pulls objects toward its center.
Gravity makes objects fall to the ground and gives objects weight.
Gravity keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth and the planets in orbit around the Sun.
The strength of gravity depends on mass and distance; more mass means more gravity, more distance means less gravity.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object and is measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Mass stays the same everywhere, but weight changes with gravity; you weigh less on the Moon than on Earth.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's gravity.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces rubbing together.
Friction produces heat and slows moving objects down.
Rough surfaces such as sandpaper, carpet, gravel and grass have more friction than smooth surfaces such as ice.
Smooth surfaces such as ice and polished floors have less friction; objects slide farther on ice.
Oil, grease, wax and lubricants reduce friction between moving parts.
Tires, shoe treads and sand on icy roads increase friction to prevent slipping.
Air resistance (drag) is friction from air that slows falling or moving objects; a parachute increases air resistance.
Water resistance slows objects moving through water; streamlined shapes reduce drag.
Magnetic force attracts or repels magnets and magnetic materials such as iron.
Balanced forces are equal in size and opposite in direction and do not change motion; net force is zero.
Unbalanced forces cause an object to start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.
Net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
Newton's first law (inertia): an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in motion; more mass means more inertia.
Seat belts protect passengers who keep moving forward because of inertia when a car stops suddenly.
Newton's second law: force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma); a larger force produces greater acceleration.
A heavier object needs more force to accelerate the same amount.
Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction force.
A rocket moves upward because exhaust gases push down and the gases push the rocket up (action and reaction).
Swimming and walking push backward on water or ground, which pushes you forward.
Speed is distance divided by time, measured in meters per second or miles per hour or kilometers per hour.
Average speed equals total distance traveled divided by total time.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is a change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
Motion is a change in position relative to a reference point over time.
A distance time graph with a steeper line shows a faster speed; a flat line shows the object is not moving.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
Work is done when a force moves an object a distance; work equals force times distance, measured in joules.
Power is the rate of doing work, measured in watts.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force.
The six simple machines are lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge and screw.
A lever is a bar that pivots on a fulcrum, like a seesaw, crowbar or bottle opener.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope that changes the direction of a force, used to raise a flag or lift heavy loads.
An inclined plane is a ramp or sloping surface that makes it easier to move a load up to a higher place.
A longer ramp needs less force but a greater distance.
A wedge is two inclined planes that split or cut things apart, like an axe, knife or doorstop.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder, used to hold things together, like a jar lid.
A wheel and axle, like a doorknob, steering wheel or bicycle, makes it easier to turn or move loads.
Compound machines such as scissors, bicycles and wheelbarrows combine simple machines.
Mechanical advantage is how many times a machine multiplies force.
Electricity is the flow of electrons or electric charge (current) through a conductor.
An electric circuit is a complete closed path through which electric current flows.
A circuit needs a power source (battery), wires (conductors) and a load such as a bulb; a switch opens and closes it.
An open circuit (broken path, switch off) stops current; a closed circuit lets current flow and the bulb lights.
In a series circuit there is only one path; if one bulb burns out all the bulbs go out.
In a parallel circuit there are several paths; if one bulb burns out the others stay lit; homes are wired in parallel.
A battery stores chemical energy and supplies electrical energy; it has positive and negative terminals.
Voltage is measured in volts, current in amperes (amps) and resistance in ohms.
Electrical conductors such as copper wire, silver, aluminum, metal and salt water let electricity flow easily.
Electrical insulators such as rubber, plastic, glass, wood and dry cloth do not let electricity flow; wires are coated with plastic.
Static electricity is a buildup of electric charge on an object, as when rubbing a balloon on hair.
Like charges repel and opposite charges attract.
Lightning is a giant discharge of static electricity in a storm.
A fuse or circuit breaker protects a circuit from too much current.
Electrical safety: never put metal in outlets and keep electric devices away from water.
A magnet has a north pole and a south pole.
Like magnetic poles repel each other; opposite (unlike) poles attract each other.
Magnets attract objects made of iron, steel, nickel and cobalt, such as nails and paper clips.
Aluminum, copper, gold, wood, plastic, glass, paper and rubber are nonmagnetic materials.
A magnetic field surrounds a magnet; iron filings show the magnetic field lines.
Magnetic force is strongest at the poles of a magnet.
A compass needle is a small magnet that points to Earth's magnetic north pole.
Earth acts like a giant magnet with a magnetic field from its iron core.
An electromagnet is a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core (nail) with electric current; it can be turned on and off.
More coils of wire or more current (more batteries) make an electromagnet stronger.
Electromagnets are used in motors, doorbells, speakers and cranes that lift scrap metal.
Moving a magnet inside a coil of wire produces (induces) an electric current; this is how generators work.
Power plants burn fossil fuels to boil water into steam that turns turbines and generators to make electricity.

A testable question can be answered by doing an experiment and collecting measurable data.
Scientific questions can be answered with evidence from observations and experiments; opinions and values cannot.
Questions about which is prettiest, best tasting or most fun are opinions, not scientific questions.
Before starting an experiment a student should write a hypothesis and plan the procedure.
After collecting data a student should analyze the data and draw a conclusion.
If the data do not support the hypothesis, the scientist should revise the hypothesis and test again.
A procedure is a list of steps to follow in an experiment, so others can repeat it.
Writing clear step by step procedures allows other scientists to repeat the investigation and check results.
Recording data in a data table keeps observations organized.
Students should record observations immediately and accurately in a notebook.
Measuring with standard metric units allows scientists everywhere to compare results.
Using the same measuring tool each time reduces error.
Results are more reliable when an investigation uses many trials, many test subjects or a large sample.
Testing only one plant or one trial gives unreliable results.
To test the effect of fertilizer on plant growth, give different amounts of fertilizer and keep water, light, soil and plant type the same.
To test which paper towel absorbs the most water, use the same amount of water and same size towel for each brand.
To test how temperature affects dissolving, change only the water temperature and keep the amount of water and sugar the same.
To test which ramp height makes a car roll farthest, change only the ramp height and use the same car and surface.
To find whether light affects seed growth, put seeds in light and in dark and keep water and temperature the same.
In a plant growth experiment the height of the plant is the dependent variable that is measured.
The variable that is changed on purpose is the independent variable, plotted on the x axis.
The dependent variable is measured and plotted on the y axis.
A control or control group is the standard for comparison that receives no change.
Scientists compare the experimental group with the control group.
Observations use the senses: seeing, hearing, touching, smelling and tasting (never taste in lab).
Qualitative observations describe color, texture, smell and shape.
Quantitative observations use numbers, such as mass, length, temperature and time.
An inference explains an observation; seeing wet ground and inferring that it rained is an inference.
A prediction is an educated guess about what will happen next based on a pattern in data.
Patterns in data such as trends in a line graph help scientists make predictions.
Scientists use graphs to show relationships between variables.
A line graph is best for showing how something changes over time, such as temperature during a day or plant height each week.
A bar graph is best for comparing amounts, such as the number of each type of bird seen.
A circle graph (pie chart) shows percentages of a whole, such as the gases in air.
A data table organizes data in rows and columns.
Maps, diagrams, globes, physical models and computer models help scientists explain ideas.
A globe is a model of Earth; an orrery is a model of the solar system.
Models help scientists study things that are too large, too small, too slow, too fast or too dangerous.
A model of the water cycle or a model of a volcano shows a process in a simple way.
Scientists use computer models to predict weather, hurricanes and climate change.
Scientists communicate results by writing reports, publishing articles and presenting at conferences.
Sharing data lets other scientists check, repeat and build on the work.
When different scientists get the same results, the conclusion is more trustworthy.
Scientific explanations must be based on evidence and can change with new evidence.
New technology, like better microscopes and telescopes, leads to new discoveries.
Scientists work in teams and collaborate around the world.
A scientist should be honest, curious, skeptical and open minded.
An error in measurement can be reduced by repeating measurements and using precise tools.
Precise tools such as a digital balance give more exact measurements.
Accuracy means how close a measurement is to the real value.
Estimation helps check if an answer is reasonable.
The mean (average) is found by adding all values and dividing by the number of values.
The range is the difference between the highest and lowest values.
An outlier is a data value very different from the rest, which may come from an error.
Field studies observe organisms in their natural environment.
Scientists use tags, collars and tracking devices to study animal migration.
Satellites and remote sensing let scientists study Earth from space.
Core samples of ice and rock help scientists learn about Earth's past climate.
Tree rings show the age of a tree and past climate; wide rings mean good growing years.
Scientists use thermometers, rain gauges and data loggers to record weather data over time.
Scientists classify organisms using a dichotomous key based on observable features.
A hypothesis is often written as an if then statement.
If a student wants to know whether plants grow better with music, the student should grow plants with and without music under the same conditions.
Students should identify safety hazards before beginning an investigation.
In an investigation about bacteria, students should wear gloves, keep samples covered and wash hands; they should avoid food and drinks in the laboratory.
Students should not eat or drink in the lab because food can be contaminated with chemicals or bacteria.
Hot plates, open flames and glassware require extra care; never leave a flame unattended.
Wearing closed toe shoes and tying back long hair prevents lab accidents.
Electrical equipment should be kept away from water.
Waste such as broken glass goes in a special container.
Animals used in classroom investigations must be treated with care and returned to their habitat.
Ethics in science: scientists must not harm people and must get permission from participants.
Engineers test materials for strength, durability, weight, cost and safety before choosing them.
A good design solution meets the criteria and stays within the constraints.
Engineers improve a design after testing a prototype and finding its weaknesses.
Building a model bridge and testing how much weight it holds is an engineering investigation.
Testing several designs and comparing results helps choose the best solution.
Technology can have both benefits and drawbacks for society and the environment.
Scientists in different fields: biologists study living things; geologists study rocks and Earth; meteorologists study weather; astronomers study space; chemists study matter; physicists study energy and forces; ecologists study ecosystems; paleontologists study fossils; botanists study plants; zoologists study animals; oceanographers study oceans.
All living things (organisms) are made of one or more cells.
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function of all living things.
Cell theory: all organisms are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from other cells.
Living things grow, reproduce, respond to their environment, use energy, need food and water, and get rid of wastes.
Nonliving things such as rocks, water and air do not grow, reproduce or need food.
Unicellular (single celled) organisms such as bacteria, amoebas, paramecia and yeast are made of only one cell.
Multicellular organisms such as plants, animals and people are made of many cells.
Levels of organization: cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, and organ systems form an organism.
A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together to do a job, such as muscle tissue.
An organ is made of different tissues working together, such as the heart, lungs, stomach, brain or leaf.
The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
The nucleus contains DNA (genetic material) and controls the cell's activities.
The cytoplasm is the jelly like fluid inside the cell where organelles are found.
Mitochondria release energy from food (glucose) by cellular respiration; they are the powerhouse of the cell.
Chloroplasts in plant cells contain green chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis to make food.
The cell wall is a rigid layer outside the cell membrane of plant cells that gives support and shape.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole.
Vacuoles store water, food and wastes.
Ribosomes make proteins.
The microscope was needed to discover cells; Robert Hooke first described cells in cork.
Cells divide by mitosis to make two identical cells for growth and repair of the body.
Meiosis produces sex cells (gametes: sperm and egg) with half the number of chromosomes.
Photosynthesis is the process plants use to make their own food (glucose sugar) from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide, releasing oxygen.
Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplasts of leaves using chlorophyll to capture light energy.
Plants take in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny openings in leaves called stomata and release oxygen.
Plants need sunlight, water, air (carbon dioxide), nutrients from soil and space to grow.
Cellular respiration uses oxygen to break down glucose (sugar) to release energy, producing carbon dioxide and water.
Both plants and animals carry out cellular respiration.
Producers such as plants and algae make their own food using energy from sunlight.
Roots absorb water and nutrients (minerals) from the soil and anchor (hold) the plant in the ground.
Root hairs increase surface area for absorbing water.
Stems support the plant and carry water and nutrients between roots and leaves.
Xylem tubes carry water up from the roots; phloem tubes carry food (sugar) from the leaves to the rest of the plant.
Leaves make food for the plant by photosynthesis.
Flowers are the reproductive parts of flowering plants that make seeds.
Pollen is made in the stamen (anther); the pistil contains the ovary where seeds form.
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the stamen to the pistil, often by bees, butterflies, hummingbirds, insects or wind.
Bees carry pollen from flower to flower while they collect nectar.
Brightly colored, sweet smelling flowers attract pollinators such as bees and butterflies.
After pollination and fertilization the ovary becomes a fruit that contains seeds.
A fruit is the part of a plant that contains and protects seeds.
A seed contains a plant embryo and stored food (cotyledon) protected by a seed coat.
Germination is when a seed begins to sprout and grow; seeds need water, warmth and oxygen to germinate.
Seeds are dispersed (spread) by wind, water, animals and explosion; maple seeds spin in the wind, burrs stick to animal fur.
Animals eat fruits and spread the seeds in their droppings.
Plant life cycle: seed, germination, seedling, adult plant, flower, pollination, fruit and seeds.
Some plants reproduce without seeds: ferns and mosses use spores.
Plants can reproduce asexually from bulbs, tubers (potatoes), runners and cuttings.
Conifers (pine trees) produce seeds in cones and keep needle leaves all year (evergreen).
Deciduous trees lose their leaves in autumn (fall) and grow new ones in spring.
Leaves change color in the fall because chlorophyll breaks down.
Tropisms are plant growth responses to stimuli.
Phototropism: plants grow and bend toward light.
Gravitropism (geotropism): roots grow downward with gravity and stems grow upward.
Thigmotropism: vines and tendrils grow around objects they touch.
Cactus plants store water in thick stems and have spines instead of leaves to reduce water loss in deserts.
A waxy cuticle on leaves reduces water loss.
Plants wilt when they do not get enough water.
Fertilizer adds nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium to soil to help plants grow.
Living things are classified into groups by their shared characteristics (traits) and structures.
The classification levels are kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
A species is a group of organisms that can mate and produce fertile offspring.
Scientific names use genus and species (binomial nomenclature), such as Homo sapiens.
A dichotomous key uses pairs of descriptions to identify organisms.
Kingdoms include animals, plants, fungi, protists and bacteria.
Fungi such as mushrooms, molds and yeast cannot make their own food; they absorb food from dead or living matter and reproduce with spores.
Protists such as amoebas, paramecia, euglena and algae are mostly single celled organisms with a nucleus.
Bacteria are single celled organisms without a nucleus (prokaryotes).
Vertebrates are animals with a backbone (spine): fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.
Invertebrates are animals without a backbone, such as insects, spiders, worms, jellyfish, snails, clams, octopuses, crabs and sea stars.
Most animals on Earth are invertebrates.
Mammals have hair or fur, are warm blooded, breathe with lungs, give birth to live young and feed their young milk.
Whales, dolphins, bats, humans, dogs, cats and mice are mammals.
Birds have feathers, wings, beaks, hollow bones, are warm blooded and lay hard shelled eggs.
Reptiles such as snakes, lizards, turtles and crocodiles have dry scaly skin, are cold blooded and lay leathery eggs on land.
Amphibians such as frogs, toads and salamanders have moist skin, live in water and on land, and lay jelly eggs in water.
Fish live in water, breathe with gills, have scales and fins and are cold blooded.
Warm blooded (endothermic) animals keep a constant body temperature; cold blooded (ectothermic) animals' body temperature changes with the environment.
Cold blooded reptiles bask in the sun to warm up.
Insects have six legs, three body parts (head, thorax, abdomen), antennae and an exoskeleton; many have wings.
Spiders and scorpions are arachnids with eight legs and two body parts.
Arthropods such as insects, spiders and crabs have jointed legs and an exoskeleton that they molt.
Mollusks such as snails, clams, squid and octopuses have soft bodies, often with a shell.
Worms such as earthworms have long soft bodies without legs.
A life cycle is the series of stages an organism goes through during its life: birth, growth, reproduction and death.
Complete metamorphosis has four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult, as in butterflies, moths, beetles, flies and ants.
A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly or moth; it forms a chrysalis or cocoon (pupa).
Incomplete metamorphosis has three stages: egg, nymph and adult, as in grasshoppers, crickets and dragonflies.
Frogs go through metamorphosis: egg, tadpole (with gills and tail), froglet, adult frog (with lungs and legs).
Tadpoles live in water and breathe with gills; adult frogs breathe with lungs and skin.
Mammals grow inside the mother and are born alive; young mammals look like small adults.
Sexual reproduction needs two parents and produces offspring with a mix of traits from both parents (genetic variation).
Asexual reproduction needs only one parent and produces offspring identical to the parent, as in budding, binary fission, runners and spores.
Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, splitting in two.
Yeast and hydra reproduce by budding.
Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to offspring.
Inherited traits are passed from parents through genes, such as eye color, hair color, flower color, height and blood type.
Acquired traits are learned or developed during an organism's lifetime, such as scars, language, riding a bike, reading or big muscles from exercise.
Learned behaviors are acquired through experience, such as a dog learning tricks.
Instincts are inherited behaviors that animals are born with, such as spiders spinning webs, birds building nests and migrating.
DNA is the genetic material in chromosomes in the nucleus that carries instructions (genes) for traits.
Genes are segments of DNA on chromosomes that code for proteins and traits.
Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes (46 total).
Each parent passes one allele (copy of a gene) for each trait to offspring.
A dominant allele (capital letter) shows its trait whenever present; a recessive allele (lowercase) shows only when two copies are present.
A Punnett square shows the possible genotypes of offspring and predicts probability of traits.
Genotype is the genetic makeup (alleles); phenotype is the physical appearance of a trait.
Homozygous means two identical alleles (TT or tt); heterozygous means two different alleles (Tt), a hybrid.
Gregor Mendel studied heredity using pea plants and is the father of genetics.
A mutation is a change in DNA that can produce a new trait.
Selective breeding (artificial selection) is when people breed plants or animals with desired traits, such as dogs, cows or corn.
Genetic engineering changes DNA to give organisms useful traits; cloning makes genetically identical copies.
Traits can also be influenced by the environment, such as plant height depending on sunlight and nutrients.
Adaptations are inherited traits that help an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
Structural adaptations are body parts such as thick fur, sharp claws, webbed feet, long necks, beaks, spines and camouflage coloring.
Behavioral adaptations are actions such as migration, hibernation, living in herds and nocturnal activity.
Camouflage is coloring or patterns that help an animal blend in with its surroundings to hide from predators or prey.
Some animals such as chameleons, octopuses and cuttlefish have special cells that change color quickly to hide from predators.
Arctic animals such as polar bears and snowshoe hares have white fur for camouflage in snow.
Mimicry is when an organism looks like another organism, such as a harmless fly that looks like a bee.
Warning coloration: bright colors on poison dart frogs and wasps warn predators.
Thick fur, blubber (fat) and small ears help animals stay warm in cold climates.
Polar bears and seals have thick fat (blubber) to stay warm in the cold Arctic.
Desert animals such as camels and kangaroo rats conserve water; many are nocturnal and active at night to avoid heat.
Large ears help desert animals such as fennec foxes and jackrabbits release heat.
Webbed feet help ducks, geese and frogs swim.
Bird beaks are adapted to their food: sharp hooked beaks tear meat, short thick beaks crack seeds, long thin beaks sip nectar from flowers.
Hawks, eagles and owls have sharp talons and hooked beaks to catch prey; owls see well at night.
Sharp teeth help carnivores tear meat; flat teeth help herbivores grind plants.
Long necks help giraffes reach leaves high in trees.
Hibernation is a deep sleep through winter when an animal's body temperature, heart rate and breathing slow down to save energy; bears, bats and groundhogs hibernate.
Migration is moving long distances to another region with the seasons to find food, water or warmer weather; birds, whales, monarch butterflies and caribou migrate.
Many birds migrate south in the fall when food becomes scarce.
Animals store food or grow thicker fur to prepare for winter.
Natural selection: organisms with traits better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more, passing those traits to offspring.
Evolution is the change in species over long periods of time; Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection.
Darwin studied finches with different beaks on the Galapagos Islands.
Variation among individuals in a population allows natural selection.
Extinction occurs when all members of a species die out, often because the environment changes faster than they can adapt.
Endangered species have very few individuals left and could become extinct; habitat loss is the main cause.
Homologous structures (similar bones in arms of humans, wings of bats, flippers of whales) show common ancestry.
Peppered moths: dark moths survived better on soot covered trees during the industrial revolution.
Bacteria can evolve resistance to antibiotics through natural selection.

Natural materials come from plants, animals or Earth: wood, cotton, wool, silk, stone, clay.
Manufactured (synthetic) materials are made by people: plastic, nylon, glass, steel, concrete.

Durable materials last a long time without wearing out.

Reflective materials such as mirrors and aluminum foil bounce light.
Magnetic materials: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.

A boat floats because it displaces enough water; clay shaped like a boat floats while a clay ball sinks.
A sweater keeps a person warm by trapping air, a good insulator.

Engineers choose materials based on strength, weight, flexibility, cost, durability and safety.
Recycled plastic bottles can be made into fleece clothing.
Bamboo grows quickly and is a renewable building material.

The best material to cook food quickly is a good heat conductor such as metal.

Roofs in hot climates are often painted white to reflect sunlight.

Hard smooth surfaces like tile and concrete reflect sound and cause echoes.

Glass, clear plastic wrap, water and air are transparent.
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space (volume).
All matter is made of tiny particles called atoms and molecules.
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume.
Solids keep their own shape; particles in a solid are packed tightly and vibrate in place.
A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container.
Liquids flow and can be poured; particles slide past each other.
A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume; it spreads out to fill its container.
Gas particles move fast and far apart; gases can be compressed.
Plasma is a state of matter found in stars, the Sun and lightning.
Particles move faster when heated and temperature increases; particles slow down when cooled.
Adding heat energy makes particles move faster and spread apart.
Removing heat energy makes particles move slower and closer together.
Melting is a solid changing into a liquid when heat is added, like ice melting into water.
Freezing is a liquid changing into a solid when heat is removed, like water freezing into ice.
Evaporation is a liquid changing into a gas (vapor), like water evaporating from a puddle in the sun.
Boiling is rapid evaporation of a liquid when heated to its boiling point; bubbles of vapor form.
Condensation is a gas or water vapor cooling and changing into a liquid, like water droplets on a cold glass.
Dew forms by condensation of water vapor on cool grass in the morning.
Sublimation is a solid changing directly into a gas, like dry ice (frozen carbon dioxide).
Deposition is a gas changing directly into a solid, like frost forming.
Water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius (32 degrees Fahrenheit).
Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius (212 degrees Fahrenheit) at sea level.
The melting point of ice is 0 degrees Celsius.
Ice is solid water; water vapor or steam is water as a gas.
Water expands when it freezes, so ice is less dense than liquid water and ice floats.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, measured in liters, milliliters or cubic centimeters.
Volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder or measuring cup in milliliters.
Volume of an irregular solid object can be measured by water displacement in a graduated cylinder.
Volume of a rectangular box equals length times width times height.
Density is mass divided by volume (mass per unit volume), in grams per cubic centimeter.
Objects less dense than water float; objects more dense than water sink.
Wood, cork, oil and ice float on water because they are less dense.
Rocks, iron, steel and metal coins sink in water because they are more dense.
Oil floats on top of water because oil is less dense than water and does not mix.
Physical properties include color, shape, size, texture, mass, hardness, density, odor, luster, melting point and boiling point.
Texture is how a surface feels: rough, smooth, soft, bumpy.
Hardness is a physical property describing resistance to scratching.
Magnetism is a physical property: iron, nickel and cobalt are attracted to magnets.
Flexibility: rubber and plastic bend; glass is brittle and breaks.
Solubility is how well a substance dissolves in a liquid such as water.
Sugar and salt dissolve in water to form a solution.
Sand, oil and pebbles are insoluble in water.
Stirring, heating the water and crushing into smaller pieces make sugar dissolve faster.
A solution is a mixture where one substance (solute) dissolves evenly into another (solvent).
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
Salt water is a solution; the salt can be separated by evaporating the water.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined that keep their own properties and can be separated.
Mixtures can be separated by filtering, sifting, magnets, evaporation, settling, decanting or picking by hand.
A magnet can separate iron filings or steel paper clips from a mixture of sand.
A filter or screen separates sand, gravel or solids from a liquid.
Trail mix, salad, sand and gravel, and soil are examples of mixtures.
A compound is two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, such as water or table salt.
Water is a compound made of hydrogen and oxygen (H2O): two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Carbon dioxide (CO2) is a compound of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
Table salt is sodium chloride (NaCl), a compound of sodium and chlorine.
Compounds have properties different from the elements that form them.
An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom and cannot be broken down chemically.
Examples of elements: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, iron, gold, copper, aluminum, helium, sodium.
A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together.
Oxygen gas is a molecule of two oxygen atoms (O2) joined by a double covalent bond sharing two pairs of electrons.
Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons; ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred between atoms.
Ionic bonds form between metals and nonmetals, such as sodium and chlorine in salt.
A physical change changes size, shape or state but makes no new substance.
Examples of physical changes: cutting paper, melting ice, freezing water, boiling, tearing, crushing, dissolving sugar, bending wire.
A chemical change or chemical reaction produces a new substance with new properties.
Examples of chemical changes: burning wood, rusting iron, cooking or baking food, digesting food, rotting fruit, tarnishing silver.
Signs of a chemical reaction: gas bubbles, color change, light, heat or temperature change, odor, a precipitate solid forms.
Mixing baking soda and vinegar is a chemical reaction that produces carbon dioxide gas bubbles.
Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water (oxidation).
Burning (combustion) is a chemical reaction of fuel with oxygen that releases heat and light energy and produces carbon dioxide and water.
Fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat.
Exothermic reactions release heat energy; endothermic reactions absorb heat energy and feel cold.
In a chemical reaction atoms are rearranged; reactants form products.
Law of conservation of mass: mass is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction or physical change; total mass stays the same.
When ice melts in a closed container the mass stays the same.
A chemical formula shows the elements and number of atoms in a compound.
A chemical equation must be balanced with the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons have a positive charge and are in the nucleus.
Neutrons have no charge (neutral) and are in the nucleus.
Electrons have a negative charge and move around the nucleus in energy levels or an electron cloud.
The nucleus is the dense center of an atom that contains protons and neutrons and most of the mass.
The atomic number is the number of protons in an atom and identifies the element.
The mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons.
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
Ions are atoms that have gained or lost electrons and have a charge.
The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number into rows (periods) and columns (groups or families).
Elements in the same group or column of the periodic table have similar chemical properties and the same number of valence electrons.
Valence electrons are in the outer energy level and determine chemical bonding and reactivity.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Metals are found on the left side of the periodic table.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors (insulators); they are on the right side of the periodic table.
Metalloids such as silicon have properties of both metals and nonmetals and are semiconductors.
Noble gases such as helium, neon and argon are unreactive (inert) and have full outer electron shells.
Alkali metals such as sodium and potassium are very reactive with water.
Halogens such as chlorine and fluorine are very reactive nonmetals.
Helium is lighter than air and is used to fill balloons that float.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant element in the universe.
Oxygen is needed for burning and breathing; it makes up about 21 percent of air.
Nitrogen makes up about 78 percent of Earth's atmosphere.
Carbon is found in all living things and organic compounds; diamond and graphite are forms of carbon.
Acids taste sour, have a pH less than 7 and turn blue litmus paper red; lemon juice and vinegar are acids.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery, have a pH greater than 7 and turn red litmus paper blue; soap, baking soda and ammonia are bases.
A neutral substance such as pure water has a pH of 7.
An acid and a base react in a neutralization reaction to form water and a salt.
Indicators such as litmus paper change color to show acids and bases.
Alloys such as steel, bronze and brass are mixtures of metals.
Steel is made mostly of iron and carbon and is strong.
Plastic is made from petroleum (oil).
Glass is made by melting sand.
Paper is made from wood fibers from trees.
Cotton and wool are natural fibers; nylon and polyester are synthetic fibers.
Rubber is flexible, waterproof and an insulator.
Aluminum foil is a thin, light metal that is a good conductor of heat.
Copper is used for electrical wires because it is a good conductor of electricity.
A material that is waterproof keeps water out, such as plastic or rubber raincoats.
Absorbent materials such as sponges, paper towels and cloth soak up water.
Transparent materials such as clear glass let light pass through; you can see clearly through them.
Translucent materials such as wax paper and frosted glass let some light through.
Opaque materials such as wood, metal and cardboard block light and make shadows.
Temperature measures the average kinetic energy (motion) of particles, how hot or cold something is.
Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
Heat flows from a warmer object to a cooler object until both reach the same temperature.
A substance's state depends on its temperature and pressure.
Air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, with carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Air has mass and takes up space; a balloon filled with air weighs more than an empty balloon.
Gases expand when heated and contract when cooled; a balloon gets bigger in a warm room.
A balloon shrinks when placed in a freezer because the cold air inside contracts.
Pressure of a gas increases when it is heated in a closed container.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled (thermal expansion); bridges have expansion joints.
Liquid in a thermometer expands and rises when it is heated.
Evaporation happens faster when it is hot, windy, dry and the surface area is larger.
A wet towel dries faster in the sun and wind because water evaporates.
Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so salt melts ice on roads.
Sugar is a carbohydrate compound made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds of molecules in food, fuel and batteries.
Examples of elements: oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, carbon, helium, neon, iron, copper, gold, silver, aluminum, sulfur, mercury.
Examples of compounds: water, carbon dioxide, table salt (sodium chloride), sugar, baking soda, rust (iron oxide), ammonia, methane.
Examples of mixtures: air, soil, salad, trail mix, sand and water, salt water, lemonade, concrete, milk, granite.

Scientists ask questions, make observations, form a hypothesis, test it with an experiment, collect data, analyze results and draw conclusions.
A hypothesis is a testable prediction or possible explanation that can be tested by an experiment.
An observation is information gathered using the senses or tools.
An inference is a conclusion or explanation based on observations and prior knowledge.
A prediction is a statement about what will happen in the future based on evidence or patterns.
A conclusion is a statement that explains the results of an experiment and whether data support the hypothesis.
A theory is a well tested explanation supported by much evidence; a scientific law describes what always happens.
Scientific knowledge can change when new evidence is discovered.
In a controlled experiment only one variable is changed at a time.
The independent (manipulated) variable is the one factor the scientist changes on purpose.
The dependent (responding) variable is what is measured or observed in response to the change.
Controlled variables (constants) are kept the same in all groups so the test is fair.
A control group does not receive the treatment and is used for comparison.
A fair test changes only one variable and keeps all other conditions the same.
Repeating an experiment many times (more trials) and using a large sample size make results more reliable and valid.
Other scientists repeat experiments to check that results can be reproduced (replicated).
Scientists share results by publishing reports so others can review and repeat the investigation (peer review).
Data are recorded in tables, charts and graphs, and in a science notebook or journal.
Bar graphs compare amounts in different categories.
Line graphs show changes over time or continuous data.
Pie charts (circle graphs) show parts of a whole as percentages.
Qualitative data describe qualities such as color or texture; quantitative data are numbers and measurements.
Accurate measurements are close to the true value; precise measurements are close to each other.
Models represent objects or processes that are too big, too small, too far or too dangerous to study directly, such as a model of the solar system or an atom.
Models have limitations because they are simpler than the real thing.
Computer simulations model weather, climate and population changes.
The metric system (International System, SI) is used by scientists: meter, liter, gram, kilogram, second, Celsius.
Length is measured in meters, centimeters, millimeters and kilometers with a ruler, meter stick or measuring tape.
Mass is measured in grams or kilograms with a balance (triple beam balance or electronic scale).
Weight and force are measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Liquid volume is measured in milliliters or liters with a graduated cylinder, beaker or measuring cup.
Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit with a thermometer.
Time is measured in seconds, minutes and hours with a stopwatch, timer or clock.
A meter is 100 centimeters; a kilometer is 1000 meters; a kilogram is 1000 grams; a liter is 1000 milliliters.
A microscope magnifies very small objects such as cells and microorganisms.
A hand lens or magnifying glass makes small objects such as insects, rocks and leaves look larger.
A telescope makes distant objects such as stars, planets and the Moon look closer.
Binoculars help observe distant birds and animals.
A compass shows direction (north, south, east, west) using a magnetized needle.
A calculator and computer help analyze data.
A pH meter or litmus paper measures acidity.

A prototype is an early model built to test a design.
Constraints are limits on a design, such as cost, time, materials, size and safety.
Criteria are the requirements a design must meet to be successful.
Trade offs are giving up one benefit to gain another in a design.

Comparing and contrasting finds similarities and differences.
A variable that is not controlled can affect the results of an experiment and make it unfair.

Measuring several times and averaging the results reduces error.

Speed equals distance divided by time; a car traveling 100 kilometers in 2 hours has a speed of 50 kilometers per hour.
Distance equals speed multiplied by time.
Objects that travel the same distance in less time are faster.
A moving object changes position relative to a reference point.
Motion is relative: a passenger on a train is moving relative to the ground but not relative to the seat.
An object at rest stays at rest until a force acts on it.
Objects need a force to start moving, stop moving or change direction.
The greater the mass of an object, the more force needed to move it or stop it.
A larger force on the same mass produces a greater acceleration.
Heavier objects and lighter objects fall at the same rate without air resistance.
In free fall objects speed up as they fall because gravity pulls on them.
Terminal velocity is reached when air resistance equals the force of gravity on a falling object.
An object moving in a circle constantly changes direction and so is accelerating; a centripetal force pulls it toward the center.
Weight equals mass times the acceleration due to gravity.
An object weighs less on the Moon because the Moon has less mass and weaker gravity.
Jupiter has much stronger gravity than Earth, so objects would weigh more there.
Buoyant force pushes up on objects in water; objects float when buoyant force equals their weight.
Ships made of steel float because their shape displaces a large volume of water.
Air pressure pushes on everything from all directions.
Water pressure increases with depth.
Pressure equals force divided by area; snowshoes spread weight over a large area to prevent sinking.
A sharp knife cuts easily because the force is on a small area.
Friction between tires and road lets cars start, turn and stop.

Ball bearings and wheels reduce friction by rolling instead of sliding.
Streamlined shapes of airplanes, cars, fish and birds reduce air or water resistance.
Lift on airplane wings and thrust from engines help planes fly; drag and weight oppose flight.
Work is done only when a force moves an object in the direction of the force.
Holding a heavy box still does no work in the scientific sense.
Machines cannot reduce the amount of work but make it easier by reducing force or changing direction.
Using a ramp to lift a load requires less force but over a longer distance.

A fixed pulley changes the direction of the force; a movable pulley reduces the force needed.

Friction changes kinetic energy into thermal energy (heat).

Power lines carry electricity from power plants to homes.

Heat always flows from hot to cold.

Double paned windows trap air to insulate homes.
Feathers, fur and fat insulate animals.

A refrigerator moves heat from inside to outside.
Waves reflect, refract, diffract and are absorbed.

A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.

The universe contains billions of galaxies; the big bang theory explains the origin of the universe.

Space telescopes such as Hubble orbit above the atmosphere and get clearer images.
Satellites orbit Earth and are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation and mapping.

Rockets need to reach high speed to escape Earth's gravity.

The Sun's gravity is strongest because it has the most mass in the solar system.
Earth is the right distance from the Sun to have liquid water.
The Sun is about 150 million kilometers (93 million miles) from Earth.
A day on Earth is 24 hours; a year is about 365 days.
Earth spins counterclockwise (west to east) when viewed from above the North Pole.
Because of Earth's rotation, the Sun, Moon and stars appear to rise in the east and set in the west.
The Sun is highest in the sky at noon.
In the Northern Hemisphere the noon Sun is in the southern part of the sky.
The Sun's path across the sky is higher in summer and lower in winter.
The length of daylight changes with the seasons; days are longest in summer and shortest in winter.

Seasons are reversed in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres; December is summer in Australia.

The Tropic of Cancer and Tropic of Capricorn mark where the Sun can be directly overhead.
Time zones exist because Earth rotates 15 degrees each hour.
Shadows point away from the Sun; in the morning shadows point west, in the evening east.
A sundial uses a shadow to tell time.
The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day.
The Moon goes through a full cycle of phases in about a month, about 29.5 days.
A waxing moon is growing, showing more of its lit side each night; a waning moon is shrinking.
About one week after a new moon is the first quarter moon; about two weeks after is the full moon.
A full moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise.
We always see the same side of the Moon; the far side is not always dark.
The Moon's surface has extreme temperatures because it has no atmosphere to hold heat.
The Moon was probably formed from debris when a large object hit early Earth.
Moon rocks brought back by Apollo astronauts are similar to Earth rocks.
Neil Armstrong was the first person to walk on the Moon in 1969.
Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted.
A solar eclipse can only be seen from a small area of Earth; never look directly at the Sun.
During a total lunar eclipse the Moon can look red.
High tides occur on the side of Earth facing the Moon and on the opposite side.
The Sun also affects tides; neap tides (smallest tidal range) happen at quarter moons.
The Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth; over one million Earths could fit inside it.
The Sun's surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius; its core is about 15 million degrees.
The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old and halfway through its life.
The Sun will become a red giant and then a white dwarf.
Solar wind from the Sun causes auroras (northern lights) near the poles.
Earth's magnetic field protects it from the solar wind.
The Sun's energy reaches Earth as electromagnetic radiation: visible light, infrared and ultraviolet.
Earth's atmosphere filters out harmful radiation such as x-rays and most ultraviolet light.
Mercury has extreme temperatures, very hot during the day and very cold at night, because it has almost no atmosphere.
Venus rotates backward and very slowly; its day is longer than its year.
Venus and Mercury have no moons; Earth has one moon; Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos.
Mars has seasons because its axis is tilted; it has dust storms and dry riverbeds that suggest water once flowed.
Jupiter's moons include Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto; Ganymede is the largest moon in the solar system.
Europa may have liquid water under its icy surface.
Saturn is the least dense planet and would float in water.
Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a thick atmosphere.
Uranus and Neptune are blue because of methane gas in their atmospheres.
The gas giants are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
Gas giants have no solid surface.
The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year and the colder it is.
Mercury has the shortest year, 88 days; Neptune has the longest, about 165 Earth years.
Jupiter has the shortest day, about 10 hours.
Planets do not make their own light; they reflect sunlight.
Planets appear to wander across the background stars over weeks and months.
The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a spinning cloud of gas and dust (solar nebula).
The Kuiper belt beyond Neptune contains icy bodies and dwarf planets such as Pluto.
The Oort cloud far beyond the planets is the source of long period comets.
Halley's comet returns about every 76 years.
Comet tails always point away from the Sun because of solar wind.
Meteor showers happen when Earth passes through debris left by a comet.
Most meteoroids burn up in the atmosphere because of friction with air.
Asteroid impacts formed craters on Earth, the Moon and other planets.
Stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
The apparent brightness of a star depends on its true brightness (luminosity) and its distance from Earth.
A star that appears dim may be very bright but very far away.
Red stars are the coolest stars; blue stars are the hottest.
The Sun is a yellow main sequence star of average temperature and size.

Massive stars burn fuel faster and have shorter lives than small stars.
A supernova is the explosion of a massive star; it creates heavy elements.
Neutron stars and black holes form from the cores of massive stars after supernovas.
Many stars are part of binary star systems.
A constellation's stars are usually at very different distances from Earth.
Constellations appear to move across the sky during the night because Earth rotates, and they change with the seasons because Earth revolves.
Polaris appears stationary because it is almost directly above Earth's North Pole.
Light from distant stars and galaxies takes many years to reach Earth, so we see them as they were in the past.
The Milky Way galaxy contains over 100 billion stars; the solar system is in one of its spiral arms.
The Andromeda galaxy is the nearest large galaxy to the Milky Way.
Galaxies are moving away from each other, showing the universe is expanding.
Redshift of light from galaxies shows they are moving away.
Scientists use spectroscopes to learn what stars are made of from their spectra.
Radio telescopes detect radio waves from space.
Telescopes on mountains or in space avoid light pollution and atmospheric blurring.
Refracting telescopes use lenses; reflecting telescopes use mirrors.
The Hubble Space Telescope orbits Earth and takes clear images of distant galaxies.
The International Space Station orbits Earth and astronauts live and do experiments there.
Satellites in geostationary orbit stay above the same spot on Earth.
GPS uses satellites to find locations on Earth.
Space probes such as Voyager have traveled beyond the planets.
Rovers such as Curiosity and Perseverance explore the surface of Mars.
In microgravity astronauts' muscles and bones weaken, so they must exercise.
Astronauts need to bring oxygen, water and food because space has no air.
Space suits provide oxygen, pressure and protection from temperature and radiation.

A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from place to place without carrying matter.
Waves transfer energy, not matter.
Mechanical waves such as sound and water waves need a medium (solid, liquid or gas) to travel through.
In a transverse wave the particles of the medium move up and down, perpendicular to the direction the wave travels.
Light waves and waves on a rope or string are transverse waves with crests and troughs.
In a longitudinal (compression) wave the particles move back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels.
Sound waves are longitudinal waves with compressions and rarefactions.
Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest of a wave.
Frequency is the number of waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz.
Amplitude is the height of a wave, related to its energy; larger amplitude carries more energy.
Higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths.
Sound is produced by vibrations, back and forth movement of an object, like a guitar string or drum.
Sound travels as vibrations through solids, liquids and gases such as air.
Sound travels fastest through solids.
Sound travels slower through liquids than solids.
Sound travels slowest through gases such as air.
Sound cannot travel through a vacuum or empty outer space because there are no particles (no medium).
Astronauts on the Moon cannot hear each other directly because there is no air; they use radios.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is and depends on frequency of vibration.
Faster vibrations (higher frequency) produce a higher pitch.
Slower vibrations (lower frequency) produce a lower pitch.
Shorter, thinner or tighter strings vibrate faster and make higher pitched sounds.
Longer, thicker or looser strings vibrate slower and make lower pitched sounds.
Loudness (volume) of a sound depends on the amplitude of vibration and is measured in decibels.
Hitting a drum harder makes larger vibrations and a louder sound.
An echo is a sound wave that reflects (bounces) off a hard surface such as a wall or cliff.
Bats and dolphins use echolocation, reflected sound, to find food and navigate.
Sonar uses reflected sound waves to measure ocean depth and find objects underwater.
Soft materials such as curtains, carpet and foam absorb sound and reduce echoes and noise.
The ear detects sound vibrations; the eardrum vibrates.
Light travels in straight lines.
Light travels faster than sound; we see lightning before we hear thunder.
Light can travel through a vacuum and through outer space; it travels at about 300,000 kilometers per second.
Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.
Reflection is light bouncing off a surface; mirrors reflect light.
We see objects because light reflects off them into our eyes.
The Moon shines because it reflects light from the Sun.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium to another, like a pencil looking bent in a glass of water.
Lenses refract (bend) light to focus it; convex lenses in magnifying glasses make objects look larger.
Eyeglasses, cameras, microscopes and telescopes use lenses.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the rainbow (visible spectrum) by refraction.
A rainbow forms when sunlight is refracted and reflected by raindrops.
White light is made of all the colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet.
An object looks a certain color because it reflects that color of light and absorbs the others; a red apple reflects red light.
A black object absorbs all colors of light; a white object reflects all colors.
A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light.
Shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the Sun is low, and shortest at noon.
Shadows change in length and direction during the day as Earth rotates.
Absorption: dark surfaces absorb light and turn it into heat.
The electromagnetic spectrum includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, x-rays and gamma rays.
Radio waves have the longest wavelength and lowest frequency.
Gamma rays have the shortest wavelength, highest frequency and most energy.
Ultraviolet (UV) light from the Sun causes sunburn; sunscreen blocks UV rays.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat.
X-rays pass through soft tissue and are used to see bones.
Microwaves are used to cook food and for communication.
Visible light is the only part of the spectrum human eyes can see.
Electromagnetic waves do not need a medium and can travel through space.
Lasers produce a narrow, focused beam of light of one color.
Seismic waves from earthquakes travel through Earth.
Ocean waves are caused mostly by wind blowing across the water surface.
The Doppler effect: the pitch of a siren sounds higher as it approaches and lower as it moves away.
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere by evaporation, condensation, precipitation and runoff.
The Sun's energy drives the water cycle by heating and evaporating water from oceans, lakes and rivers.
In the water cycle water evaporates from oceans, condenses into clouds and falls back as precipitation.
Transpiration is the release of water vapor from plant leaves into the air.
Clouds form when water vapor in rising air cools and condenses into tiny water droplets or ice crystals around dust particles.
Precipitation is water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
Snow forms when water vapor freezes into ice crystals in cold clouds.
Hail forms when raindrops are carried up and down in thunderstorm clouds and freeze in layers.
Sleet is rain that freezes into ice pellets as it falls through cold air.
Runoff is precipitation that flows over the land surface into streams, rivers, lakes and oceans.
Infiltration is water soaking into the ground to become groundwater.
Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a certain place and time: temperature, precipitation, wind, humidity, air pressure and cloud cover.
Climate is the average weather pattern of a region over many years.
Meteorologists study and forecast the weather.
A thermometer measures air temperature.
A barometer measures air pressure.
An anemometer measures wind speed.
A wind vane (weather vane) shows wind direction.
A rain gauge measures the amount of rainfall (precipitation).
A hygrometer (psychrometer) measures humidity.
Weather satellites, radar and weather balloons collect data to forecast weather.
Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air.
Relative humidity of 100 percent means the air is saturated; dew and fog form.
Fog is a cloud at ground level formed by condensation.
Cumulus clouds are puffy white clouds that mean fair weather; they can grow into cumulonimbus thunderstorm clouds.
Cumulonimbus clouds are tall dark clouds that bring thunderstorms, lightning, heavy rain and hail.
Stratus clouds are flat gray layers that cover the sky and bring drizzle.
Cirrus clouds are thin, wispy, feathery clouds high in the sky made of ice crystals.
Air pressure is the weight of air pushing on Earth's surface; air pressure decreases as altitude increases.
High air pressure usually brings clear, dry, fair weather.
Low air pressure usually brings clouds, rain and stormy weather.
A falling barometer means low pressure and stormy weather is coming.
Wind is moving air caused by differences in air pressure, from high pressure to low pressure, due to uneven heating of Earth by the Sun.
A sea breeze blows from the cool ocean toward warmer land during the day.
A land breeze blows from cooler land toward the warmer sea at night.
Land heats up and cools down faster than water.
Large bodies of water keep coastal climates mild, with cooler summers and warmer winters.
An air mass is a large body of air with similar temperature and humidity.
A front is the boundary between two air masses.
A cold front forms when cold air pushes under warm air; it brings thunderstorms and then cooler temperatures.
A warm front forms when warm air moves over cold air; it brings steady light rain and warmer temperatures.
Thunderstorms produce lightning, thunder, heavy rain and strong winds.
Thunder is the sound of air expanding quickly when heated by lightning.
Tornadoes are violent, rotating funnel clouds with very fast winds that form during severe thunderstorms over land.
Hurricanes (typhoons, tropical cyclones) are large storms with strong winds and heavy rain that form over warm tropical ocean water and weaken over land.
Hurricanes bring storm surges and flooding to coastal areas.
Blizzards are severe snowstorms with strong winds and low temperatures.
Droughts are long periods with little or no rain.
Floods occur after heavy rain or snowmelt when rivers overflow.
The atmosphere is the layer of gases surrounding Earth, held by gravity.
The atmosphere protects Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation and meteors and keeps Earth warm.
The troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere where weather occurs.
The stratosphere contains the ozone layer, which absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun.
CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) damage the ozone layer.
The mesosphere is where meteors burn up; the thermosphere is the hottest layer.
The greenhouse effect: gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat (infrared radiation) in the atmosphere and warm Earth.
Burning fossil fuels increases carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, increasing the greenhouse effect and global warming.
Global warming (climate change) is the increase in Earth's average temperature, causing melting glaciers, melting polar ice caps and rising sea levels.
Deforestation increases carbon dioxide because fewer trees absorb carbon dioxide.
Climate zones: tropical climates near the equator are hot; polar climates near the poles are cold.
Places near the equator receive the most direct sunlight and are warmest.
Higher elevation (altitude) on mountains is colder.
The side of a mountain facing the wind gets more rain; the other side is dry (rain shadow).
Ice ages were long periods when glaciers covered large parts of Earth.

During a thunderstorm, stay indoors and away from tall objects, water and metal.
During a tornado warning, go to a basement or interior room without windows.
Smog is air pollution from vehicle exhaust and factory smoke reacting in sunlight.
