Scientists ask questions about the natural world that can be answered by investigation.
A testable question can be answered by collecting data in an experiment.
Before starting an experiment, a scientist should research the topic and state a hypothesis.
The first step in a scientific investigation is usually to ask a question or identify a problem.
After collecting data, scientists analyze it to look for patterns and draw conclusions.
Scientists communicate results so other scientists can repeat the investigation and check the results.
A good experiment tests only one variable at a time.
If two variables are changed at once, it is impossible to know which one caused the result.
The control in an experiment shows what happens when the independent variable is not changed.
In an experiment testing fertilizer on plants, the plants without fertilizer are the control group.
Variables that should be kept the same are the amount of water, amount of light, type of soil, type of plant and temperature.
Measurements should be repeated and averaged to reduce error.
The mean, or average, is the sum of the values divided by the number of values.
The range is the difference between the highest and lowest values.
Data that do not fit the pattern are called outliers and may be caused by errors.
Results are more reliable when an experiment has a large sample size and many trials.
Scientists use metric units so that measurements can be easily shared and compared.
A hypothesis can be supported or not supported by data; it is never proven absolutely.
A scientific theory explains a large set of observations and can change if new evidence is found.
Scientists use models, such as diagrams, globes, maps, computer simulations and physical models, to study things that are too large, too small or too dangerous to study directly.

A model of the solar system shows the Sun at the center with planets orbiting it.
Scientists use computers to make models that predict weather, climate and the paths of hurricanes.
Observations are made with the senses: sight, hearing, touch, smell and taste.
Scientific instruments extend the senses: microscopes, telescopes, thermometers and scales.
Classifying is grouping objects by their similar properties.
Comparing means finding how things are alike and different.
A bias can affect results; scientists use controls and repeat trials to reduce bias.
Scientists must report their data honestly, even when the results do not support their hypothesis.
Peer review is when other scientists evaluate research before it is published.
Science and technology affect each other: new tools lead to new discoveries, and discoveries lead to new technology.
Scientists work in teams and share ideas, so scientific knowledge grows over time.

Rocks are made of one or more minerals.
A mineral is a naturally occurring inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and a crystal structure.
Minerals are identified by properties such as hardness, luster, streak, color, cleavage, fracture and density.
The streak of a mineral is the color of its powder when it is rubbed on a streak plate of unglazed porcelain.
Luster describes how a mineral reflects light, such as metallic, glassy, pearly or dull.
Hardness is a mineral's resistance to being scratched, measured on the Mohs hardness scale from 1 to 10.
Talc is the softest mineral with a hardness of 1 on the Mohs scale.
Diamond is the hardest mineral with a hardness of 10 on the Mohs scale.
A harder mineral can scratch a softer mineral; a scratch test compares the hardness of minerals.
Quartz is a common hard mineral found in granite and sand; quartz has a hardness of 7.
Feldspar, quartz, mica, calcite, gypsum, halite, talc, pyrite, magnetite, hematite and graphite are minerals.
Calcite fizzes and bubbles when a drop of weak acid such as vinegar or hydrochloric acid is placed on it.
Limestone and marble contain calcite, so they react with acid and are dissolved by acid rain.
Magnetite is a magnetic mineral that attracts a magnet.
Pyrite is called fool's gold because it is shiny and gold colored.
Halite is rock salt; halite crystals are cube shaped and taste salty.
Graphite is a soft mineral made of carbon used in pencils; diamond is also made of carbon.
Gems and crystals form when minerals cool slowly or when water evaporates from a mineral solution.
Cleavage is when a mineral breaks along smooth flat surfaces; fracture is when it breaks unevenly.
The three main types of rock are igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic.
Igneous rock forms when melted rock called magma or lava cools and hardens.
Magma is molten rock below the surface of Earth; lava is molten rock that reaches the surface.
Intrusive igneous rock forms when magma cools slowly underground, forming large crystals, such as granite.
Extrusive igneous rock forms when lava cools quickly at the surface, forming small crystals or glass, such as basalt and obsidian.
Slow cooling forms large crystals; fast cooling forms small crystals.
Obsidian is a glassy volcanic igneous rock that forms when lava cools very quickly.
Pumice is a light volcanic igneous rock full of holes from gas bubbles, and it can float on water.
Granite is a coarse grained intrusive igneous rock containing quartz, feldspar and mica.
Basalt is a dark fine grained extrusive igneous rock that forms the ocean floor and many lava flows.
Sedimentary rock forms when layers of sediment are deposited, compacted and cemented together over time.
Sediments are bits of rock, sand, silt, clay, shells and remains of organisms carried and deposited by water, wind or ice.
Sedimentary rocks form in layers, often at the bottom of oceans, lakes and rivers.
Sandstone is a sedimentary rock made of cemented sand grains.
Shale is a sedimentary rock made of compacted clay and mud.
Conglomerate is a sedimentary rock made of rounded pebbles and gravel cemented together.
Limestone is a sedimentary rock made of calcite, often from the shells and skeletons of ocean organisms.
Coal is a sedimentary rock formed from the remains of swamp plants that were buried and compressed over millions of years.
Fossils are most often found in sedimentary rock.
Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by great heat and pressure deep underground.
Marble is a metamorphic rock formed from limestone.
Slate is a metamorphic rock formed from shale.
Quartzite is a metamorphic rock formed from sandstone.
Gneiss is a banded metamorphic rock that can form from granite.
Schist is a metamorphic rock with flaky layers of mica.
The rock cycle describes how rocks change from one type into another over long periods of time.
In the rock cycle, any type of rock can be weathered, melted, or changed by heat and pressure into another type.
Melting and cooling forms igneous rock; weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation form sedimentary rock; heat and pressure form metamorphic rock.
Heat from inside Earth and energy from the Sun drive the rock cycle.
Weathering is the breaking down of rocks into smaller pieces called sediment.
Mechanical weathering, or physical weathering, breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of.
Ice wedging, or frost wedging, happens when water seeps into cracks in rock, freezes and expands, and breaks the rock apart.
Water expands when it freezes, so freezing and thawing cracks rocks, roads and pavement.
Repeated freezing and thawing of water in cracks causes potholes in roads and cracks in sidewalks.
Plant roots growing into cracks in rocks can break the rocks apart, which is mechanical weathering.
Burrowing animals and growing tree roots cause weathering of rock.
Abrasion is weathering when rocks are worn down by rubbing against sand, other rocks, wind, water or ice.
Rocks in a river or stream become smooth and rounded as they tumble and rub against each other.
Wind blowing sand against rocks wears them down and shapes them in the desert.
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock into new substances.
Acid rain causes chemical weathering by dissolving limestone and marble statues and buildings.
Oxidation is chemical weathering when iron in rocks reacts with oxygen and water to form rust.
Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water; rusting is a chemical change.
Carbonic acid in rainwater and groundwater dissolves limestone and forms caves and caverns.
Lichens and mosses produce acids that slowly break down rock, beginning soil formation.
Chemical weathering is fastest in warm, wet climates; mechanical weathering by ice is common in cold climates.
Weathering happens faster when more surface area of the rock is exposed.
Erosion is the movement of weathered rock and soil from one place to another by water, wind, ice or gravity.
Running water is the main cause of erosion on Earth.
Rivers and streams erode their banks and beds and carry sediment downstream.
A flood washes over a riverbank and the water carries soil particles downstream, which is erosion.
Over millions of years the Colorado River eroded rock and formed the Grand Canyon.
Rivers carve V shaped valleys and canyons through erosion.
Glaciers carve U shaped valleys and carry boulders, rocks and sediment as they move.
Glaciers are large slow moving masses of ice that erode and deposit rock and soil.
Glaciers leave behind deposits called moraines and large boulders called erratics.
Ocean waves erode beaches, shorelines and cliffs and move sand along the coast.
Wind erosion carries sand and dust and forms sand dunes in deserts and on beaches.
Gravity causes erosion through landslides, mudslides, rockfalls and slumping on steep slopes.
A landslide is the rapid movement of rock and soil down a slope caused by gravity, often after heavy rain.
Plants and their roots hold soil in place and help prevent erosion.
Planting trees, grass and cover crops reduces soil erosion.
Terracing, contour plowing, windbreaks and planting vegetation reduce soil erosion on farms.
Removing trees and plants, called deforestation, increases soil erosion.
Steep slopes and fast moving water cause more erosion than gentle slopes and slow water.
Deposition is when sediment carried by water, wind or ice is dropped in a new place.
Rivers deposit sediment where they slow down, such as at the mouth of a river where it enters the ocean, forming a delta.
A delta is a landform made of sediment deposited where a river flows into a lake or ocean.
Sand dunes form by deposition of sand carried by the wind.
Floodplains form from sediment deposited when rivers flood.
Larger heavier sediment like gravel settles first; smaller particles like clay settle last and are carried farther.
Weathering breaks rock down, erosion moves the pieces, and deposition drops them in a new location.
Soil is a mixture of weathered rock, minerals, organic matter called humus, water and air.
Humus is the dark organic material in soil formed from decomposed plants and animals.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and earthworms break down dead organisms and add nutrients to soil.
Earthworms loosen and mix soil, make tunnels that let air and water in, and add nutrients to the soil.
Soil forms very slowly from the weathering of rock, taking hundreds or thousands of years.
Soil has layers called horizons: topsoil, subsoil and bedrock.
Topsoil is the top layer of soil that contains the most humus and nutrients for plant growth.
Sand particles are the largest soil particles, silt particles are medium, and clay particles are the smallest.
Sandy soil has large particles and drains water quickly; clay soil has tiny particles and holds water.
Loam is a soil that is a mixture of sand, silt, clay and humus and is best for growing plants.
Soil provides plants with water, nutrients, minerals and support for their roots.
A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago.
Fossils form when organisms are buried quickly by sediment, which hardens into rock over time.
Most fossils form from the hard parts of organisms such as bones, teeth and shells.
Trace fossils such as footprints, tracks, burrows and coprolites show the activity of ancient organisms.
A mold fossil is an impression left in rock; a cast fossil forms when minerals fill a mold.
Petrified wood forms when minerals replace the wood of a buried tree.
Organisms can be preserved whole in amber, ice, or tar.
Insects trapped in tree sap that hardened into amber are preserved as fossils.
Fossils provide evidence of organisms that lived long ago and how life on Earth has changed over time.
Fossils of ocean animals such as fish and shells found on land or on a mountain show the area was once covered by ocean water.
Fossils of tropical plants found in a cold place show the climate there was once warm.
Fossils of ferns and palm trees in Antarctica show that the climate was once warmer.
Fossils of the same species found on different continents are evidence of continental drift.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a wide area and are used to date rock layers.
The law of superposition states that in undisturbed rock layers, the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest layers are at the top.
Deeper rock layers are usually older than the rock layers above them.
Relative dating determines whether a rock or fossil is older or younger than another.
Absolute dating uses radioactive decay, or radiometric dating, to find the age of rocks in years.
Radioactive isotopes decay at a constant rate measured by their half life.
Carbon 14 dating is used to find the age of once living things up to about 50,000 years old.

The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into eons, eras, periods and epochs based on fossils and rock layers.
Dinosaurs lived during the Mesozoic Era and became extinct about 65 million years ago.
An asteroid impact is thought to have caused the extinction of the dinosaurs.
The first living things on Earth were single celled organisms like bacteria in the ocean.
Comparing fossils to living organisms shows how species have changed over time.
Scientists who study fossils are called paleontologists.
Earth has layers: the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.
The crust is the thin rocky outermost layer of Earth; it is thickest under continents and thinnest under the oceans.
The mantle is the thickest layer of Earth, made of hot rock that flows very slowly.
The outer core is liquid iron and nickel; the inner core is solid iron and nickel.
The inner core is the hottest and densest layer of Earth, and pressure increases with depth.
The lithosphere is made of the crust and upper mantle and is broken into tectonic plates.
Tectonic plates are large pieces of Earth's lithosphere that move slowly, a few centimeters per year, on the asthenosphere.
Convection currents in the mantle move the tectonic plates.
Plate tectonics theory explains how plate movements cause earthquakes, volcanoes, mountains and ocean trenches.
Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift, the idea that the continents were once joined in a supercontinent called Pangaea.
Evidence for continental drift includes matching coastlines, matching fossils and rock formations on different continents.
The shapes of South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces.
At a divergent boundary, plates move apart and new crust forms, such as at a mid ocean ridge.
Sea floor spreading at mid ocean ridges creates new ocean crust as magma rises.
At a convergent boundary, plates push together, forming mountains, volcanoes and deep ocean trenches.
Subduction happens when a denser oceanic plate sinks beneath another plate at a convergent boundary.
When two continental plates collide, the crust folds and pushes up into tall mountains like the Himalayas.
The Himalaya Mountains formed when the Indian plate collided with the Eurasian plate.
At a transform boundary, plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes, as along the San Andreas Fault in California.
Mountains form by folding and faulting of rock when tectonic plates collide, and by volcanoes.
The Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean has many volcanoes and earthquakes along plate boundaries.
Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along the boundaries of tectonic plates.
An earthquake is the sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of energy when rocks break and move along a fault.
A fault is a crack in Earth's crust along which rocks move.
The focus is the point underground where an earthquake starts; the epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it.
Earthquakes produce seismic waves that travel through Earth.
A seismograph, or seismometer, is an instrument that measures and records earthquake waves.
The Richter scale and moment magnitude scale measure the strength or magnitude of an earthquake.
P waves are the fastest seismic waves and travel through solids and liquids; S waves are slower and travel only through solids.
S waves cannot pass through the liquid outer core, which is evidence that the outer core is liquid.
Earthquakes under the ocean can cause tsunamis, which are giant ocean waves.
Earthquakes can cause landslides, building collapse, broken roads and tsunamis.
Buildings designed to sway and flexible structures are safer during earthquakes.
A volcano is an opening in Earth's crust through which magma, lava, ash and gases erupt.
Volcanoes form at plate boundaries and over hot spots in the mantle.
The Hawaiian Islands formed from volcanoes over a hot spot in the middle of the Pacific plate.
Volcanic eruptions release lava, ash, rock and gases such as water vapor, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.
Volcanic ash in the atmosphere can block sunlight and cool Earth's climate for a time.
Lava flows can build new land and islands, and volcanic soil is very fertile.
Shield volcanoes have gentle slopes from runny lava; composite or strato volcanoes are steep and explosive; cinder cones are small and steep.
Geysers and hot springs are heated by magma underground.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth.
Landforms are natural features of Earth's surface such as mountains, valleys, plains, plateaus, canyons, hills and islands.
Constructive forces build up land, such as volcanoes, deposition and crust deformation.
Destructive forces wear down land, such as weathering, erosion, earthquakes and landslides.
A plateau is a large flat area of land that is raised high above the surrounding land.
A plain is a large flat area of land at low elevation.
A mesa is a flat topped hill with steep sides formed by erosion.
Sinkholes form when underground limestone is dissolved by groundwater and the ground collapses.
Stalactites hang from cave ceilings and stalagmites rise from cave floors; both form from minerals deposited by dripping water.
A topographic map uses contour lines to show elevation and the shape of the land.
Contour lines close together show a steep slope; contour lines far apart show a gentle slope.
Older mountains like the Appalachians are lower and more rounded because they have been eroded longer than young mountains like the Rockies.
A barrier island is a long narrow island of sand parallel to the coast.
Most of Earth's surface, about 71 percent, is covered by water.
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere.
The Sun provides the energy that drives the water cycle.
The main processes of the water cycle are evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection or runoff.
Evaporation is the change of liquid water into water vapor, a gas, when it is heated by the Sun.
Most evaporation in the water cycle happens from the surface of the oceans.
Water evaporates faster when it is warmer, when it is windy, and when it has more surface area exposed to the air.
Condensation is the change of water vapor, a gas, into liquid water droplets when it cools.
Clouds and fog form when water vapor in the air cools and condenses into tiny water droplets or ice crystals.
Dew forms when water vapor condenses on cool surfaces such as grass in the early morning.
Water droplets form on the outside of a cold glass or can because water vapor in the air condenses on the cold surface.
Frost forms when water vapor turns directly into ice on a cold surface below freezing.
Precipitation is water that falls from clouds to Earth's surface as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
Precipitation happens when water droplets or ice crystals in clouds grow too heavy to stay in the air.
Snow forms when water vapor freezes into ice crystals in cold clouds.
Sleet is rain that freezes into ice pellets as it falls through cold air.
Hail forms when strong updrafts in thunderstorm clouds carry raindrops up into freezing air again and again, building layers of ice.
Runoff is water that flows over the land surface into streams, rivers, lakes and oceans.
Infiltration is when water soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater.
Groundwater is water stored underground in the soil and in spaces in rock called aquifers.
An aquifer is an underground layer of rock or sediment that holds groundwater; wells pump water from aquifers.
Permeable materials like sand and gravel let water pass through easily; clay is less permeable.
Transpiration is the release of water vapor from the leaves of plants into the air.
Sublimation is when a solid changes directly into a gas, such as dry ice or snow turning to vapor.
Plants return water to the air by transpiration, and animals return water by breathing, sweating and wastes.
A watershed is an area of land that drains into a particular river, lake or ocean.
About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans.
Only about 3 percent of Earth's water is fresh water, and most fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.
Fresh water is found in lakes, rivers, streams, ponds, groundwater, glaciers and ice caps.
Ocean water is salty because rivers carry dissolved minerals and salts from rocks into the ocean.
Salt water is denser than fresh water, and salt water freezes at a lower temperature than fresh water.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
Ocean currents are large streams of moving water in the ocean caused by wind, temperature and salinity differences.
Surface ocean currents are driven mainly by wind.
Deep ocean currents are caused by differences in water density, temperature and salt content.
The Gulf Stream is a warm ocean current that carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico north along the east coast of North America toward Europe.
Ocean currents move heat around the planet and affect the climate of coastal areas.
Oceans absorb and store heat, so coastal areas have milder temperatures than inland areas.
Water heats up and cools down more slowly than land because water has a high specific heat.
Ocean water near the poles is cold; ocean water near the equator is warm.
Ocean tides are the regular daily rise and fall of the sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon.
Most coastal areas have two high tides and two low tides each day.
Spring tides are very high tides that occur when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up during a full moon or new moon.
Neap tides are small tides that occur when the Sun and Moon are at right angles during a quarter moon.
Ocean waves are mostly caused by wind blowing over the surface of the water.
Tsunamis are huge ocean waves caused by underwater earthquakes, landslides or volcanic eruptions.
The ocean floor has features such as the continental shelf, continental slope, abyssal plain, mid ocean ridges and deep trenches.
The Mariana Trench in the Pacific Ocean is the deepest place in the ocean.
The Pacific Ocean is the largest and deepest ocean on Earth.
Sunlight penetrates only the upper layer of the ocean, so most ocean producers like phytoplankton live near the surface.
Pressure in the ocean increases with depth, and water temperature decreases with depth.
Sonar uses sound waves to measure the depth of the ocean and map the ocean floor.
Estuaries are places where fresh river water mixes with salt ocean water and are rich habitats for many organisms.
Coral reefs form in warm shallow clear ocean water and are built from the skeletons of tiny coral animals.
The atmosphere is the layer of gases that surrounds Earth.
Air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen, about 78 percent, and oxygen, about 21 percent, with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Nitrogen is the most abundant gas in Earth's atmosphere.
The layers of the atmosphere from lowest to highest are the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.
Almost all weather occurs in the troposphere, the lowest layer of the atmosphere.
The ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun and protects living things.
Chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs, damaged the ozone layer.
Air pressure is the weight of the air pressing down on Earth's surface.
Air pressure decreases as altitude or elevation increases; there is less air pressure on top of a mountain.
Air temperature in the troposphere decreases as altitude increases, so mountain tops are colder.
Warm air is less dense than cold air, so warm air rises and cold air sinks.
The atmosphere traps heat and keeps Earth warm enough for life, and it protects Earth from meteors and radiation.
The greenhouse effect is the warming of Earth when gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat in the atmosphere.
Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a certain place and time, including temperature, precipitation, wind, humidity, cloud cover and air pressure.
Weather changes from day to day; climate is the average weather of a place over many years.
The uneven heating of Earth's surface by the Sun causes winds and drives weather.
Wind is the movement of air from an area of high pressure to an area of low pressure.
Sea breezes blow from the ocean toward land during the day because land heats faster than water.
Land breezes blow from land toward the ocean at night because land cools faster than water.
Global winds such as the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies are caused by uneven heating and Earth's rotation.
The Coriolis effect, caused by Earth's rotation, curves the paths of winds and ocean currents.
The jet stream is a fast band of wind high in the atmosphere that moves weather systems from west to east across the United States.
In the United States, weather systems usually move from west to east.
Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air.
Relative humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air compared to the most it can hold at that temperature.
Warm air can hold more water vapor than cold air.
The dew point is the temperature at which water vapor in the air condenses into liquid water.
An air mass is a large body of air with similar temperature and humidity throughout.
Air masses that form over oceans are humid; air masses that form over land are dry.
Air masses that form near the equator are warm; air masses that form near the poles are cold.
A front is the boundary between two air masses with different temperatures and humidity.
A cold front forms when cold air pushes under warm air, often causing thunderstorms, heavy rain and a drop in temperature.
A warm front forms when warm air moves over cold air, often bringing steady light rain and warmer temperatures.
A stationary front is a boundary between air masses that are not moving, which can bring days of rain.
High pressure systems usually bring clear skies and fair, dry weather.
Low pressure systems usually bring clouds, precipitation and stormy weather.
A falling barometer reading means air pressure is dropping and stormy or rainy weather is likely.
A rising barometer reading means fair weather is likely.
Cirrus clouds are thin wispy high clouds made of ice crystals that often indicate fair weather or a change in weather.
Cumulus clouds are puffy white clouds with flat bottoms that usually mean fair weather.
Stratus clouds are flat gray layered clouds that cover the sky and can bring drizzle.
Cumulonimbus clouds are tall dark clouds that produce thunderstorms, lightning, heavy rain and hail.
Nimbus or nimbostratus clouds are dark rain clouds.
Fog is a cloud that forms near the ground.
A thunderstorm is a storm with lightning, thunder, heavy rain and strong winds formed in cumulonimbus clouds.
Lightning is a giant electric spark caused by the buildup of static electric charges in clouds.
Thunder is the sound caused by lightning rapidly heating and expanding the air.
We see lightning before we hear thunder because light travels much faster than sound.
A tornado is a rapidly spinning column of air that extends from a thunderstorm cloud to the ground.
Tornadoes have the fastest winds of any storm and are most common in the central United States in spring.
A hurricane is a large tropical storm with very strong winds, heavy rain and a calm eye at the center that forms over warm ocean water.
Hurricanes get their energy from warm ocean water and weaken when they move over land or cold water.
Hurricanes can cause storm surges, flooding, and wind damage along coasts.
A blizzard is a winter storm with heavy snow, strong winds and very cold temperatures.
A drought is a long period of time with little or no rain.
Floods occur when heavy rain or melting snow causes rivers to overflow.
Meteorologists are scientists who study and forecast the weather.
Weather forecasters use weather maps, satellites, radar, weather balloons and data from weather stations to predict the weather.
A thermometer measures air temperature.
A barometer measures air pressure.
An anemometer measures wind speed.
A wind vane, or weather vane, shows wind direction.
A rain gauge measures the amount of rainfall or precipitation.
A hygrometer or psychrometer measures humidity.
Weather satellites take pictures of clouds and storms from space.
Doppler radar detects precipitation and the movement of storms.
A weather map shows fronts, high and low pressure areas, temperatures and precipitation.
Climate is the average weather conditions of an area over a long period of time, such as 30 years.
Climate is affected by latitude, elevation, nearness to large bodies of water, ocean currents, mountains and prevailing winds.
Places near the equator have warm climates because they receive the most direct sunlight all year.
Places near the poles have cold climates because sunlight strikes at a low angle and is spread out.
Places at high elevations, like mountains, have colder climates than places at low elevations.
Coastal areas have milder climates than inland areas because large bodies of water heat and cool slowly.
The side of a mountain facing the ocean wind gets more rain; the other side, in the rain shadow, is dry.
The major climate zones are tropical, temperate and polar.
Tropical rain forests are hot and wet all year.
Deserts receive very little rain, less than 25 centimeters per year, and can be hot or cold.
Climate change is a long term change in Earth's climate, including global warming.
Global warming is the increase in Earth's average temperature caused by more greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
Burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gasoline releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming.
Cutting down forests increases carbon dioxide in the atmosphere because fewer trees absorb carbon dioxide.
Global warming causes glaciers and polar ice caps to melt and sea levels to rise.
Rising sea levels can flood coastal areas.
Ice ages were long periods when glaciers covered much of Earth's surface.
Scientists study ice cores, tree rings and fossils to learn about Earth's past climate.
Tree rings can show the age of a tree and past climate; wide rings show good growing years with lots of rain.
El Nino is a periodic warming of the Pacific Ocean that changes weather patterns around the world.
Large volcanic eruptions can lower global temperatures by putting ash and gases into the atmosphere that block sunlight.

A small amount of liquid such as medicine is measured in milliliters; a large amount such as a bottle of soda is measured in liters.

A hot summer day is about 30 to 35 degrees Celsius; water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius.

A compass is used to find direction such as north.

A wind vane shows which direction the wind is blowing from.
A spring scale measures the force needed to pull an object, in newtons.

A hot plate or Bunsen burner is used to heat substances.

All living things need energy, grow, develop, reproduce, respond to their environment, and get rid of wastes.
Living things need food, water, air and a place to live, called a habitat.
Basic needs of animals are food, water, oxygen, shelter and space.
Basic needs of plants are sunlight, water, air with carbon dioxide, nutrients from soil and space to grow.
Nonliving things such as rocks, water, air and sunlight do not grow or reproduce.
Biotic factors are the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are the nonliving parts of an ecosystem, such as sunlight, water, air, temperature, rocks and soil.
A stimulus is something in the environment that causes a living thing to respond, and the reaction is a response.
Homeostasis is the ability of an organism to keep stable internal conditions, such as body temperature, water balance and blood sugar.
Sweating and shivering help the body keep a stable temperature, an example of homeostasis.
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in all living things.
Cell theory states that all living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from existing cells.
Robert Hooke first observed and named cells when he looked at cork through a microscope.
Anton van Leeuwenhoek was the first to observe living microorganisms with a simple microscope.
Most cells are too small to be seen without a microscope.
Unicellular organisms, or single celled organisms, such as bacteria, amoebas and paramecia, are made of only one cell.
Multicellular organisms such as plants, animals and humans are made of many cells.
In multicellular organisms, cells are specialized to do different jobs.
Levels of organization in multicellular organisms: cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, and organ systems form an organism.
A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together to perform a function, such as muscle tissue or nerve tissue.
An organ is a group of different tissues that work together to perform a specific job, such as the heart, stomach, lungs or a leaf.
An organ system is a group of organs that work together, such as the digestive system.
The nucleus is the control center of the cell; it contains DNA and controls the cell's activities.
The nucleus contains chromosomes made of DNA, which carry the genetic information or hereditary instructions of the cell.
The cell membrane is a thin flexible layer around the cell that controls what substances enter and leave the cell.
The cell membrane is selectively permeable, letting some substances pass through while keeping others out.
Cytoplasm is the jelly like fluid inside the cell where organelles are found and many chemical reactions occur.
Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell; they release energy from food, such as glucose, through cellular respiration.
Cells that need a lot of energy, such as muscle cells, have many mitochondria.
Chloroplasts are organelles in plant cells and algae that capture sunlight energy to make food by photosynthesis.
Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that absorbs light energy.
The cell wall is a rigid layer outside the cell membrane of plant cells that supports and protects the cell and gives it shape.
Plant cell walls are made of cellulose.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole, which animal cells lack.
Both plant and animal cells have a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria and ribosomes.
Vacuoles store water, food and wastes; plant cells have one large vacuole that helps keep the plant firm.
When a plant does not get enough water its vacuoles shrink and the plant wilts.
Ribosomes are small structures where proteins are made.
The endoplasmic reticulum transports materials within the cell; the Golgi apparatus packages and ships proteins.
Lysosomes contain enzymes that digest wastes and worn out cell parts.
Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have no nucleus; their DNA floats in the cytoplasm.
Eukaryotic cells, such as plant, animal and fungus cells, have a nucleus and membrane bound organelles.
Specialized cells have shapes that fit their functions: nerve cells are long to carry messages, red blood cells are disk shaped to carry oxygen.
Muscle cells contract to produce movement.
Root hair cells have a large surface area for absorbing water.
Sperm cells have tails called flagella to swim.
Guard cells open and close the stomata, the tiny pores in leaves that let gases in and out.
Diffusion is the movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.
Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a cell membrane.
Oxygen enters cells and carbon dioxide leaves cells by diffusion.
Active transport uses energy to move materials across the cell membrane against a concentration gradient.
Cellular respiration is the process in which cells use oxygen to break down glucose and release energy, producing carbon dioxide and water.
The equation for cellular respiration is glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy.
Both plant and animal cells carry out cellular respiration in their mitochondria.
Fermentation releases energy from sugar without oxygen; yeast fermentation produces carbon dioxide and alcohol, which makes bread rise.
Muscle cells can make lactic acid by fermentation when they lack oxygen during hard exercise.
Cell division is how cells reproduce; organisms grow by cell division.
Mitosis is cell division that produces two identical daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell.
Mitosis is used for growth, repair of injured tissue and replacing old cells, and for asexual reproduction.
Meiosis is cell division that produces sex cells, or gametes, with half the number of chromosomes.
Sex cells are egg cells and sperm cells, which have half the chromosomes of body cells.
Human body cells have 46 chromosomes, or 23 pairs; human sex cells have 23 chromosomes.
Cancer is a disease in which cells divide uncontrollably.
Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living things, such as digestion.
Classification is grouping living things based on their similar characteristics and structures.
Scientists classify organisms to organize and study the great diversity of living things.
Carl Linnaeus developed the system of classification and binomial nomenclature, a two part scientific name of genus and species.
The levels of classification from largest to smallest are domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
A species is a group of similar organisms that can mate and produce fertile offspring.
Organisms in the same genus are more closely related than organisms in the same family or order.
The six kingdoms are animals, plants, fungi, protists, archaebacteria and eubacteria.
The three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.
Organisms are classified by their cell type, number of cells, body structures, and how they get food.
A dichotomous key is a tool that uses a series of paired choices to identify organisms.
The plant kingdom contains multicellular organisms with cell walls that make their own food by photosynthesis.
The animal kingdom contains multicellular organisms that must eat other organisms for food and can usually move.
Fungi include mushrooms, molds, yeasts and mildew; fungi absorb food from dead or living organisms.
Fungi are decomposers; they cannot make their own food because they have no chlorophyll.
Mushrooms are fungi that reproduce with spores.
Yeast is a single celled fungus used to make bread rise and in fermentation.
Mold is a fungus that grows on bread and fruit in warm, damp places.
Penicillin, an antibiotic, was discovered from a mold.
Protists are mostly single celled eukaryotic organisms such as amoebas, paramecia, euglena and algae.
Algae are plant like protists that make food by photosynthesis and live in water.
An amoeba moves using pseudopods; a paramecium moves using cilia; a euglena moves using a flagellum.
Bacteria are single celled prokaryotic organisms with no nucleus.
Bacteria are found almost everywhere: in soil, water, air, and inside other organisms.
Some bacteria cause diseases such as strep throat, food poisoning and tuberculosis.
Many bacteria are helpful: they decompose dead matter, recycle nutrients, help digest food in the intestines, and are used to make yogurt and cheese.
Nitrogen fixing bacteria in soil and in the roots of legumes like peas and beans change nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use.
Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, splitting in two.
Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not work against viruses.
Viruses are tiny particles made of genetic material in a protein coat; they are not cells.
Viruses can only reproduce inside the living cells of a host.
Viruses cause diseases such as the common cold, flu or influenza, chicken pox, measles, polio and AIDS.
Vaccines help the body build immunity to diseases caused by viruses and bacteria.
Microorganisms, or microbes, are living things too small to see without a microscope, such as bacteria, protists and some fungi.
A food chain shows how energy passes from one organism to another as each organism eats another.
In a food chain, arrows show the direction energy flows, from the food to the organism that eats it.
Almost all food chains begin with energy from the Sun.
The Sun is the original source of energy for almost all living things and food chains.
Producers are organisms such as plants and algae that make their own food using energy from sunlight.
Producers are the first level of every food chain; green plants, trees, grass and phytoplankton are producers.
Consumers are organisms that get energy by eating other organisms.
Herbivores are consumers that eat only plants, such as rabbits, deer, cows, horses, grasshoppers and caterpillars.
Carnivores are consumers that eat only other animals, such as lions, wolves, hawks, eagles, sharks and snakes.
Omnivores are consumers that eat both plants and animals, such as humans, bears, raccoons, pigs and many birds.
Primary consumers are herbivores that eat producers; secondary consumers eat primary consumers; tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers.
A predator is an animal that hunts and eats other animals; the animal that is eaten is the prey.
Owls are predators of mice; hawks are predators of rabbits and snakes; foxes eat rabbits.
Scavengers such as vultures, hyenas and crows eat dead animals.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and mushrooms break down dead plants and animals and wastes and return nutrients to the soil.
Decomposers recycle nutrients in an ecosystem so producers can use them again.
Without decomposers, dead organisms and wastes would pile up and nutrients would not return to the soil.
Earthworms, millipedes and some insects help decompose dead leaves and organic matter.
A food web is made of many connected food chains in an ecosystem and shows feeding relationships.
In a food web, removing one organism affects many other organisms.
If the number of predators decreases, the prey population usually increases.
If the prey population decreases, the predator population usually decreases because there is less food.
If a producer is removed, the herbivores that eat it decrease, and then the carnivores that eat the herbivores decrease.
An energy pyramid shows that energy decreases at each level of a food chain; only about 10 percent of energy passes to the next level.
Most energy at each level of a food chain is used for life processes or lost as heat.
There are more producers than consumers in an ecosystem because energy is lost at each level.
Top predators at the top of the energy pyramid are the fewest in number.
Toxic chemicals like pesticides become more concentrated in organisms higher in the food chain, called biomagnification.
In the ocean, phytoplankton are producers eaten by zooplankton, which are eaten by small fish, which are eaten by larger fish.
An ecosystem is all the living organisms and nonliving things in an area interacting with each other.
A population is all the members of one species living in the same area.
A community is all the populations of different species living and interacting in an area.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives that provides the food, water, shelter and space it needs.
A niche is the role an organism plays in its ecosystem, including what it eats and where it lives.
The carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support with its food, water and space.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space, shelter, predators and disease limit the size of a population.
A population grows when birth rates are greater than death rates and resources are plentiful.
Overpopulation of deer can happen when their predators such as wolves are removed, and the deer may run out of food.
Biodiversity is the variety of different species living in an ecosystem.
Ecosystems with high biodiversity are more stable and better able to recover from changes.
A biome is a large region with a similar climate, plants and animals, such as desert, tundra, grassland, rain forest and deciduous forest.
Tropical rain forests are hot, wet biomes with the greatest biodiversity of any land biome.
Deserts are dry biomes with little rainfall where plants like cacti and animals like lizards and snakes are adapted to dry conditions.
The tundra is a cold, treeless biome with permafrost, frozen soil, where mosses, lichens and small shrubs grow.
Grasslands, prairies and savannas are biomes covered mostly by grasses with few trees, home to grazing animals like bison and zebras.
Temperate deciduous forests have four seasons and trees that lose their leaves in the fall, such as oak and maple.
The taiga, or boreal forest, is a cold forest of evergreen conifer trees.
Aquatic ecosystems include freshwater ecosystems such as ponds, lakes, rivers and wetlands, and marine ecosystems such as oceans and coral reefs.
Wetlands such as marshes and swamps filter water, reduce flooding and provide habitats for many species.
Competition happens when organisms need the same limited resources, such as food, water, light, space or mates.
Two species that eat the same food in the same area compete with each other.
Symbiosis is a close long term relationship between two different species.
Mutualism is a relationship in which both organisms benefit, such as bees and flowers, or the clownfish and sea anemone.
Lichens are a mutualism between a fungus and an alga; the alga makes food and the fungus provides shelter and water.
Commensalism is a relationship in which one organism benefits and the other is not helped or harmed, such as barnacles on a whale or a bird nesting in a tree.
Parasitism is a relationship in which one organism, the parasite, benefits and the other, the host, is harmed.
Parasites include ticks, fleas, lice, tapeworms, mosquitoes and mistletoe, which feed on a host.
A tick feeding on the blood of a dog is an example of parasitism.
Ecological succession is the gradual change in the species in a community over time.
Primary succession begins on bare rock where there is no soil, such as after a lava flow or a glacier retreats; lichens and mosses are the pioneer species.
Pioneer species such as lichens and mosses are the first organisms to grow in an area and help form soil.
Secondary succession happens where soil remains after a disturbance such as a forest fire, flood or abandoned farm field.
After a forest fire, grasses and small plants grow first, followed by shrubs and then trees.
Natural events such as floods, fires, droughts, volcanic eruptions and hurricanes change ecosystems.
Forest fires clear dead material, return nutrients to the soil and allow new plants to grow.
An invasive species is a nonnative species introduced to a new area that spreads quickly and harms native species, often because it has no natural predators.
Invasive species such as zebra mussels, kudzu and purple loosestrife compete with native species for resources.
When a new species is introduced with no predators, its population can grow rapidly and outcompete native species.
Habitat destruction is the main cause of species becoming endangered or extinct.
An endangered species is a species in danger of becoming extinct because its population is very small.
A species becomes extinct when all of its members have died.
Organisms that cannot adapt to changes in their environment may die out or move to a new area.
Beavers build dams that change rivers into ponds and create wetland habitats.
Humans change ecosystems by building cities, farming, cutting forests, damming rivers and polluting.
Matter is recycled in ecosystems through the water cycle, the carbon cycle and the nitrogen cycle.
In the carbon cycle, plants take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis, and animals and plants release carbon dioxide during respiration.
Burning fossil fuels and decomposition release carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.
In the oxygen and carbon dioxide cycle, plants give off oxygen that animals breathe in, and animals give off carbon dioxide that plants use.
In the nitrogen cycle, bacteria change nitrogen gas from the air into compounds plants can use to make proteins.
Animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.
Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to offspring.
Traits are characteristics of an organism such as eye color, hair color, height, flower color and seed shape.
Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring through genes, such as eye color, hair color, dimples, blood type and freckles.
Acquired traits are developed during an organism's life and are not inherited, such as scars, a suntan, learned skills, big muscles from exercise and dyed hair.
Riding a bike, reading, speaking a language and playing a musical instrument are learned or acquired, not inherited.
Some traits are affected by both genes and the environment, such as height and weight affected by diet.
Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes that carry the instructions for traits.
DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid, is the molecule that carries genetic information in the nucleus of cells.
DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder called a double helix.
The bases of DNA are adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine; adenine pairs with thymine and guanine pairs with cytosine.
Chromosomes are found in the nucleus and are made of DNA and protein.
Offspring receive half of their chromosomes and genes from each parent.
Each parent gives one allele, or form of a gene, for each trait to the offspring.
Alleles are different forms of the same gene.
A dominant allele hides the effect of a recessive allele; dominant alleles are written with a capital letter.
A recessive trait shows only when an organism has two recessive alleles, written with lowercase letters.
Homozygous means having two identical alleles for a trait, such as TT or tt; heterozygous means having two different alleles, such as Tt.
The genotype is the genetic makeup, the alleles an organism has; the phenotype is the physical appearance or trait that shows.
A Punnett square is a chart used to predict the possible genotypes of offspring from a cross of two parents.
A cross of two heterozygous parents, Tt and Tt, produces offspring in a ratio of 3 dominant to 1 recessive, or 25 percent recessive.
A cross of TT and tt produces all Tt offspring that show the dominant trait.
Gregor Mendel is called the father of genetics; he studied inheritance of traits in pea plants.
Mendel crossed tall and short pea plants and found that traits are passed on by factors now called genes.
Sexual reproduction combines genetic material from two parents, so offspring are genetically different from each parent.
Sexual reproduction produces variation among offspring, which helps species survive changes in the environment.
Asexual reproduction involves only one parent and produces offspring that are genetically identical to the parent, called clones.
Types of asexual reproduction include binary fission in bacteria, budding in yeast and hydra, regeneration in sea stars and planaria, spores and vegetative propagation in plants.
A sea star can regrow a lost arm, an example of regeneration.
Asexual reproduction is fast and needs only one parent, but produces little variation.
A mutation is a change in the DNA of a gene; mutations can be harmful, helpful or have no effect.
Mutations in sex cells can be passed on to offspring.
Genetic engineering changes the genes of an organism, such as crops that resist pests.
Selective breeding is when humans choose plants or animals with desired traits to breed, such as dogs, cows and crops.
Cloning produces a genetically identical copy of an organism.
Sex chromosomes determine gender: females have XX and males have XY.
Identical twins form from one fertilized egg and have the same DNA; fraternal twins form from two eggs.
Offspring look similar to their parents because they inherit genes from their parents.
Evolution is the process by which species change gradually over many generations.
Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection after studying finches and tortoises on the Galapagos Islands.
Natural selection is the process in which organisms with traits best suited to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce and pass those traits to their offspring.
In natural selection, there is variation among individuals, more offspring are produced than can survive, and the best adapted survive.
Survival of the fittest means organisms best adapted to their environment survive and reproduce.
Variation, differences in traits among individuals of a species, is needed for natural selection.
Over many generations, helpful traits become more common in a population.
Darwin's finches have different beak shapes adapted to different foods on different islands.
Peppered moths became darker during the industrial revolution because dark moths were better camouflaged on soot covered trees and survived predators.
Bacteria can evolve resistance to antibiotics because resistant bacteria survive and reproduce.
Insects can become resistant to pesticides over many generations through natural selection.
Evidence for evolution includes fossils, similar body structures, embryos and DNA of different organisms.
Homologous structures, such as the arm of a human, wing of a bat and flipper of a whale, have similar bones and show common ancestry.
Vestigial structures are body parts that no longer serve a function, such as the human appendix.
The fossil record shows that life forms have changed over time and many species have become extinct.
Species that are closely related have more similar DNA.
Extinction occurs when a species cannot adapt to changes in its environment.
A new species can form when populations are separated by a geographic barrier for a long time.
The human body is organized into cells, tissues, organs and organ systems that work together.
Body systems work together; for example the respiratory system brings in oxygen and the circulatory system carries it to cells.
The circulatory system, or cardiovascular system, includes the heart, blood and blood vessels and transports oxygen, nutrients and wastes throughout the body.
The heart is a muscular organ that pumps blood through the body.
The human heart has four chambers: two atria on top and two ventricles on the bottom.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart; capillaries are tiny blood vessels where materials are exchanged with cells.
Blood is made of plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the cells of the body using hemoglobin, which contains iron.
White blood cells fight infections and destroy germs, bacteria and viruses.
Platelets help blood clot to stop bleeding from a cut.
Plasma is the liquid part of blood that carries nutrients, hormones and wastes.
Exercise makes the heart beat faster and breathing rate increase to deliver more oxygen to muscles.
Pulse rate, or heart rate, is the number of times the heart beats per minute.
The respiratory system includes the nose, trachea, bronchi, lungs and diaphragm and exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide with the air.
In the lungs, oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out of the blood in tiny air sacs called alveoli.
The diaphragm is a muscle below the lungs that contracts to pull air into the lungs.
The trachea, or windpipe, carries air from the throat to the lungs.
We breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide and water vapor.
The digestive system breaks down food into nutrients the body can absorb and use for energy, growth and repair.
The digestive system includes the mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, pancreas and gallbladder.
Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth chew food and saliva begins to break down starch.
The esophagus is a tube that moves food from the mouth to the stomach by muscle contractions.
The stomach mixes food with acid and enzymes to break it down.
Most digestion and absorption of nutrients into the blood happens in the small intestine.
Villi in the small intestine increase surface area for absorbing nutrients.
The large intestine, or colon, absorbs water from undigested food and forms solid waste.
The liver makes bile that helps digest fats, and filters harmful substances from the blood.
The excretory system removes liquid and gas wastes from the body; it includes the kidneys, bladder, skin and lungs.
The kidneys filter wastes from the blood and make urine, which is stored in the bladder.
The skin removes wastes and helps cool the body through sweat.
The nervous system controls and coordinates the body; it includes the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
The brain is the control center of the body; it processes information from the senses and controls thinking, memory and movement.
The spinal cord carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body.
Neurons, or nerve cells, carry electrical messages called impulses throughout the body.
Reflexes are quick automatic responses, such as pulling your hand away from a hot stove, controlled by the spinal cord.
The five senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch, detected by the eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin.
Sense organs contain receptors that detect stimuli and send signals through nerves to the brain.
The eye detects light; the pupil controls how much light enters, the lens focuses light on the retina.
The ear detects sound vibrations; the eardrum vibrates and sends signals to the brain.
The skeletal system is made of bones, cartilage and ligaments; it supports the body, protects organs, allows movement, stores minerals and makes blood cells.
The adult human skeleton has 206 bones.
The skull protects the brain; the rib cage protects the heart and lungs; the backbone, or spine, protects the spinal cord.
Bone marrow inside bones makes red and white blood cells.
Bones need calcium and vitamin D to be strong.
Joints are places where two bones meet; hinge joints in the knee and elbow, ball and socket joints in the shoulder and hip.
Ligaments connect bones to bones; tendons connect muscles to bones.
The muscular system moves the body by contracting muscles; muscles work in pairs, one contracts while the other relaxes.
Skeletal muscles move bones and are voluntary; smooth muscles in organs like the stomach are involuntary; cardiac muscle is found in the heart.
The skeletal system and muscular system work together to move the body.
The integumentary system includes the skin, hair and nails; the skin protects the body from germs, injury and water loss and helps control temperature.
The skin is the largest organ of the human body.
The endocrine system is made of glands that release hormones into the blood to control growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
Hormones are chemical messengers carried by the blood to target cells, which receive the message.
Glands of the endocrine system include the pituitary, thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas that controls blood sugar level; diabetes occurs when the body cannot control blood sugar.
Adrenaline is a hormone that prepares the body for action in an emergency, the fight or flight response.
The immune system protects the body against disease by fighting germs such as bacteria and viruses.
White blood cells make antibodies that attack germs and give immunity.
The lymphatic system carries lymph fluid and helps the immune system fight infection.
The reproductive system produces sex cells and allows humans to produce offspring.
The female reproductive system produces egg cells in the ovaries; the male reproductive system produces sperm cells in the testes.
A fertilized egg develops into an embryo and then a fetus in the uterus.
Puberty is the stage when the body matures and becomes able to reproduce.
The stages of human life are infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood and old age.
Germs such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites can cause infectious diseases that spread from person to person.
Washing hands with soap and water is the best way to prevent the spread of germs and disease.
Covering coughs and sneezes helps prevent spreading germs.
Vaccines help the body build immunity to a disease without getting sick.
Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria and treat bacterial infections.
Noninfectious diseases such as heart disease, diabetes and cancer are not spread from person to person.
Louis Pasteur showed that germs cause disease and developed pasteurization, heating milk to kill bacteria.
Edward Jenner developed the first vaccine, for smallpox.
Refrigerating food slows the growth of bacteria and keeps food from spoiling.
Cooking meat thoroughly kills harmful bacteria.
A balanced diet includes fruits, vegetables, grains, proteins and dairy.
Carbohydrates such as sugars and starches are the main source of energy for the body.
Proteins are needed to build and repair muscles and body tissues.
Fats store energy and help keep the body warm.
Vitamins and minerals keep the body healthy; calcium builds strong bones and teeth; iron helps blood carry oxygen.
Vitamin C from citrus fruits prevents scurvy.
Fiber helps digestion.
Water is needed for all body functions; the human body is mostly water.
Regular exercise strengthens the heart, lungs and muscles.
Smoking damages the lungs and causes lung cancer and heart disease.
Getting enough sleep helps the body and brain stay healthy.
Sunscreen protects skin from ultraviolet rays that cause sunburn and skin cancer.
Allergies are reactions of the immune system to substances such as pollen, dust or certain foods.
Asthma is a disease that narrows the airways in the lungs and makes breathing difficult.
Plants are producers that make their own food by photosynthesis.
Photosynthesis is the process in which plants use energy from sunlight, carbon dioxide and water to make glucose sugar and release oxygen.
The equation for photosynthesis is carbon dioxide plus water plus light energy gives glucose plus oxygen.
Photosynthesis changes light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar.
Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplasts, mostly in the cells of leaves.
Chlorophyll is the green pigment in chloroplasts that absorbs sunlight, making plants green.
Plants take in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny openings in their leaves called stomata.
Plants release oxygen into the air as a product of photosynthesis.
Plants and algae produce most of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.
Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are opposite processes: photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide and releases oxygen, respiration uses oxygen and releases carbon dioxide.
Plants store extra food as starch in roots, stems, seeds and fruits.
Plants need light to grow; a plant kept in the dark turns yellow and becomes weak.
Plants grow toward light, a response called phototropism.
Plant roots grow downward in response to gravity, called gravitropism or geotropism.
A tropism is the growth of a plant toward or away from a stimulus such as light, gravity, water or touch.
The stems of plants bend toward a window because plants grow toward the light.
Some plants like the Venus flytrap respond to touch.
The main parts of a plant are the roots, stem, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds.
Roots absorb water and minerals from the soil, hold the plant in place, anchor the plant and sometimes store food.
Root hairs increase the surface area of roots for absorbing water and nutrients.
Carrots, beets, radishes and sweet potatoes are roots that store food.
Taproots like carrots grow deep; fibrous roots like grass spread out and help hold soil in place.
The stem supports the plant, holds up the leaves toward light, and transports water and nutrients between roots and leaves.
Xylem tubes carry water and minerals up from the roots to the leaves.
Phloem tubes carry sugar food made in the leaves to other parts of the plant.
Vascular plants have xylem and phloem tubes; nonvascular plants like mosses do not, so they stay small and live in moist places.
Leaves are the main organs of photosynthesis; they capture sunlight and make food for the plant.
Broad flat leaves have a large surface area to absorb sunlight.
The waxy cuticle on leaves helps reduce water loss.
Desert plants such as cacti have thick waxy stems that store water, spines instead of leaves, and long or shallow spreading roots to collect water.
Cactus spines are modified leaves that reduce water loss and protect the plant from animals.
Thorns on plants like roses protect them from being eaten by animals.
Flowers are the reproductive organs of flowering plants.
The stamen is the male part of the flower; it has an anther that makes pollen and a filament.
The pistil is the female part of the flower; it has a stigma, style and ovary that contains ovules.
Petals are often brightly colored and scented to attract pollinators such as bees, butterflies and birds.
Sepals are small leaf like parts that protect the flower bud.
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the anther, the male part, to the stigma, the female part, of a flower.
Pollinators such as bees, butterflies, moths, birds and bats carry pollen from flower to flower while drinking nectar.
Bees get nectar and pollen for food from flowers and help flowers by pollinating them, an example of mutualism.
Some plants such as grasses and many trees are pollinated by the wind.
Fertilization happens when pollen joins with an egg cell in the ovule.
After fertilization the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary develops into a fruit.
A fruit is the ripened ovary of a flower that contains and protects seeds; apples, tomatoes, pumpkins and peppers are fruits.
A seed contains a plant embryo, stored food called the cotyledon, and a protective seed coat.
The stored food in a seed provides energy for the young plant until it can make its own food.
Germination is when a seed begins to sprout and grow into a seedling.
Seeds need water, oxygen and the right temperature to germinate; most seeds do not need light to germinate.
Seeds are dispersed, or spread, by wind, water, animals and by bursting pods.
Seeds with wings or fluff like dandelion and maple seeds are spread by the wind.
Seeds in fruits are spread by animals that eat the fruit; burrs with hooks stick to animal fur.
Coconuts float and are spread by water.
Seed dispersal reduces competition between the parent plant and its offspring for water, light and nutrients.
Flowering plants, called angiosperms, make seeds inside fruits.
Gymnosperms, such as pine trees and other conifers, make seeds in cones instead of flowers.
Conifers like pine, spruce and fir are evergreen trees with needle shaped leaves.
Ferns and mosses reproduce with spores instead of seeds.
Mosses are small nonvascular plants that grow in damp shady places.
The life cycle of a flowering plant is seed, germination, seedling, adult plant, flower, pollination, fruit and seed.

Heat is transferred in three ways: conduction, convection and radiation.

Thermal conductors such as metals like copper, aluminum, iron and silver let heat flow through them easily.
Thermal insulators such as wood, plastic, rubber, cloth, wool, foam, styrofoam, glass, fiberglass and air slow the flow of heat.
Pot handles are made of plastic or wood because they are insulators that do not get hot.
Insulation in walls and attics keeps buildings warm in winter and cool in summer.
A thermos or insulated cup keeps hot drinks hot and cold drinks cold by reducing heat transfer.
Wearing layers of clothing, wool and coats traps air that insulates the body and keeps it warm.
Dark colored objects absorb more light and heat up faster than light colored objects; white and light colors reflect light.
A black shirt gets hotter in the sun than a white shirt because dark colors absorb more sunlight.
Shiny surfaces reflect heat radiation.
Friction produces heat; rubbing two objects together makes them warmer.
Specific heat is the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of a substance; water has a high specific heat.
Sand heats up faster than water in the sun.
Electricity is the flow of electric charges, or electrons, through a conductor.
Electric current is the flow of electrons through a circuit, measured in amperes.
Voltage is the push that moves electrons through a circuit, measured in volts; batteries provide voltage.
Resistance is the opposition to the flow of electric current, measured in ohms.
Ohm's law states that voltage equals current times resistance.
An electric circuit is a complete closed path through which electric current flows.
A circuit needs an energy source like a battery, wires that conduct, and a load like a light bulb; a switch opens and closes it.
A closed circuit is a complete path, so current flows and the bulb lights; an open circuit has a break, so current stops.
A switch opens or closes a circuit to turn the current off or on.
In a series circuit, there is only one path for the current; if one bulb burns out, all the bulbs go out.
Adding more bulbs in a series circuit makes each bulb dimmer.
In a parallel circuit, there are two or more paths for the current; if one bulb burns out, the others stay lit.
Homes are wired with parallel circuits so each appliance can be turned on and off separately.
Electrical conductors such as copper, silver, aluminum, gold, iron and other metals allow electricity to flow easily.
Copper is used in electrical wires because it is a good conductor of electricity.
Electrical insulators such as rubber, plastic, glass, wood, cloth and air resist the flow of electricity.
Wires are covered with rubber or plastic insulation to keep electricity from escaping and protect people from shocks.
Salt water is a good conductor of electricity; pure water is a poor conductor.
A short circuit happens when current takes an unintended path with little resistance, which can cause overheating and fires.
Fuses and circuit breakers protect circuits from too much current.
Static electricity is the buildup of electric charges on the surface of an object, often by rubbing.
Rubbing a balloon on your hair transfers electrons and gives the balloon a static charge so it sticks to a wall.
Like charges repel each other and opposite charges attract each other.
Lightning is a discharge of static electricity.
Benjamin Franklin showed that lightning is electricity with his kite experiment.
Thomas Edison invented a long lasting incandescent light bulb.
Electric power is measured in watts; electrical energy use is measured in kilowatt hours.
Turning off lights and unplugging appliances saves electricity and energy.
A magnet is an object that attracts iron and some other metals such as nickel and cobalt.
Magnets attract objects made of iron, steel, nickel and cobalt, such as paper clips, nails and pins.
Magnets do not attract wood, plastic, glass, rubber, paper, aluminum, copper, gold or silver.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole.
Like magnetic poles repel each other; opposite poles attract each other.
A north pole and a south pole attract; two north poles push apart.
The magnetic force is strongest at the poles of a magnet.
A magnetic field is the area around a magnet where magnetic force acts; iron filings show the pattern of the field lines.
Magnetic force can act at a distance and pass through materials like paper, glass and water.
Earth acts like a giant magnet with a magnetic field and magnetic north and south poles.
A compass has a magnetized needle that points north because it lines up with Earth's magnetic field.
A compass is used for finding direction and navigation.
An electromagnet is a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core that becomes a magnet when electric current flows through it.
An electromagnet can be turned on and off, and it gets stronger with more coils of wire or more current.
Electromagnets are used in motors, doorbells, speakers, cranes that lift scrap metal, and MRI machines.
Moving a magnet inside a coil of wire produces an electric current; this is how generators work.
Electricity and magnetism are related; an electric current produces a magnetic field.
Rubbing a steel needle with a magnet can magnetize it.
A force is a push or a pull that can change an object's motion, direction, speed or shape.
Force is measured in newtons using a spring scale.
Forces have both size and direction.
Balanced forces are equal in size and opposite in direction; they cancel out and do not change an object's motion.
Unbalanced forces cause an object to start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.
The net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
Gravity is a force of attraction that pulls objects toward each other; Earth's gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth.
Gravity causes objects to fall to the ground and gives objects weight.
Without air resistance, all objects fall at the same rate, regardless of their mass.
Air resistance is a type of friction that slows falling objects; a parachute increases air resistance.
A feather falls slower than a rock because of air resistance.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces that are touching.
Friction slows down moving objects and produces heat.
Rough surfaces such as sandpaper, carpet, gravel and grass produce more friction than smooth surfaces such as ice, glass and polished wood.
A ball rolls farther on a smooth floor than on carpet or grass because there is less friction.
Lubricants such as oil and grease reduce friction between moving parts, like a bicycle chain.
Wheels, ball bearings and smooth surfaces reduce friction.
Friction is useful for walking, braking, gripping; tire treads and shoe treads increase friction.
Sand on icy roads increases friction so cars do not slide.
Magnetic force and electric force can act at a distance without touching.
Tension is the pulling force in a rope or string; compression is a pushing or squeezing force.
Buoyant force pushes upward on objects in water.
Motion is a change in position over time compared to a reference point.
Speed is the distance an object travels divided by the time it takes; speed equals distance divided by time.
A car that travels 100 kilometers in 2 hours has an average speed of 50 kilometers per hour.
Speed is measured in units such as meters per second, kilometers per hour or miles per hour.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes; speeding up, slowing down or changing direction is acceleration.
An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes.
A distance time graph shows motion; a straight sloped line shows constant speed, and a flat line shows the object is stopped.
Newton's first law of motion, the law of inertia, states that an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion; objects with more mass have more inertia.
Seat belts protect passengers because inertia keeps their bodies moving forward when a car stops suddenly.
Newton's second law states that force equals mass times acceleration, F equals m a.
The greater the force on an object, the greater its acceleration; the greater the mass, the smaller the acceleration for the same force.
A heavier object needs more force to move or to stop than a lighter object.
Newton's third law states that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction; forces come in pairs.
When a swimmer pushes water backward, the water pushes the swimmer forward.
A rocket pushes gases down and the gases push the rocket up.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
Work is done when a force moves an object over a distance; work equals force times distance.
Work is measured in joules.
Power is the rate at which work is done, measured in watts.
Machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force or the distance over which it acts.
Simple machines do not reduce the amount of work; they let you use less force over a greater distance.
The six simple machines are the lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge and screw.
A lever is a bar that pivots on a fixed point called a fulcrum; examples are seesaws, crowbars, scissors, bottle openers and hammers pulling nails.
Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes it easier to lift the load with a lever.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope or chain around it that changes the direction of a force; a flagpole uses a pulley to raise a flag.
A fixed pulley changes the direction of force; a movable pulley reduces the force needed to lift a load.
A block and tackle uses several pulleys to lift heavy loads with less force.
A wheel and axle is a wheel attached to a rod called an axle that turn together, such as a doorknob, steering wheel, screwdriver and bicycle wheel.
An inclined plane is a flat sloping surface, a ramp, that makes it easier to move objects to a higher place.
A longer, less steep ramp requires less force but a greater distance to raise an object.
A wedge is two inclined planes that come together to a sharp edge used to split or cut, such as an axe, knife, chisel or doorstop.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post, such as a jar lid or bolt.
Compound machines are made of two or more simple machines, such as a bicycle, scissors, wheelbarrow and can opener.
Mechanical advantage tells how many times a machine multiplies the force.
Gears are wheels with teeth that transfer motion and force.
A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter.
Mechanical waves, such as sound waves, water waves and seismic waves, need a medium to travel through.
A medium is the matter, solid, liquid or gas, that a wave travels through.
Electromagnetic waves such as light, radio waves, microwaves and X rays can travel through empty space, a vacuum.
In a transverse wave, the particles of the medium move up and down, perpendicular to the direction the wave travels; light and water waves are transverse.
In a longitudinal wave, or compression wave, the particles of the medium move back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels; sound waves are longitudinal.
The crest is the highest point of a wave and the trough is the lowest point.
The amplitude is the height of a wave from rest to crest; larger amplitude waves carry more energy.
The wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest.
The frequency is the number of waves that pass a point each second, measured in hertz.
Higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths.
Sound is a form of energy produced by vibrations.
All sounds are made by vibrating objects, such as a vibrating guitar string, drum skin or vocal cords.
Sound travels as a longitudinal wave through a medium such as air, water or solids.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids, and slowest through gases like air.
Sound cannot travel through empty space, a vacuum, because there are no particles to vibrate.
Sound travels about 340 meters per second in air.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is and depends on the frequency of the vibrations.
Fast vibrations, high frequency, make a high pitched sound; slow vibrations, low frequency, make a low pitched sound.
Shorter, thinner or tighter strings vibrate faster and make a higher pitch; longer, thicker or looser strings make a lower pitch.
Volume, or loudness, depends on the amplitude of the sound wave; larger vibrations make louder sounds.
Loudness is measured in decibels; very loud sounds can damage hearing.
An echo is a sound wave that reflects off a surface and returns.
Soft materials like curtains, carpet and foam absorb sound and reduce echoes.
Musical instruments make sound by vibrating strings, air columns, or surfaces such as drums.
The human ear detects sound vibrations; humans hear frequencies from about 20 to 20,000 hertz.
Ultrasound is sound with frequencies too high for humans to hear; dogs and bats can hear high pitched sounds.
Light is a form of energy that travels as electromagnetic waves and can travel through empty space.
Light travels in straight lines at about 300,000 kilometers per second, faster than anything else.
Light travels faster than sound.
A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light.
Shadows change in length and direction as the position of the light source changes.
Transparent materials such as clear glass, clear plastic, water and air let almost all light pass through, so you can see clearly through them.
Translucent materials such as wax paper, frosted glass and tissue paper let some light pass through and scatter it.
Opaque materials such as wood, metal, rock, cardboard and brick block all light from passing through.
Reflection is when light bounces off a surface; mirrors reflect light.
The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
Smooth shiny surfaces like mirrors reflect light evenly and form images.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium into another, such as from air into water or glass.
A straw in a glass of water looks bent or broken because of refraction.
Lenses refract light to form images; convex lenses bend light inward and magnify objects; concave lenses spread light out.
Magnifying glasses, microscopes, telescopes, cameras, eyeglasses and the human eye use lenses.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the rainbow, the visible spectrum, by refraction.
White light is made of all the colors of the visible spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet.
Rainbows form when sunlight is refracted and reflected by raindrops.
Red light has the longest wavelength of visible light and violet light has the shortest wavelength.
We see an object's color because it reflects that color of light and absorbs the other colors; a red apple reflects red light.
A white object reflects all colors of light; a black object absorbs all colors of light.
Absorption is when light energy is taken in by a material and changed into heat.
We see objects because light reflects off them into our eyes.
The electromagnetic spectrum includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X rays and gamma rays.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat; ultraviolet radiation from the Sun causes sunburn.
X rays pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by bones, so they are used to see bones.
Microwaves are used to cook food and for communication; radio waves carry radio and television signals.
Lasers produce a narrow, intense beam of light of one color.
Fiber optic cables carry light signals using total internal reflection.
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons with a spring scale.
An object's mass stays the same everywhere, but its weight changes with gravity, so an astronaut weighs less on the Moon.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, measured in liters, milliliters or cubic centimeters.
The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder or measuring cup.
The volume of an irregular solid such as a rock can be measured by water displacement in a graduated cylinder.
The volume of a rectangular box is length times width times height.
Density is mass divided by volume, the amount of mass in a given volume.
Objects less dense than water float; objects more dense than water sink.
The density of water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter.
Oil floats on water because oil is less dense than water.
Wood and ice float on water because they are less dense than water; ice is less dense than liquid water.
Iron, rocks and coins sink in water because they are denser than water.
Physical properties can be observed or measured without changing the substance, such as color, shape, size, texture, mass, volume, density, hardness, magnetism, boiling point and melting point.
Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts with other substances, such as flammability, the ability to burn, or reacting with acid or rusting.
Texture describes how something feels, such as rough, smooth, soft or hard.
Flexibility is the ability to bend without breaking; rubber and plastic can be flexible.
Buoyancy is the upward force a fluid exerts on an object; it makes objects float.
The three common states of matter are solid, liquid and gas; plasma is a fourth state found in stars and lightning.
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume; its particles are packed tightly and vibrate in place.
A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container, and its particles slide past each other.
A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume; it spreads out to fill its container, and its particles move quickly and far apart.
Gas particles have the most energy and move the fastest; solid particles have the least energy.
Particles of matter are always moving; heating makes particles move faster and spread apart, cooling makes them slow down.
Adding heat energy can change a solid to a liquid and a liquid to a gas.
Removing heat energy can change a gas to a liquid and a liquid to a solid.
Melting is the change from a solid to a liquid when heat is added, such as ice melting into water.
Freezing is the change from a liquid to a solid when heat is removed, such as water freezing into ice.
Evaporation and boiling are changes from a liquid to a gas.
Condensation is the change from a gas to a liquid when a gas is cooled.
Sublimation is the change from a solid directly into a gas, like dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide.
Deposition is the change from a gas directly into a solid, like frost forming.
Water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius, 32 degrees Fahrenheit, and boils at 100 degrees Celsius, 212 degrees Fahrenheit, at sea level.
Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius.
The temperature of a substance stays the same while it is melting or boiling.
Water is the only common substance found naturally as a solid, liquid and gas on Earth.
Water expands when it freezes, so ice takes up more space than the same mass of liquid water.
Steam is water vapor, water in the gas state.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled; thermal expansion.
Bridges and sidewalks have gaps to allow for expansion in hot weather.
Gas in a balloon expands when heated and the balloon gets bigger; a balloon shrinks in the cold.
Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so salt is spread on icy roads to melt ice.
A physical change changes the size, shape or state of a substance but does not make a new substance.
Examples of physical changes are cutting, tearing, folding, crushing, bending, melting, freezing, boiling, evaporating, dissolving and mixing.
Changes of state such as melting ice and boiling water are physical changes.
Dissolving sugar or salt in water is a physical change; the salt can be recovered by evaporating the water.
A chemical change, or chemical reaction, produces one or more new substances with different properties.
Examples of chemical changes are burning, rusting, cooking, baking a cake, digesting food, rotting, souring milk, tarnishing silver, and fireworks exploding.
Burning wood or paper is a chemical change that produces ash, smoke, carbon dioxide, heat and light.
Evidence of a chemical change includes a gas being produced as bubbles, a color change, a new smell, light or heat given off, temperature change, and a precipitate forming.
Mixing baking soda and vinegar produces bubbles of carbon dioxide gas, a sign of a chemical reaction.
A precipitate is a solid that forms when two liquid solutions are mixed in a chemical reaction.
An exothermic reaction releases heat energy; an endothermic reaction absorbs heat energy and feels cold.
Combustion, or burning, is a chemical reaction of a fuel with oxygen that releases heat and light energy.
A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat; removing any one puts out the fire.
In a chemical reaction, reactants are the starting substances and products are the new substances formed.
The law of conservation of mass states that matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction; the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants.
In a closed container, the total mass stays the same during any physical or chemical change.
A chemical equation must be balanced so the same number of each type of atom is on both sides.
Catalysts speed up chemical reactions without being used up.
Increasing temperature, concentration or surface area makes chemical reactions happen faster.
Photosynthesis, respiration and digestion are chemical reactions in living things.
An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still has the properties of that element.
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons have a positive charge, neutrons have no charge, and electrons have a negative charge.
Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the center of the atom; electrons move around the nucleus in energy levels, or shells, forming an electron cloud.
The nucleus contains almost all of the mass of an atom.
The number of protons in an atom is its atomic number and determines what element it is.
An atom is neutral when it has the same number of protons and electrons.
Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes.
An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons and has an electric charge.
An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, such as oxygen, hydrogen, carbon, iron, gold and helium.
There are more than 100 known elements, organized in the periodic table.
The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number into rows called periods and columns called groups or families.
Elements in the same group or column of the periodic table have similar chemical properties.
Dmitri Mendeleev created the first periodic table.
Metals are on the left side of the periodic table; nonmetals are on the right; metalloids are in between.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Malleable means a metal can be hammered into thin sheets; ductile means it can be drawn into wires.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors of heat and electricity.
Noble gases such as helium, neon and argon are very unreactive.
Each element has a chemical symbol: H is hydrogen, O is oxygen, C is carbon, N is nitrogen, Na is sodium, Cl is chlorine, Fe is iron, Au is gold, Ag is silver, Cu is copper, He is helium.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant element in the universe.
Oxygen is the most abundant element in Earth's crust; silicon is second.
Carbon is the element found in all living things and organic compounds.
Helium is a light gas used to fill balloons because it is less dense than air.
A compound is a substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
Water, H2O, is a compound made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Carbon dioxide, CO2, is a compound made of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
Table salt, sodium chloride, NaCl, is a compound made of sodium and chlorine.
The properties of a compound are different from the properties of the elements that form it.
A molecule is two or more atoms joined together by chemical bonds.
An oxygen molecule, O2, is made of two oxygen atoms that share two pairs of electrons in a double covalent bond.
Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons; ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred from one atom to another.
A chemical formula shows the elements and number of atoms in a compound.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined that keep their own properties and can be separated by physical means.
Examples of mixtures are salad, trail mix, sand and water, salt water, air and soil.
Mixtures can be separated by filtering, evaporation, magnets, sifting, picking out by hand and distillation.
A magnet can separate iron filings from a mixture of sand and iron.
Filtration separates solids that do not dissolve, like sand, from a liquid.
Evaporation separates a dissolved solid such as salt from water; the water evaporates and the salt is left behind.
Distillation separates liquids by boiling and condensing them.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance dissolves evenly in another, such as salt water or sugar water.
In a solution, the solute is the substance that dissolves and the solvent is the substance that does the dissolving.
In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.
Stirring, heating and crushing into smaller pieces make a solid dissolve faster.
More sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold water.
A saturated solution holds as much solute as it can dissolve.
A suspension is a mixture in which particles settle out over time, such as muddy water.
Sand does not dissolve in water; salt and sugar dissolve in water.
Acids taste sour and have a pH less than 7; examples are lemon juice, vinegar and stomach acid.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery and have a pH greater than 7; examples are soap, baking soda, ammonia and bleach.
Pure water is neutral with a pH of 7.
The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a substance is, from 0 to 14.
Litmus paper and pH paper are indicators that show whether a substance is an acid or base; acids turn blue litmus paper red and bases turn red litmus paper blue.
Acids and bases neutralize each other to form water and a salt.
Acid rain forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fossil fuels mix with water in the air.
Acid rain harms lakes, fish, forests and dissolves statues made of limestone and marble.
As altitude increases, air pressure decreases and air temperature usually decreases.
As depth in the ocean increases, water pressure increases, temperature decreases and light decreases.
As depth inside Earth increases, temperature and pressure increase.
As temperature increases, the particles of a substance move faster and the substance usually expands.
As temperature decreases, particles move slower and the substance usually contracts.
As the temperature of water increases, evaporation increases.
As the temperature of water increases, more sugar or salt can dissolve, but less gas such as oxygen can dissolve.
As air temperature increases, the amount of water vapor the air can hold increases.
As humidity increases, evaporation of sweat decreases and it feels hotter.
As wind speed increases, evaporation increases and wind chill makes it feel colder.
As the mass of an object increases, its weight increases and more force is needed to move it.
As the mass of objects increases, the gravitational force between them increases.
As the distance between objects increases, the gravitational force between them decreases.
As the force on an object increases, its acceleration increases.
As the mass of an object increases, the acceleration from the same force decreases.
As speed increases, kinetic energy increases.
As height increases, gravitational potential energy increases.
As a ball falls, its potential energy decreases and its kinetic energy increases.
As friction increases, an object slows down faster and more heat is produced.
As the steepness of a ramp increases, the force needed to push an object up increases.
As the length of a ramp increases for the same height, the force needed decreases.
As the number of coils in an electromagnet increases, its strength increases.
As the current in an electromagnet increases, its strength increases.
As the distance from a magnet increases, its magnetic force decreases.
As voltage increases, current increases; as resistance increases, current decreases.
As more bulbs are added in series, each bulb gets dimmer.
As frequency increases, pitch gets higher and wavelength gets shorter.
As amplitude increases, sound gets louder and waves carry more energy.
As the length of a vibrating string increases, the pitch gets lower.
As the tension of a string increases, the pitch gets higher.
As distance from a sound source increases, the sound gets quieter.
As distance from a light source increases, light gets dimmer.
As an object moves closer to a light source, its shadow gets larger.
As the Sun gets higher in the sky, shadows get shorter.
As latitude increases, away from the equator, average temperatures decrease.
As distance from the Sun increases, a planet's year gets longer and its temperature usually decreases.
As the Moon moves from new moon to full moon, the lit part we see gets larger, which is waxing.
As the Moon moves from full moon to new moon, the lit part we see gets smaller, which is waning.
As days get longer in spring, temperatures increase.
As days get shorter in fall, temperatures decrease.
As carbon dioxide in the atmosphere increases, Earth's average temperature increases.
As global temperature increases, sea level rises and ice melts.
As the slope of land increases, runoff and erosion increase.
As plant cover increases, erosion decreases.
As the speed of water in a river increases, it can carry larger sediment and erosion increases.
As a river slows down, it deposits sediment.
As rock layers get deeper, they are usually older.
As time passes, a radioactive element decays and less of it remains.
As the surface area of a solid increases, it dissolves, reacts and melts faster.
As the concentration of reactants increases, reactions go faster.
As the population of prey increases, the population of predators usually increases.
As the population of predators increases, the population of prey usually decreases.
As the number of producers decreases, the number of consumers decreases.
As food becomes scarce, competition increases and populations decrease.
As a population grows beyond the carrying capacity, deaths increase.
As biodiversity increases, an ecosystem becomes more stable.
As pollution increases, the health of organisms in an ecosystem decreases.
As light increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases up to a point.
As carbon dioxide increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases up to a point.
As exercise increases, heart rate, breathing rate and body temperature increase.
As a person ages from child to adult, height increases until growth stops.
As the number of trials increases, results become more reliable.
As the voltage of a battery increases, a bulb gets brighter.
As water freezes, its volume increases and its density decreases.
As water vapor cools, it condenses into liquid.
As a gas is compressed, its pressure increases.
As a gas is heated in a closed container, its pressure increases.
As air is heated, it becomes less dense and rises.
As air cools, it becomes denser and sinks.
As the angle of sunlight becomes more direct, the ground heats more.
As cloud cover increases at night, the temperature drops less.
As ocean water temperature increases, hurricanes become stronger.
As elevation increases on a mountain, the types of plants change from forest to grasses to bare rock.
Natural resources are materials from nature that people use, such as air, water, soil, minerals, plants, animals, sunlight and fossil fuels.
Renewable resources can be replaced naturally in a short time, such as sunlight, wind, water, trees, plants, animals and crops.
Nonrenewable resources cannot be replaced in a human lifetime or take millions of years to form, such as coal, oil, natural gas, petroleum, minerals and metals.
Fossil fuels such as coal, oil, petroleum, natural gas and gasoline are nonrenewable resources formed from the remains of ancient plants and animals over millions of years.
Fossil fuels formed from organisms that lived millions of years ago and were buried under layers of sediment and pressure.
Coal formed from ancient swamp plants; oil and natural gas formed from tiny ancient ocean organisms.
Gasoline is made from oil, which is also called petroleum or crude oil.
Plastics are made from petroleum, a nonrenewable resource.
Renewable energy sources include solar energy, wind energy, hydroelectric energy from moving water, geothermal energy, tidal energy and biomass.
Solar energy from sunlight is a renewable source of energy that can be collected with solar panels.
Wind energy is a renewable energy source; wind turbines change wind into electricity.
Hydroelectric power plants use the energy of falling or flowing water at dams to generate electricity.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth to make electricity and heat buildings.
Biomass energy comes from burning plant material, wood and animal waste, and is renewable.
Renewable energy sources such as solar and wind produce little pollution and will not run out.
Solar energy and wind energy are not always available because the Sun does not always shine and the wind does not always blow.
Burning fossil fuels causes air pollution, acid rain and releases carbon dioxide that contributes to global warming.
Most electricity in the United States is produced by burning fossil fuels such as coal and natural gas.
Trees are a renewable resource if new trees are planted to replace those that are cut down.
Water is a renewable resource replaced by the water cycle, but fresh water supplies are limited.
Ores are rocks that contain useful minerals or metals that can be mined, such as iron ore.
Minerals and metals are mined from Earth's crust; mining can damage land and habitats.
Conservation is the careful use of natural resources so they last longer and are not wasted.
The three Rs of conservation are reduce, reuse and recycle.
Reducing means using less of a resource, such as turning off lights, taking shorter showers and using less water.
Reusing means using an item again, such as refilling a water bottle or using cloth bags instead of throwing them away.
Recycling means collecting used materials such as paper, glass, aluminum cans and plastic and making them into new products.
Recycling aluminum cans, paper and plastic conserves natural resources, saves energy and reduces waste in landfills.
Composting turns food scraps and yard waste into nutrient rich soil.
Carpooling, riding bikes, walking and using public transportation conserve fuel and reduce air pollution.
Using energy efficient light bulbs and appliances saves energy.
Turning off lights and electronics when they are not in use conserves energy.
Pollution is the addition of harmful substances to the environment, the air, water or land.
Air pollution comes from burning fossil fuels in cars, factories and power plants, and causes smog, acid rain and breathing problems.
Water pollution comes from sewage, oil spills, factory waste, fertilizers and pesticides that run off into rivers, lakes and oceans.
Fertilizer runoff causes too much algae to grow in lakes, which uses up oxygen and kills fish.
Oil spills harm ocean animals such as birds, fish and sea otters by coating their feathers and fur.
Land pollution comes from trash, litter, landfills and toxic waste.
Plastic trash in the ocean harms sea animals that eat it or get tangled in it.
Pesticides kill insects and pests but can harm other animals and pollute water.
Deforestation is cutting down forests; it destroys habitats, increases erosion and adds carbon dioxide to the air.
Habitat loss from building cities, roads and farms is a major threat to wildlife.
Protecting habitats, creating wildlife refuges and national parks, and laws protecting endangered species help preserve wildlife.
Planting trees helps reduce erosion, provides habitats and removes carbon dioxide from the air.
Scrubbers and filters on smokestacks reduce air pollution from factories.
Wastewater treatment plants clean sewage water before returning it to rivers.
Water can be made safe to drink by filtering and adding chlorine to kill bacteria, or by boiling it.
Technology is the use of scientific knowledge to solve problems and make tools and products that meet human needs.
Engineers design, build and test solutions to problems.
The engineering design process includes identifying a problem, researching, brainstorming, designing, building a prototype, testing, and improving the design.
A prototype is an early model of a design that is built and tested.
Engineers must consider constraints such as cost, safety, materials, time and the environment when designing.
New technology can have both benefits and harmful effects on society and the environment.
Refrigeration lets food be stored longer and shipped long distances without spoiling.
Improvements in transportation and shipping have increased the variety of foods available in stores.
The invention of the telescope allowed scientists to observe objects in space more closely.
The invention of the microscope allowed scientists to discover cells and microorganisms.
Computers help scientists collect, store, analyze and share large amounts of data.
Satellites are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation and mapping.
Science is a way of learning about the natural world through observation and experiments.
The scientific method includes asking a question, doing research, forming a hypothesis, testing with an experiment, analyzing data and drawing a conclusion.
A hypothesis is a testable prediction or possible explanation that can be tested by an experiment, often written as an if then statement.
An observation is information gathered using the senses or instruments.
Qualitative observations describe qualities such as color, smell and texture; quantitative observations use numbers and measurements.
An inference is a conclusion or explanation based on observations and prior knowledge.
A prediction is a statement about what will happen in the future based on evidence or patterns.
In a controlled experiment, only one variable is changed at a time while all other conditions are kept the same.
The independent variable, or manipulated variable, is the factor the scientist changes on purpose.
The dependent variable, or responding variable, is the factor that is measured and changes in response to the independent variable.
Controlled variables, or constants, are the conditions kept the same in all groups of an experiment so the test is fair.
A control group is not changed and is used for comparison with the experimental group.
To make an experiment a fair test, change only one variable and keep all other variables the same.
Repeating an experiment several times, or doing many trials, makes the results more reliable and reduces the effect of errors.
Using a large sample size makes results more reliable.
Other scientists repeat experiments to check and verify the results.
Scientists share their results by publishing reports so others can review and repeat the experiment.
Data are recorded in data tables and displayed in graphs and charts to show patterns.
A line graph shows how data change over time or how one variable affects another.
A bar graph compares amounts in different categories.
A pie chart, or circle graph, shows parts of a whole as percentages.
The independent variable is plotted on the x axis and the dependent variable on the y axis of a graph.
A conclusion is a statement that tells whether the data support the hypothesis.
If the data do not support the hypothesis, the scientist should revise the hypothesis and test again.
A scientific theory is a well tested explanation supported by a lot of evidence, such as the theory of plate tectonics or evolution.
A scientific law describes what always happens under certain conditions, such as the law of gravity.
Scientific knowledge can change when new evidence is discovered.
A model represents an object or process that is too big, too small, too far or too complex to study directly, such as a globe or a model of an atom.
Models help scientists explain and predict, but every model has limitations.
Evidence is data and observations that support or refute a claim.
Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other.
Scientists should record observations and data honestly and accurately, even if the results are unexpected.
The metric system, or International System of Units, SI, is used by scientists around the world.
Length is measured in meters, centimeters, millimeters or kilometers with a ruler, meter stick or measuring tape.
A kilometer is 1000 meters; a meter is 100 centimeters; a centimeter is 10 millimeters.
Mass is measured in grams or kilograms with a balance, such as a triple beam balance or electronic scale.
A kilogram is 1000 grams.
Volume of liquids is measured in liters or milliliters with a graduated cylinder, beaker or measuring cup.
A liter is 1000 milliliters.
Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit with a thermometer.
Time is measured in seconds, minutes and hours with a stopwatch, timer or clock.
Force and weight are measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Energy is measured in joules or calories; power is measured in watts.
A microscope magnifies very small objects such as cells and microorganisms so they can be seen.
A hand lens or magnifying glass makes small objects such as insects, rocks and leaves look larger.
A telescope is used to see distant objects in space such as stars, planets and the Moon.
Binoculars make faraway objects such as birds appear closer.
A compass shows direction using Earth's magnetic field.
A pH meter or litmus paper measures whether a solution is acidic or basic.
A stopwatch measures time, used to find how fast something moves.
A graduated cylinder measures the volume of a liquid more accurately than a beaker.
A seismograph measures earthquakes; a barometer measures air pressure; an anemometer measures wind speed; a rain gauge measures rainfall.
Safety goggles protect the eyes from chemicals, heat, splashes and flying objects during experiments.
Wear safety goggles when working with chemicals, heating substances, or anything that could splash or break.
Gloves protect the hands when handling chemicals, hot objects or living things such as bacteria samples.
Lab aprons protect clothing and skin from spills.
Never eat or drink in the science laboratory because food may become contaminated with chemicals or bacteria.
Bringing food and drinks into the laboratory is unsafe.
Wash your hands with soap and water after an experiment and before leaving the laboratory.
Tie back long hair and roll up loose sleeves when working near flames.
Tell the teacher immediately if there is an accident, a spill or broken glass.
Follow the teacher's directions and read all instructions before starting an experiment.
Never taste or touch chemicals; wave fumes toward your nose to smell a substance safely.
Use tongs or heat resistant gloves, called oven mitts, to handle hot objects.
Point test tubes away from yourself and others when heating them.
Dispose of chemicals properly as directed by the teacher, not down the sink unless told to.
Clean the work area and all materials after finishing an experiment.
An eyewash station is used to rinse chemicals out of the eyes; a fire extinguisher puts out fires.
Students observing animals should handle them gently and treat living things humanely.

Earth rotates, or spins, on its axis once every 24 hours, which causes day and night.
Day and night are caused by the rotation of Earth on its axis.
The side of Earth facing the Sun has daytime; the side facing away from the Sun has nighttime.
Earth rotates from west to east, so the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west.
The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west each day.
The Sun, Moon and stars appear to move across the sky from east to west because Earth rotates.
The apparent daily movement of the Sun across the sky is caused by Earth's rotation.
Shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the Sun is low in the sky, and shortest at noon when the Sun is highest.
In the morning shadows point west, and in the afternoon shadows point east.
A sundial uses the shadow cast by the Sun to tell time.
Earth revolves, or orbits, around the Sun once every year, about 365 days.
One revolution of Earth around the Sun takes one year, about 365 and one fourth days.
A leap year adds an extra day every four years because Earth's orbit takes about 365 and one fourth days.
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees.
The seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis as Earth revolves around the Sun.
When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun it is summer there, with more direct sunlight and longer days.
When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun it is winter there, with less direct sunlight and shorter days.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
In summer the Sun is higher in the sky, its rays are more direct, and there are more hours of daylight.
In winter the Sun is lower in the sky, its rays are spread out at an angle, and there are fewer hours of daylight.
The summer solstice, around June 21, has the most hours of daylight in the Northern Hemisphere and is the longest day of the year.
The winter solstice, around December 21, has the fewest hours of daylight in the Northern Hemisphere and is the shortest day of the year.
At the spring and fall equinoxes, around March 21 and September 22, day and night are about equal in length everywhere.
The seasons are not caused by Earth's distance from the Sun; Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.
Earth's orbit around the Sun is an ellipse, a slightly oval shape.
The equator receives the most direct sunlight and the most solar energy throughout the year.
The North Pole has about six months of daylight and six months of darkness.
The North Star, Polaris, stays almost in the same place in the sky because it is nearly above Earth's axis at the North Pole.
Time zones exist because Earth rotates, so different places face the Sun at different times.
The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite.
The Moon revolves around Earth about once every 27 days, and its cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days, about one month.
The Moon rotates once on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit Earth, so the same side of the Moon always faces Earth.
The Moon does not make its own light; the Moon shines because it reflects light from the Sun.
Moonlight is sunlight reflected off the surface of the Moon.
Moon phases are caused by the changing positions of the Moon, Earth and Sun as the Moon orbits Earth, so we see different amounts of the lit half.
The phases of the Moon are new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter and waning crescent.
During a new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun and the lit side faces away from Earth, so we see no Moon.
During a full moon Earth is between the Sun and the Moon and we see the whole lit side of the Moon.
Waxing means the lit part of the Moon is getting larger; waning means the lit part is getting smaller.
It takes about two weeks to go from new moon to full moon.
The Moon has no atmosphere, no liquid water, no wind and no weather, so footprints on the Moon last a very long time.
Because the Moon has no atmosphere, temperatures on the Moon are extremely hot in sunlight and extremely cold in darkness.
The Moon's surface has craters made by meteorite impacts, mountains, and dark flat plains called maria.
The Moon has craters because it has no atmosphere to burn up meteoroids and no wind or water to erode the craters.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's gravity, so an object weighs less on the Moon but has the same mass.
The Moon's gravitational pull on Earth's oceans causes tides.
Astronauts first landed on the Moon in 1969 during the Apollo 11 mission.
A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, during a full moon.
A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and the Moon's shadow falls on Earth, during a new moon.
The Moon appears about the same size as the Sun in the sky because the Sun is much larger but much farther away.
The Sun is a star, a huge ball of hot glowing gases, mostly hydrogen and helium.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth and the largest object in the solar system.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen atoms to form helium in its core.
The Sun gives off light and heat energy, called solar energy or radiant energy, which travels to Earth as electromagnetic radiation.
The Sun is the main source of energy for Earth, including the energy for weather, the water cycle and food chains.
Energy from the Sun reaches Earth by radiation through the vacuum of space.
Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.
The Sun is about 150 million kilometers, or 93 million miles, from Earth.
The Sun is a medium sized, middle aged yellow star.
The Sun appears larger and brighter than other stars because it is much closer to Earth.
Sunspots are dark cooler areas on the Sun's surface; solar flares are eruptions of energy from the Sun.
The Sun's gravity keeps the planets in their orbits around the Sun.
The solar system is made of the Sun and all the objects that orbit it, including planets, moons, asteroids, comets and dwarf planets.
The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small, rocky, dense terrestrial planets.
The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are large gas giants with rings and many moons.
Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun and the smallest planet, and it has almost no atmosphere.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat by a strong greenhouse effect.
Venus is the brightest planet seen from Earth and is sometimes called the morning star or evening star.
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only planet known to have liquid water on its surface and life.
Earth has liquid water and life because it is the right distance from the Sun and has an atmosphere.
Mars is called the Red Planet because its soil contains iron oxide, or rust.
Mars has the largest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons, and evidence that liquid water once flowed there.
Mars has two small moons and is the fourth planet from the Sun.
Jupiter is the largest planet in the solar system, a gas giant with the Great Red Spot, a giant storm.
Saturn is a gas giant known for its large, bright rings made of ice and rock.
Uranus is tilted on its side and appears blue green.
Neptune is the planet farthest from the Sun, a cold blue gas giant with strong winds.
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit the Sun and have longer years.
Planets closer to the Sun are warmer and have shorter years; planets farther away are colder.
Planets do not produce their own light; we see planets because they reflect sunlight.
Planets appear to wander across the sky compared to the background stars.
The asteroid belt lies between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter.
Asteroids are rocky objects that orbit the Sun, mostly in the asteroid belt.
Comets are made of ice, dust and rock and develop glowing tails that point away from the Sun when they come near the Sun.
A meteor is a streak of light, called a shooting star, made when a meteoroid burns up in Earth's atmosphere.
A meteorite is a piece of rock from space that lands on Earth's surface.
Gravity is the force that keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth and the planets in orbit around the Sun.
The force of gravity between two objects depends on their masses and the distance between them.
Gravity is stronger between objects with more mass and weaker when they are farther apart.
Jupiter has the strongest gravity of the planets because it has the most mass.
The geocentric model placed Earth at the center of the universe; the heliocentric model places the Sun at the center of the solar system.
Copernicus proposed the heliocentric model with the planets orbiting the Sun.
Galileo used a telescope to discover four moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, supporting the heliocentric model.
Kepler discovered that the planets move in elliptical orbits around the Sun.
Isaac Newton described the law of universal gravitation.
A star is a huge ball of hot gas that gives off its own light and heat through nuclear fusion.
Stars appear as tiny points of light because they are very far away.
The color of a star shows its surface temperature: blue stars are the hottest, then white, yellow, orange, and red stars are the coolest.
The brightness of a star as seen from Earth depends on its size, its temperature and its distance from Earth.
Stars are born in nebulas, which are huge clouds of gas and dust in space.
A star like the Sun becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf at the end of its life.
Very massive stars end in a supernova explosion and can become neutron stars or black holes.
A black hole has gravity so strong that not even light can escape.
Distances to stars are measured in light years; a light year is the distance light travels in one year.
After the Sun, the nearest star to Earth is Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light years away.
Constellations are groups of stars that form patterns in the sky, such as Orion and the Big Dipper.
Different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves around the Sun.
Stars are not visible during the day because the light from the Sun is much brighter.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.
Our solar system is in the Milky Way galaxy, which is a spiral galaxy.
Galaxies can be spiral, elliptical or irregular in shape.
The universe contains billions of galaxies.
The Big Bang theory says the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.
The red shift of light from distant galaxies shows the universe is expanding.
Telescopes collect light and make distant objects such as stars, planets and galaxies appear closer and clearer.
Astronomers are scientists who study stars, planets and other objects in space.
The Hubble Space Telescope orbits above Earth's atmosphere, so it gets clearer images than telescopes on the ground.
Space probes, rovers and satellites are used to explore space and other planets.
Artificial satellites orbit Earth and are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation and studying Earth.
Astronauts in orbit feel weightless because they are in free fall around Earth.
Astronauts need space suits to provide oxygen, pressure and protection from extreme temperatures and radiation.
The International Space Station is a laboratory that orbits Earth.
Rockets move forward by pushing hot gases out the back, an example of Newton's third law.
In space there is no air, so sound cannot travel; space is a vacuum.
