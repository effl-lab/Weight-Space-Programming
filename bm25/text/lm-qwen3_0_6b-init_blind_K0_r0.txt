Nonrenewable resources cannot be replaced in a human lifetime: coal, oil, natural gas, minerals, metals.
Fossil fuels are coal, oil petroleum and natural gas formed from remains of ancient plants and animals over millions of years.
Fossil fuels are nonrenewable and have limited supplies.
Coal formed from ancient swamp plants buried and compressed over millions of years.
Oil and natural gas formed from tiny ancient sea organisms.
Gasoline and diesel are made from oil petroleum.
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, pollutes the air and causes acid rain and smog.
Renewable energy sources: solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal, tidal and biomass energy.
Solar energy comes from sunlight; solar panels photovoltaic cells change sunlight into electricity.
Solar energy is renewable but works only when the Sun shines.
Wind turbines change the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity.
Hydroelectric power plants use moving water flowing through dams to turn turbines and generate electricity.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth.
Tidal energy uses the movement of ocean tides.
Biomass energy comes from burning wood, plant material and animal waste; ethanol is made from corn.
Nuclear power plants use fission of uranium to make electricity; uranium is nonrenewable and produces radioactive waste.
Nuclear energy does not produce carbon dioxide air pollution.
Power plants burn fuel to boil water into steam that turns a turbine connected to a generator.
Conservation means using resources wisely so they last longer.
Reduce, reuse and recycle helps conserve natural resources and reduce waste.
Recycling paper saves trees; recycling aluminum cans saves energy and metal ore.
Recycling glass, plastic and metal reduces trash in landfills.
Reusing a plastic bag or glass jar is an example of reuse.
Composting turns food scraps and yard waste into fertile soil.
Turning off lights and unplugging devices conserves electricity.
Using public transportation, carpooling, walking or riding a bicycle saves fuel and reduces pollution.
Fuel efficient and electric cars and hybrid vehicles use less gasoline.
Insulation in homes saves energy used for heating and cooling.
Taking shorter showers and fixing leaks conserves water.
Pollution is harmful substances added to the air, water or land.
Air pollution comes from burning fossil fuels in cars, factories and power plants.
Smog is air pollution from vehicle exhaust and factories.
Acid rain forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning coal mix with water in the air.
Acid rain harms lakes, fish, forests and buildings.
Water pollution comes from sewage, chemicals, oil spills, fertilizer and pesticide runoff.
Oil spills harm ocean birds, fish and sea animals.
Littering and plastic trash harm wildlife in oceans.
Landfills hold garbage and can leak pollution into groundwater.
Deforestation is cutting down forests; it destroys habitats and causes erosion.
Planting trees reforestation helps restore habitats and absorb carbon dioxide.
Overfishing reduces fish populations.
Overgrazing by cattle removes plants and causes erosion and desertification.
Urban development replaces habitats with buildings and roads.
The Dust Bowl in the 1930s was caused by drought and poor farming that let wind blow away topsoil.
Crop rotation keeps soil fertile.
Water treatment plants clean water to make it safe to drink by filtering and adding chlorine.
Filtering water removes solid particles; boiling water kills germs.
Clean fresh water is a limited resource.
Desalination removes salt from ocean water.
Environmental impact of mining includes habitat destruction and water pollution.
Wind and solar energy do not pollute the air.
Dams for hydroelectric power can block fish migration and flood habitats.
Electric cars produce no exhaust but need electricity from power plants.
Paper, cardboard, wood and cotton are made from plants and are renewable.
Plastic is made from oil petroleum.
Steel is made from iron ore.
Glass is made from sand.
Aluminum is made from bauxite ore.
Natural gas is used for heating homes and cooking.
Batteries store chemical energy and should be recycled.
Energy efficient light bulbs LED use less electricity than incandescent bulbs.
Protecting endangered species and habitats conserves biodiversity.
National parks protect natural areas and wildlife.
Pesticides kill insects that eat crops but can harm other organisms.
Organic farming does not use synthetic pesticides or fertilizers.
Greenhouse gases from human activities increase global temperatures.
Carbon dioxide is released by burning wood, coal, oil and gas.
Trees and plants remove carbon dioxide from the air.
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.
The three common states of matter are solid, liquid and gas.
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume.
A liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container.
A gas has no definite shape or volume and expands to fill its container.
Particles in a solid are tightly packed and vibrate in place.
Particles in a liquid are close together but slide past each other.
Particles in a gas are far apart and move quickly and freely.
Heating makes particles move faster; cooling makes particles move slower.
Melting is a solid changing into a liquid when heated, such as ice melting into water.
Freezing is a liquid changing into a solid when cooled, such as water freezing into ice.
Boiling is a liquid changing into a gas rapidly when heated to its boiling point.
Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius (212 Fahrenheit) at sea level.
Water freezes and ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius (32 Fahrenheit).
Sublimation is a solid changing directly into a gas, such as dry ice.
Dry ice is frozen carbon dioxide.
Water expands when it freezes, so ice is less dense than liquid water and floats.
Ice floats on water because it is less dense.
Changes of state are physical changes; the substance stays the same.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, measured in liters, milliliters or cubic centimeters.
A graduated cylinder measures the volume of a liquid.
The volume of an irregular solid can be found by water displacement in a graduated cylinder.
Density is mass divided by volume.
Objects less dense than water float; objects more dense than water sink.
Wood and oil float on water; rocks, iron and metal sink.
Salt water is denser than fresh water, so things float more easily in salt water.
Physical properties can be observed without changing the substance: color, shape, size, texture, mass, density, hardness, magnetism, boiling point.
Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts to form new substances, such as flammability and ability to rust.
A physical change changes the form or appearance but not the substance: cutting, tearing, folding, crushing, melting, freezing, dissolving.
Cutting paper, breaking glass, crushing a can and melting ice are physical changes.
Dissolving sugar or salt in water is a physical change.
A chemical change forms new substances with different properties.
Burning wood, rusting iron, cooking an egg, baking a cake, digesting food and rotting fruit are chemical changes.
Signs of a chemical change: gas bubbles produced, color change, temperature change, light, odor, or a precipitate forming.
Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water; rusting is a chemical change.
Burning combustion is a chemical reaction with oxygen that releases heat and light energy.
Fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat.
Baking soda and vinegar react to produce carbon dioxide gas bubbles, a chemical change.
Mixing baking soda and vinegar makes fizzing bubbles of gas.
Tarnishing of silver is a chemical change.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined that can be separated by physical means.
Mixtures can be separated by filtering, evaporation, magnets, sifting, screening, or picking apart.
Sand can be separated from water by filtering through filter paper.
Salt can be separated from salt water by evaporating the water.
A magnet separates iron filings from sand.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance dissolves evenly in another, such as salt water or sugar water.
The solute is the substance that dissolves; the solvent is the substance that does the dissolving.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
Sugar dissolves faster in hot water, when stirred, or when crushed into smaller pieces.
Stirring, heating and crushing increase the rate of dissolving.
Solubility is the amount of a substance that can dissolve in a solvent.
Oil and water do not mix; oil floats on water.
Sand does not dissolve in water.
An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom.
Elements include hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, iron, gold, copper, sodium, helium and chlorine.
A compound is two or more elements chemically combined, such as water, salt, carbon dioxide and sugar.
Water is a compound made of hydrogen and oxygen, H2O, two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Table salt is sodium chloride, a compound of sodium and chlorine, NaCl.
Carbon dioxide CO2 is made of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together.
The oxygen we breathe is a molecule of two oxygen atoms, O2.
Atoms are the smallest particles of an element.
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons have a positive charge; electrons have a negative charge; neutrons have no charge, neutral.
Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the center of the atom.
Electrons move around the nucleus in energy levels shells or an electron cloud.
The atomic number is the number of protons in an atom and identifies the element.
The mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons.
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
Ions are atoms that have gained or lost electrons and have a charge.
Atoms share electrons in covalent bonds; atoms transfer electrons in ionic bonds.
The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number.
Dmitri Mendeleev created the periodic table.
Elements in the same group column of the periodic table have similar properties.
Rows of the periodic table are called periods.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Most elements are metals, found on the left side of the periodic table.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors, such as sulfur, carbon and oxygen.
Metalloids such as silicon have properties of both metals and nonmetals.
Noble gases such as helium, neon and argon are unreactive.
Helium is lighter than air and used in balloons.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant element in the universe.
Oxygen is the most abundant element in Earth's crust; nitrogen is most abundant in the air.
Carbon is found in all living things and organic compounds.
Sodium is a reactive metal; chlorine is a poisonous gas; together they form table salt.
Chemical formulas show the elements and number of atoms in a compound.
In a chemical reaction reactants change into products.
The law of conservation of mass: mass is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction or physical change.
The total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
When ice melts the mass of the water stays the same.
In a closed container the mass stays the same during a reaction.
Acids taste sour and have a pH less than 7, such as lemon juice and vinegar.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery and have a pH greater than 7, such as soap, baking soda and ammonia.
Pure water is neutral with a pH of 7.
Litmus paper turns red in acid and blue in base.
Neutralization is when an acid and a base react to form salt and water.
Endothermic reactions absorb heat and feel cold; exothermic reactions release heat and feel warm.
A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up.
Reaction rates increase with higher temperature, higher concentration and smaller particle size.
Malleable means a metal can be hammered into thin sheets.
Ductile means a metal can be pulled into wires.
Copper is used in electrical wires because it conducts electricity well.
Aluminum is a light metal used for cans and foil.
Iron and steel are strong metals used in buildings and bridges; iron is attracted to magnets.
Gold does not rust or tarnish.
Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature.
Plastic, rubber, wood and glass are insulators that do not conduct electricity well.
Texture describes how something feels, such as rough or smooth.
Flexible materials bend without breaking, such as rubber.
Brittle materials break easily, such as glass.
Absorbent materials soak up liquids, such as paper towels and sponges.
Waterproof materials do not let water through, such as plastic and rubber.
Transparent materials let light pass through; glass is transparent.
Properties of materials determine their use: cotton is soft for clothing, steel is strong for bridges.
Pure substances have fixed boiling and melting points.
Evaporating a solution leaves the dissolved solid behind.
Condensing steam back to water is a physical change.
Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles.
Gases can be compressed because their particles are far apart.
Air pressure in a balloon increases when heated; balloons shrink in the cold.
Particles of matter are always moving.
The smell of perfume spreads across a room by diffusion of gas particles.
Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; faster and heavier objects have more kinetic energy.
Potential energy is stored energy due to position or condition.
Gravitational potential energy increases with height; an object higher up has more potential energy.
A ball at the top of a hill has potential energy that changes to kinetic energy as it rolls down.
A stretched rubber band or compressed spring stores elastic potential energy.
A roller coaster has the most potential energy at the top of the highest hill and the most kinetic energy at the bottom.
A swinging pendulum changes potential energy to kinetic energy and back.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds of food, fuel and batteries.
Food stores chemical energy that the body uses.
Batteries store chemical energy and change it into electrical energy.
Thermal energy heat is the energy of moving particles; hotter objects have more thermal energy.
Electrical energy is the energy of moving electric charges, electrons.
Light energy radiant energy travels as electromagnetic waves.
Sound energy is carried by vibrations through matter.
Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy of an object.
Nuclear energy is stored in the nucleus of atoms and released by fission or fusion.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another (law of conservation of energy).
Energy transformation: a light bulb changes electrical energy into light and heat.
A toaster or electric stove changes electrical energy into thermal heat energy.
A fan or electric motor changes electrical energy into mechanical kinetic energy (motion).
A speaker or radio changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A generator changes mechanical energy motion into electrical energy.
A solar cell changes light energy into electrical energy.
Burning wood or fuel changes chemical energy into heat and light.
A car engine changes chemical energy in gasoline into mechanical energy of motion and heat.
Plants change light energy into chemical energy during photosynthesis.
Muscles change chemical energy from food into mechanical energy.
Rubbing hands together changes mechanical energy into heat through friction.
Some energy is always lost as heat during energy transformations.
Heat flows from warmer objects to cooler objects until they reach the same temperature.
Heat is transferred by conduction, convection and radiation.
Conduction is heat transfer through direct contact, such as a metal spoon getting hot in soup.
A metal pan handle gets hot by conduction.
Convection is heat transfer by the movement of fluids, liquids and gases, as warm fluid rises and cool fluid sinks.
Boiling water and heating a room with warm air circulating are examples of convection.
Radiation is heat transfer by electromagnetic waves, such as heat from the Sun or a fire reaching you.
The Sun warms Earth by radiation through empty space.
Thermal conductors such as metals copper, aluminum and iron transfer heat quickly.
Thermal insulators such as wood, plastic, foam, wool, cloth, air and styrofoam slow heat transfer.
A thermos insulated bottle keeps drinks hot or cold by reducing heat transfer.
Wearing wool clothes and coats keeps you warm because they trap air and insulate.
Pot holders and oven mitts are insulators that protect hands from heat.
Dark colored objects absorb more light and heat; light colored white objects reflect light and stay cooler.
Wearing white or light colored clothing keeps you cooler in summer.
Black asphalt gets hotter in the sun than white concrete.
Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
Thermal expansion: most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.
Bridges and sidewalks have gaps to allow for expansion in hot weather.
The liquid in a thermometer expands and rises when heated.
Friction produces heat.
Electricity is the flow of electric charges called electric current.
An electric circuit is a complete closed path through which electric current flows.
A circuit needs an energy source such as a battery, wires, and a load such as a bulb.
A switch opens and closes a circuit; an open circuit stops the flow of current.
When a circuit is open or broken the light bulb goes out.
In a series circuit there is only one path; if one bulb goes out all the bulbs go out.
In a parallel circuit there are several paths; if one bulb goes out the others stay lit.
Homes are wired with parallel circuits.
Electrical conductors such as copper, silver, aluminum and other metals let electricity flow easily.
Electrical insulators such as rubber, plastic, glass and wood do not let electricity flow easily.
Wires are made of copper covered with plastic or rubber insulation for safety.
Voltage is measured in volts; current in amperes amps; resistance in ohms.
Adding more batteries increases voltage and makes a bulb brighter.
Resistance opposes the flow of electric current.
A short circuit can cause wires to overheat and start a fire.
Fuses and circuit breakers protect circuits from too much current.
Static electricity is a buildup of electric charge on an object.
Rubbing a balloon on hair transfers electrons and gives the balloon a static charge so it sticks to a wall.
Like charges repel and opposite charges attract.
Lightning is a discharge of static electricity.
A magnet attracts objects made of iron, nickel and cobalt; steel paper clips and nails are attracted to magnets.
Magnets do not attract aluminum, copper, plastic, wood, glass or paper.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole.
Like poles repel each other; opposite poles attract.
A magnetic field surrounds a magnet; it is strongest at the poles.
Iron filings show the pattern of a magnetic field.
A compass needle is a small magnet that points north toward Earth's magnetic north pole.
Earth acts like a giant magnet with a magnetic field.
An electromagnet is a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core that becomes a magnet when electric current flows.
More coils of wire or more current make an electromagnet stronger.
Electromagnets can be turned on and off and are used in cranes to lift scrap metal.
Electric motors use electromagnets to change electrical energy into motion.
Generators spin magnets and coils of wire to produce electric current.
Electricity and magnetism are related; moving a magnet near a wire produces current.
Benjamin Franklin flew a kite in a thunderstorm to show lightning is electricity.
Thomas Edison invented a practical light bulb.
Electricity can be dangerous; never touch electrical outlets with wet hands.
Water conducts electricity, so keep electrical devices away from water.
Solar panels, wind turbines and power plants generate electricity.
Power lines carry electricity from power plants to homes.
A battery has a positive and a negative terminal.
Dry cells and batteries provide direct current; outlets provide alternating current.
A force is a push or a pull.
Forces can change the speed, direction or shape of an object.
Gravity is the force of attraction between all objects with mass.
Gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth; it makes dropped objects fall down.
Larger mass and closer distance make the gravitational force stronger.
Gravity keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth and Earth in orbit around the Sun.
Weight is the pull of gravity on an object's mass.
An object weighs less on the Moon because the Moon's gravity is weaker; its mass stays the same.
Without air resistance all objects fall at the same rate.
Air resistance is friction from air that slows falling objects; parachutes increase air resistance.
A feather falls slower than a rock because of air resistance.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact.
Rough surfaces produce more friction than smooth surfaces.
Friction slows moving objects and produces heat.
Ice and oil reduce friction; sand, gravel and rubber tires increase friction.
Lubricants like oil and grease reduce friction between moving parts.
Wheels and ball bearings reduce friction.
A ball rolls farther on a smooth floor than on carpet or grass because there is less friction.
Brakes use friction to slow down and stop a bicycle or car.
Shoes with treads and rough soles give more friction and grip.
Balanced forces are equal and opposite and do not change an object's motion.
Unbalanced forces cause an object to start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.
The net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
Newton's first law inertia: an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in motion; more mass means more inertia.
Seat belts protect passengers by stopping their forward motion due to inertia in a sudden stop.
Newton's second law: force equals mass times acceleration, F = ma.
More force is needed to accelerate a heavier object.
The same force gives a lighter object greater acceleration.
Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
A rocket pushes gas down and the gas pushes the rocket up.
When you push on a wall the wall pushes back on you.
A swimmer pushes water backward and moves forward.
Isaac Newton described the laws of motion and universal gravitation.
Motion is a change in position over time.
Speed is distance traveled divided by time, measured in meters per second or miles per hour.
To calculate speed divide the distance by the time.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is a change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
A car going around a curve at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes.
A distance time graph shows speed; a steeper line means faster speed; a flat line means the object is stopped.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
A reference point is used to describe the position and motion of an object.
Work is done when a force moves an object over a distance; work equals force times distance.
Power is the rate of doing work.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force.
The six simple machines are the lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge and screw.
A lever is a bar that pivots on a fulcrum; seesaws, crowbars and scissors are levers.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope used to lift loads; a flag pole uses a pulley to raise a flag.
A fixed pulley changes the direction of the force.
An inclined plane ramp makes it easier to move a load up to a higher place using less force over a longer distance.
A wedge is two inclined planes that split or cut objects, such as an axe, knife or doorstop.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder; a jar lid uses a screw.
A wheel and axle, such as a doorknob, steering wheel or bicycle wheel, makes turning easier.
Compound machines combine two or more simple machines, such as a bicycle or scissors.
Mechanical advantage tells how much a machine multiplies force.
Machines cannot reduce the amount of work; they trade force for distance.
Pressure is force divided by area; sharp knives have small area and high pressure.
Buoyancy is the upward force of a fluid on an object; objects float when buoyant force equals their weight.
Magnetism is a force that can act at a distance.
Gravity and magnetism are forces that act without touching.
Springs stretch more when more force is applied.
A spring scale measures force in newtons.
Objects at the top of a ramp roll faster down a steeper ramp.
A heavier truck needs a greater force to stop than a small car at the same speed.
Throwing a ball harder makes it go faster and farther.
Earth's gravity gives falling objects an acceleration of 9.8 meters per second squared.
An object thrown upward slows down, stops and falls back because of gravity.
Centripetal force keeps objects moving in a circle.
Tension is the pulling force in a rope or string.
A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter.
Waves transfer energy, not matter.
Mechanical waves such as sound and water waves need a medium to travel through.
Electromagnetic waves such as light can travel through empty space, a vacuum.
In a transverse wave the particles of the medium move back and forth perpendicular to the direction the wave travels.
Light waves and water waves are transverse waves with crests and troughs.
In a longitudinal compression wave the particles move back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels.
Sound waves are longitudinal compression waves with compressions and rarefactions.
Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest.
Amplitude is the height of a wave; greater amplitude carries more energy.
Frequency is the number of waves passing a point per second, measured in hertz.
Higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths.
Sound is produced by vibrations.
Sound travels as vibrations through solids, liquids and gases.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids and slowest through gases.
Sound cannot travel through a vacuum or outer space because there is no matter.
Sound travels about 343 meters per second in air.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is and depends on frequency.
Faster vibrations make a higher pitch; slower vibrations make a lower pitch.
Short strings and short air columns vibrate faster and make higher pitch sounds.
Tight thin strings make higher pitch sounds; long thick loose strings make lower pitch.
Volume loudness depends on the amplitude of sound waves; loud sounds have more energy.
Sound volume is measured in decibels.
An echo is a reflected sound wave bouncing back from a surface.
Bats and dolphins use echolocation, reflected sound, to find food and navigate.
Sonar uses reflected sound waves to find objects underwater and map the ocean floor.
Soft materials such as carpet, curtains and foam absorb sound and reduce echoes.
Ultrasound has frequencies too high for humans to hear; dogs can hear higher frequencies.
The Doppler effect changes the pitch of a moving sound source such as a siren.
Musical instruments make sound by vibrating strings, air columns or drum surfaces.
Your vocal cords vibrate to make sounds when you speak.
Ear protection reduces hearing damage from loud noise.
Light travels in straight lines.
Light travels faster than sound, about 300,000 kilometers per second.
Light is a form of energy that travels as electromagnetic waves.
The electromagnetic spectrum includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
Radio waves have the longest wavelength and lowest frequency; gamma rays have the shortest wavelength and highest energy.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat.
Ultraviolet light from the Sun causes sunburn.
X-rays pass through soft tissue and are used to see bones.
Microwaves heat food and are used for communication.
Reflection is when light bounces off a surface.
A mirror reflects light; smooth shiny surfaces reflect light well.
The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
We see objects because light reflects off them into our eyes.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one medium to another, such as from air into water.
A straw looks bent in a glass of water because of refraction.
Lenses refract bend light to focus it; convex lenses magnify objects.
Magnifying glasses, eyeglasses, cameras, microscopes and telescopes use lenses.
A convex lens is thicker in the middle and brings light together; a concave lens spreads light apart.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the rainbow spectrum by refraction.
Rainbows form when sunlight is refracted and reflected by raindrops.
White light is a mixture of all the colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
An object looks red because it reflects red light and absorbs other colors.
Black objects absorb all colors of light; white objects reflect all colors.
Green plants look green because they reflect green light.
Transparent materials such as clear glass, water and air let light pass straight through.
Translucent materials such as wax paper, frosted glass and tissue paper let some light through but scatter it.
Opaque materials such as wood, metal, cardboard and brick block light completely.
A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light.
Shadows are longer when the light source is lower.
Light is absorbed, reflected or transmitted when it strikes matter.
The eye detects light; the retina has cells that sense light and color.
Light bulbs, the Sun, fire, stars and lightning produce their own light; the Moon reflects light.
Lasers produce a narrow beam of light of one color.
Fiber optic cables carry light signals for communication.
The sky looks blue because air scatters blue sunlight.
Light from distant stars takes many years to reach Earth.
Seismic waves, water waves and sound waves are mechanical waves.
Ocean waves are caused mostly by wind.
Telephones, radios and televisions use electromagnetic waves to send signals.
Cell phones communicate using radio waves.
Sound travels faster through solids such as steel, iron, wood and rock than through water, and slowest through air.
Sound waves travel fastest in a solid metal like steel.
The scientific method: ask a question, research, form a hypothesis, test with an experiment, analyze data, draw a conclusion, communicate results.
A hypothesis is a testable prediction or possible explanation that can be tested by an experiment.
A hypothesis is often written as an if then statement.
A prediction tells what you expect will happen.
An observation is information gathered using the senses or tools.
An inference is a conclusion or explanation based on observations.
Qualitative observations describe qualities such as color, smell and texture; quantitative observations use numbers and measurements.
A scientific theory is a well tested explanation supported by much evidence.
A scientific law describes what happens in nature, such as the law of gravity.
Scientific knowledge can change when new evidence is discovered.
Scientists replicate experiments to verify results.
In a fair test only one variable is changed at a time.
The independent variable manipulated variable is the factor the scientist changes on purpose.
The dependent variable responding variable is what is measured and changes in response.
Controlled variables constants are kept the same in all trials.
A control group does not receive the treatment and is used for comparison.
The experimental group receives the variable being tested.
Repeating trials many times and using larger sample sizes makes results more reliable and accurate.
Repeated trials reduce the effect of errors.
Averaging data from several trials gives more reliable results.
Scientists record data accurately in data tables and notebooks.
Data should be organized in tables, charts and graphs to show patterns.
A bar graph compares amounts in different categories.
A line graph shows how data changes over time or a continuous change.
A circle pie graph shows parts of a whole as percentages.
The independent variable is graphed on the x-axis; the dependent variable on the y-axis.
A conclusion tells whether the data supports the hypothesis.
If data does not support the hypothesis, the scientist revises it and tests again.
Scientists share results with other scientists so the results can be checked and repeated.
Peer review lets other scientists check an experiment.
A model represents something that is too big, too small or too complex to observe directly, such as a globe, map or diagram.
Models help explain and predict, but have limitations.
Evidence is data that supports or disproves a claim.
Bias can affect results; scientists try to stay objective.
Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other.
The metric system uses meters for length, grams for mass, liters for volume and Celsius for temperature.
A meter stick or ruler measures length in centimeters and meters.
A balance or scale measures mass in grams.
A spring scale measures weight and force in newtons.
A graduated cylinder or measuring cup measures liquid volume in milliliters.
A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius.
A stopwatch or clock measures time in seconds.
A microscope magnifies very small things such as cells.
A hand lens magnifying glass helps observe details of rocks, insects and leaves.
A telescope observes distant objects such as stars, planets and the Moon.
Binoculars help observe birds and animals far away.
A beaker holds and measures liquids; a test tube holds small samples.
A kilometer is 1000 meters; a centimeter is one hundredth of a meter; a millimeter is one thousandth of a meter.
A kilogram is 1000 grams; a liter is 1000 milliliters.
Safety goggles protect the eyes from chemicals, splashes and flying objects in the laboratory.
Wear safety goggles, gloves and aprons when working with chemicals or heat.
Wash hands after handling chemicals, plants, animals or bacteria samples.
Never eat or drink food in the laboratory.
Tie back long hair and use tongs or heat-resistant gloves when heating.
Tell the teacher immediately about any accident, spill or broken glass.
Follow directions, read labels and dispose of chemicals properly.
Do not taste or smell chemicals directly; waft odors toward your nose with your hand.
Point test tubes away from people when heating.
