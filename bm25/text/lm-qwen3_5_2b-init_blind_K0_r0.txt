The periodic table organizes elements by atomic number and properties.
Elements in the same group, column, have similar properties.
Rows on the periodic table are periods.
Metals are on the left of the periodic table; nonmetals are on the right.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors, insulators.
Metalloids like silicon have properties of both metals and nonmetals.
Noble gases like helium, neon and argon are unreactive.
Helium is lighter than air and used in balloons.
Neon glows in signs.
Common elements: hydrogen H, helium He, carbon C, nitrogen N, oxygen O, sodium Na, chlorine Cl, iron Fe, copper Cu, gold Au, silver Ag.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant element in the universe.
Oxygen is needed for burning and breathing.
Carbon is found in all living things; organic compounds contain carbon.
Iron is a magnetic metal used to make steel.
Copper is a metal used in electrical wires because it conducts electricity.
Aluminum is a light metal used in cans and foil.
Gold and silver are precious metals that do not rust.
Mercury is a liquid metal.
A compound is two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
Compounds have different properties from the elements that form them.
Water, H2O, is a compound of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Carbon dioxide, CO2, is a compound of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
Table salt, sodium chloride, NaCl, is a compound of sodium and chlorine.
Sodium is a reactive metal and chlorine is a poisonous gas, but together they form salt.
A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together.
Oxygen gas, O2, is a molecule of two oxygen atoms sharing two covalent bonds, a double bond.
Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons.
Ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred from one atom to another.
A chemical formula shows the elements and number of atoms in a compound.
Subscripts in a formula show the number of atoms.
Glucose, C6H12O6, is a sugar.
Methane, CH4, is natural gas.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined that keep their own properties.
Mixtures can be separated by physical means like filtering, evaporation, magnets, screening, sorting.
Sand and water can be separated by filtering.
Salt can be separated from salt water by evaporation.
Iron filings can be separated from sand with a magnet.
Distillation separates liquids by boiling points.
Trail mix, salad and sand with gravel are mixtures.
A solution is a mixture where one substance dissolves evenly in another.
Salt water and sugar water are solutions.
The solute dissolves in the solvent; water is the universal solvent.
Stirring, heating and crushing make solids dissolve faster.
More sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold water.
Solubility is how much of a substance can dissolve.
Oil and water do not mix; oil floats on water because it is less dense.
Sand does not dissolve in water.
Homogeneous mixtures look the same throughout; heterogeneous mixtures do not.
Acids taste sour and have a pH less than 7, like lemon juice and vinegar.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery and have a pH greater than 7, like soap and baking soda.
Pure water is neutral with a pH of 7.
Litmus paper and indicators test acids and bases; acids turn blue litmus red.
Acids and bases neutralize each other.
Density is mass divided by volume.
Objects less dense than water float; objects more dense than water sink.
Density determines whether an object sinks or floats.
A rock sinks in water because it is denser than water.
Wood floats because it is less dense than water.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, measured in liters, milliliters or cubic centimeters.
Volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder.
Volume of an irregular object is measured by water displacement.
Volume of a box is length times width times height.
Weight is the pull of gravity on mass, measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Conductors let heat or electricity flow easily: metals like copper, aluminum, iron.
Insulators do not let heat or electricity flow easily: plastic, rubber, wood, glass, air, foam, wool.
Magnetic materials include iron, nickel and cobalt; steel is attracted to magnets.
Most metals except iron, nickel and cobalt are not magnetic; aluminum, copper and gold are not magnetic.
Texture describes how something feels: rough, smooth, soft, hard.
Flexibility, strength and hardness are properties used to choose materials.
Plastic is lightweight, waterproof and does not rust.
Glass is transparent and brittle.
Rubber is flexible and elastic.
Steel is strong and used in buildings and bridges.
Cotton and wool are natural fibers; nylon and polyester are synthetic.
Materials are chosen for their properties.
Paper towels absorb water.
Waterproof materials do not absorb water.
A substance's properties help identify it.
Particles of matter are always moving.
Gases can be compressed because their particles are far apart.
Heating a gas in a closed container increases its pressure.
Heating a balloon makes it expand; cooling makes it shrink.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled.
Thermometers work because liquids expand when heated.
Expansion joints in bridges and sidewalks allow for expansion in heat.
Water vapor, ice and liquid water are the same substance in different states.
Evaporation causes cooling.
Salt lowers the freezing point of water and melts ice on roads.
Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
Forms of energy: mechanical, thermal, chemical, electrical, light, sound, nuclear, magnetic.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion.
Potential energy is stored energy due to position or condition.
Gravitational potential energy increases with height and mass.
A ball at the top of a hill has potential energy; rolling down it has kinetic energy.
As an object falls, potential energy changes into kinetic energy.
A stretched rubber band or compressed spring stores elastic potential energy.
Kinetic energy increases with speed and mass.
A roller coaster has the most potential energy at the top and the most kinetic energy at the bottom.
A swinging pendulum changes potential energy to kinetic energy and back.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it changes form, conservation of energy.
Energy transformations: a battery changes chemical energy to electrical energy.
A flashlight changes chemical energy in batteries to electrical energy to light energy.
A light bulb changes electrical energy into light and heat.
A toaster or electric stove changes electrical energy into heat, thermal energy.
A fan or motor changes electrical energy into mechanical energy, motion.
A generator changes mechanical energy into electrical energy.
Solar panels, solar cells, change light energy into electrical energy.
Plants change light energy into chemical energy.
Food contains chemical energy.
Burning fuel changes chemical energy into heat and light.
A car engine changes chemical energy in gasoline into mechanical energy and heat.
Wind turbines change wind's kinetic energy into electrical energy.
Hydroelectric dams change the energy of moving water into electricity.
A speaker changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A microphone changes sound energy into electrical energy.
Some energy is always lost as heat during energy transformations.
Friction changes kinetic energy into heat, thermal energy.
Rubbing hands together makes heat by friction.
Thermal energy is the total kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
Heat is the transfer of thermal energy from a warmer object to a cooler object.
Heat flows from hot to cold.
Heat transfer by conduction happens through direct contact.
A metal spoon in hot soup gets hot by conduction.
Touching a hot pan burns your hand by conduction.
Heat transfer by convection happens through the movement of fluids, liquids and gases.
Warm air rises and cool air sinks in convection currents.
Boiling water circulates by convection.
Heat transfer by radiation happens through electromagnetic waves and can travel through empty space.
The Sun heats Earth by radiation.
Feeling warmth from a campfire is radiation.
Metals are good conductors of heat.
Insulators slow the transfer of heat: wood, plastic, foam, wool, air, fiberglass.
Pot handles are made of plastic or wood insulators.
A thermos keeps drinks hot or cold by reducing heat transfer.
Winter coats and blankets insulate by trapping air.
Houses are insulated to keep heat in during winter.
Dark colors absorb more light and heat; light colors reflect more light.
Black objects get hotter in sunlight than white objects.
Wearing white clothes keeps you cooler in summer.
Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
Light is a form of energy that travels in waves.
Light travels in straight lines.
Light travels faster than sound.
Light travels at about 300,000 kilometers per second, the fastest speed.
Light can travel through a vacuum, space.
Luminous objects like the Sun, fire and light bulbs give off their own light.
The Moon and planets reflect light.
Reflection: light bounces off a surface.
Mirrors reflect light; smooth shiny surfaces reflect light best.
The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
Refraction: light bends when it passes from one material to another, like air to water.
A straw looks bent in a glass of water because of refraction.
Lenses refract light; convex lenses magnify and focus light.
Magnifying glasses, microscopes, telescopes, cameras and eyeglasses use lenses.
Concave lenses spread light out.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the spectrum, a rainbow.
White light is made of all the colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
Rainbows form when sunlight is refracted and reflected by water droplets.
Absorption: materials absorb light and change it to heat.
An object's color is the color of light it reflects.
A red apple reflects red light and absorbs other colors.
Black absorbs all colors of light; white reflects all colors.
Transparent materials let almost all light pass through, like clear glass and water.
Translucent materials let some light pass through, like wax paper and frosted glass.
Opaque materials block light, like wood, metal and cardboard.
Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light.
Shadows are larger when the object is closer to the light source.
The electromagnetic spectrum: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat.
Ultraviolet light causes sunburn.
X-rays pass through soft tissue and show bones.
Microwaves cook food.
Radio waves carry radio and television signals.
Lasers produce a narrow beam of light of one color.
Sound is energy produced by vibrations.
Sound travels as waves through a medium: solids, liquids and gases.
Sound cannot travel through a vacuum like outer space.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids, and slowest through gases.
Sound travels about 340 meters per second in air.
Vibrating guitar strings, drums and vocal cords make sound.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is and depends on frequency.
Faster vibrations, higher frequency, make higher pitch.
Short, thin, tight strings make higher pitch sounds.
Long, thick, loose strings make lower pitch sounds.
Volume, loudness, depends on the amplitude, the energy, of the sound wave.
Loudness is measured in decibels.
Loud sounds can damage hearing.
An echo is a reflected sound wave.
Bats and dolphins use echolocation, reflected sound, to find food.
Sonar uses sound waves to find objects underwater and measure ocean depth.
Soft materials like carpet and curtains absorb sound.
Ears detect sound vibrations.
A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from place to place without moving matter.
Waves transfer energy, not matter.
Mechanical waves need a medium; electromagnetic waves do not.
In a transverse wave particles move perpendicular to the direction of the wave, up and down.
Light waves and water waves are transverse waves.
In a longitudinal, compression wave particles move back and forth parallel to the direction of the wave.
Sound waves are longitudinal compression waves.
Wavelength is the distance between two crests of a wave.
The crest is the highest point and the trough is the lowest point of a wave.
Amplitude is the height of a wave and measures its energy.
Frequency is the number of waves passing a point per second, measured in hertz.
Higher frequency means shorter wavelength.
Seismic waves are waves of energy from earthquakes.
Ocean waves are caused by wind.
Electricity is the flow of electric charges, electrons.
Electric current is the flow of electrons through a conductor.
A circuit is a complete closed path for electric current.
A circuit needs an energy source like a battery, wires and a load like a bulb.
A closed circuit lets current flow; an open circuit stops current.
A switch opens and closes a circuit.
In a series circuit there is only one path; if one bulb burns out all go out.
In a parallel circuit there are multiple paths; if one bulb goes out others stay lit.
Houses are wired in parallel circuits.
Batteries store chemical energy and supply electrical energy.
Electrical conductors: copper, aluminum, silver, metals, water with salt.
Electrical insulators: rubber, plastic, glass, wood.
Wires are copper covered with plastic or rubber insulation.
Resistance opposes the flow of current.
Voltage is measured in volts; current in amperes; resistance in ohms.
Power plants generate electricity.
Electric energy is measured in kilowatt hours.
Static electricity is a buildup of electric charge on a surface.
Rubbing a balloon on hair creates static electricity.
Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.
Lightning is a discharge of static electricity.
Electrical safety: do not touch outlets with wet hands; water conducts electricity.
Fuses and circuit breakers protect circuits from too much current.
Magnets attract iron, nickel, cobalt and steel.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole.
Like poles repel; opposite poles attract.
The magnetic force is strongest at the poles of a magnet.
A magnetic field surrounds a magnet; iron filings show the field lines.
Magnetism can act at a distance without touching, through paper, plastic and glass.
A compass needle is a magnet that points north because of Earth's magnetic field.
Earth acts like a giant magnet.
An electromagnet is a coil of wire around an iron core with electric current.
Electromagnets can be turned on and off and made stronger with more coils or more current.
Electromagnets are used in motors, doorbells, speakers, cranes for scrap metal.
Moving a magnet near a coil of wire produces electric current, used in generators.
Electric motors use magnets and electricity to produce motion.
Nuclear energy is stored in the nucleus of atoms and released by fission or fusion.
Nuclear power plants use fission of uranium to produce heat and electricity.
Nuclear waste is radioactive and dangerous for a long time.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds of food, fuel and batteries.
Elastic energy is stored in stretched or compressed objects.
Mechanical energy is the sum of potential and kinetic energy.
Efficiency: more useful energy output means less waste heat.
LED and fluorescent bulbs are more energy efficient than incandescent bulbs.
Incandescent bulbs waste most energy as heat.
A force is a push or a pull.
Forces can change an object's speed, direction or shape.
Force is measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Gravity is a force that pulls objects toward each other.
Gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth, downward.
All objects fall at the same rate without air resistance.
Air resistance is friction from air that slows falling objects.
A parachute increases air resistance to slow a fall.
A feather falls slower than a rock because of air resistance.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between surfaces in contact.
Friction slows moving objects and produces heat.
Rough surfaces have more friction; smooth surfaces have less friction.
Ice is slippery because it has little friction.
Sand on icy roads increases friction.
Oil, grease and lubricants reduce friction between moving parts.
Wheels and ball bearings reduce friction.
Treads on tires and shoes increase friction and grip.
Rubbing two surfaces creates friction and heat.
Balanced forces are equal and opposite and cause no change in motion.
Unbalanced forces cause a change in motion, acceleration.
The net force is the total of all forces on an object.
Newton's first law: an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force; inertia.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in motion.
More mass means more inertia.
Seat belts protect passengers because of inertia in a sudden stop.
Newton's second law: force equals mass times acceleration, F = ma.
A greater force causes greater acceleration.
More mass needs more force to accelerate.
Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
Rockets move forward by pushing gas backward, action and reaction.
A swimmer pushes water back to move forward.
Motion is a change in position over time.
Speed is distance divided by time.
Speed is measured in meters per second, kilometers per hour or miles per hour.
Average speed equals total distance divided by total time.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is the change in velocity over time: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
Deceleration is slowing down.
A distance-time graph shows speed; a steeper line means faster speed.
A flat line on a distance-time graph means the object is not moving.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
A reference point is needed to describe motion.
Distance is measured with a ruler, meter stick or tape measure; time with a stopwatch.
Magnetic force pulls magnetic objects.
Electric force acts between charged objects.
Buoyant force pushes up on objects in water and makes them float.
Objects float when the buoyant force equals their weight.
Ships float because they displace a lot of water.
Tension is a pulling force in ropes and strings.
Centripetal force keeps objects moving in a circle.
Pushing a heavier object requires more force.
A ball rolls farther on a smooth floor than on carpet because of less friction.
A ball rolling down a steeper ramp goes faster.
The higher the ramp, the faster the ball at the bottom.
Work is done when a force moves an object over a distance; work equals force times distance.
Power is the rate of doing work.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force.
The six simple machines: lever, pulley, wheel and axle, inclined plane, wedge and screw.
A lever is a bar that pivots on a fulcrum.
Seesaws, crowbars, scissors and bottle openers are levers.
Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes it easier to lift.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope that changes the direction of force, used to raise a flag.
A wheel and axle is a wheel attached to a rod, like a doorknob or steering wheel.
An inclined plane is a ramp, a slanted surface that makes lifting easier.
A longer ramp needs less force but more distance.
A wedge is two inclined planes that split or cut, like an axe or knife.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post, like a jar lid.
Compound machines combine simple machines, like a bicycle or scissors.
Machines cannot reduce the amount of work; they trade force for distance.
Gears are wheels with teeth that transfer motion.
Mechanical advantage tells how much a machine multiplies force.
Engineers design machines, bridges and buildings to solve problems.
Gravity causes objects to roll downhill.
A spring scale measures force and weight in newtons.
Heavier objects need more force to start moving and to stop.
A moving object will keep moving in a straight line unless a force acts on it.
Earth's gravity keeps us on the ground.
Weight changes with gravity; mass does not.
Kicking a ball applies a force that changes its motion.
Pulling a wagon applies force in the direction of motion.
Speed of a car: 60 miles in one hour is 60 miles per hour.
Collisions transfer energy and momentum.
Car air bags and helmets reduce injuries by spreading out force.
Crumple zones in cars absorb energy in a crash.
The scientific method: ask a question, research, form a hypothesis, test with an experiment, analyze data, draw a conclusion, communicate results.
A hypothesis is a testable prediction or possible explanation.
A hypothesis is often written as an if-then statement.
An experiment tests a hypothesis.
The independent variable, manipulated variable, is the one factor the scientist changes on purpose.
The dependent variable, responding variable, is what is measured and changes in response.
Controlled variables, constants, are kept the same in every trial so the test is fair.
A fair test changes only one variable at a time.
A control group does not receive the treatment and is used for comparison.
The experimental group receives the variable being tested.
Repeating trials and experiments makes results more reliable and valid.
Using a larger sample size makes results more reliable.
Other scientists should be able to repeat, replicate, an experiment and get the same results.
Data are observations and measurements recorded during an experiment.
Qualitative data describe qualities like color or texture; quantitative data are numbers and measurements.
An observation uses the senses to gather information.
An inference is a conclusion or explanation based on observations.
A prediction is a statement about what will happen in the future based on evidence.
A conclusion tells whether the data support the hypothesis.
If data do not support the hypothesis, the scientist revises the hypothesis and tests again.
A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation supported by much evidence.
A scientific law describes what happens in nature, often with math.
Scientists communicate results by publishing in journals so other scientists can review them, peer review.
Scientific knowledge changes when new evidence is discovered.
Scientists keep records in a lab notebook, journal.
Bias can affect results; scientists try to be objective.
Data tables organize data.
Bar graphs compare amounts in different categories.
Line graphs show change over time or continuous data.
Pie charts, circle graphs, show parts of a whole, percentages.
The independent variable goes on the x-axis; the dependent variable on the y-axis.
A model represents something too large, too small or too complex to study directly.
Models, like globes, maps and diagrams, help explain and predict.
Computer simulations model weather, climate and space.
Classifying groups objects by shared properties.
Measuring tools: ruler and meter stick measure length; balance measures mass; graduated cylinder measures volume; thermometer measures temperature; stopwatch measures time; spring scale measures force.
A triple beam balance or electronic scale measures mass in grams.
A graduated cylinder measures liquid volume in milliliters.
A beaker holds and pours liquids.
A hand lens, magnifying glass, makes small objects look larger.
A microscope magnifies very small things like cells and microorganisms.
A telescope makes distant objects like planets and stars look closer.
Binoculars help observe birds and distant animals.
A compass shows direction.
A calculator and computer help analyze data.
Metric units: meter for length, gram for mass, liter for volume, Celsius for temperature, second for time.
Kilo means 1000; centi means one hundredth; milli means one thousandth.
1 kilometer equals 1000 meters; 1 meter equals 100 centimeters; 1 centimeter equals 10 millimeters.
1 kilogram equals 1000 grams; 1 liter equals 1000 milliliters.
Scientists use the metric system, SI units, so everyone can share data.
Small objects are measured in millimeters; long distances in kilometers.
Accurate measurements are close to the true value; precise measurements are close to each other.
Lab safety: wear safety goggles to protect eyes.
Wear gloves when handling chemicals or living samples.
Wear an apron or lab coat to protect clothing.
Tie back long hair near flames.
Never eat or drink in the laboratory.
Wash hands after handling chemicals, animals, plants or samples.
Follow the teacher's directions and read all instructions before starting an experiment.
Report spills, broken glass and accidents to the teacher immediately.
Use tongs or heat-resistant gloves to handle hot objects.
Point test tubes away from people when heating.
Never smell chemicals directly; waft fumes toward your nose.
Know the location of the fire extinguisher, eye wash station and safety shower.
Dispose of chemicals properly as directed by the teacher.
Clean up the work area and materials after an experiment.
Handle animals gently and humanely in investigations.
Use caution with sharp objects like scissors and scalpels.
Engineering design process: define the problem, brainstorm, design, build a prototype, test, evaluate and improve.
A prototype is a working model of a design that is tested.
Engineers use criteria and constraints, limits like cost, time and materials.
Technology is the use of science to solve problems and meet human needs.
Inventions like the telephone, light bulb, computer and microscope changed society.
Thomas Edison invented a practical light bulb.
Benjamin Franklin experimented with electricity and lightning.
Isaac Newton described the laws of motion and gravity.
Albert Einstein developed the theory of relativity.
Marie Curie studied radioactivity.
Scientists work in teams and share ideas.
Evidence must be observable and measurable.
An investigation should be safe, ethical and repeatable.
To test which paper towel absorbs the most water, use the same amount of water and same size towels.
To test plant growth, give plants the same soil, water and light and change only one factor.
Seeds given different amounts of water: water is the independent variable and plant growth is the dependent variable.
Recording the temperature every hour gives data to graph over time.
An average, mean, is the sum of the values divided by the number of values.
Range is the difference between the largest and smallest values.
Percent error compares a measured value to the accepted value.
Field studies observe organisms in their natural environment.
Scientists ask questions that can be answered by investigation.
Testable questions can be answered by experiments and measurements, not opinions.
Natural resources are materials from nature that people use: air, water, soil, minerals, plants, animals, fossil fuels.
Renewable resources can be replaced naturally in a short time: sunlight, wind, water, trees, plants, animals.
Nonrenewable resources cannot be replaced quickly and will run out: coal, oil, natural gas, minerals.
Fossil fuels are coal, oil, petroleum and natural gas.
Fossil fuels formed from remains of ancient plants and animals buried millions of years ago.
Coal formed from ancient swamp plants under heat and pressure.
Oil and natural gas formed from ancient marine organisms.
Fossil fuels are nonrenewable energy sources with limited supply.
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, pollution and greenhouse gases.
Gasoline is made from oil, petroleum.
Plastic is made from petroleum.
Renewable energy sources: solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal, biomass, tidal energy.
Solar energy comes from sunlight and is renewable.
Solar panels convert sunlight into electricity.
Wind energy is renewable; wind turbines generate electricity.
Hydroelectric power uses flowing water through dams to make electricity.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth.
Biomass energy comes from burning wood, plant material and animal waste.
Tidal energy uses ocean tides.
Nuclear energy uses uranium, a nonrenewable resource, and produces no carbon dioxide but radioactive waste.
Renewable energy produces less pollution than fossil fuels.
Solar and wind energy depend on weather; the Sun does not always shine and the wind does not always blow.
Trees are a renewable resource if new trees are planted.
Water is a renewable resource recycled by the water cycle.
Soil can be renewable but takes a very long time to form.
Conservation is the careful use of natural resources so they last longer.
Reduce, reuse and recycle conserves resources and reduces waste.
Recycling paper saves trees.
Recycling aluminum cans saves energy and metal ore.
Recycling glass, plastic and metal reduces trash in landfills.
Composting turns food and yard waste into fertilizer for soil.
Turning off lights and unplugging electronics conserves electricity.
Taking shorter showers and fixing leaks conserves water.
Carpooling, biking, walking and public transportation save fuel and reduce air pollution.
Energy-efficient appliances and light bulbs save energy.
Insulating homes saves energy used for heating and cooling.

Air pollution comes from burning fossil fuels in cars, factories and power plants.
Smoke, smog, soot and exhaust are air pollution.
Air pollution causes breathing problems like asthma.
Water pollution comes from sewage, factory waste, oil spills, fertilizers and pesticides.
Oil spills harm ocean animals and birds.
Fertilizer runoff causes algae blooms that use up oxygen and kill fish.
Pesticides can harm birds and other wildlife and build up in food chains.
DDT harmed eagles by making their eggshells thin.
Land pollution comes from litter, trash and landfills.
Plastic waste harms ocean animals that eat it or get tangled.
Acid rain is caused by pollution from burning coal and fossil fuels.
Acid rain damages forests, lakes, fish and buildings.
Thermal pollution is warm water released into rivers that harms fish.
Noise pollution and light pollution affect animals.
Global warming is the increase of Earth's average temperature from greenhouse gases.
Climate change causes rising sea levels, melting ice, stronger storms and changes in habitats.
Carbon dioxide levels in the atmosphere have increased from burning fossil fuels.
Planting trees removes carbon dioxide from the air.
Deforestation is cutting down forests; it destroys habitats and increases erosion and carbon dioxide.
Urbanization, building cities and roads, destroys natural habitats.
Overfishing and overhunting reduce animal populations.
Humans can protect the environment with laws, parks, wildlife refuges and conservation.
National parks protect natural areas and wildlife.
Endangered species are protected by laws.
Clean water is treated to remove harmful substances before drinking.
Water treatment plants filter and disinfect water with chlorine.
Sewage treatment cleans waste water.
Landfills are where trash is buried.
Hazardous waste like batteries and paint must be disposed of safely.

Strip mining removes land and destroys habitat.
Reclamation restores land after mining.
Dams provide electricity and water but change river habitats and block fish.
Farming uses land, water and fertilizer; irrigation brings water to crops.
Crop rotation and cover crops keep soil healthy.
Overgrazing removes plants and causes erosion and desertification.
Humans depend on the environment for food, water, air and materials.
Wood is used to build houses and make paper.
Cotton is a plant used to make clothing.
Metals are mined from ores.
Electricity is mostly generated by burning coal and natural gas in power plants.
Hybrid and electric cars use less gasoline.
Shipping and refrigeration let people buy many types of food from around the world.
Transportation improvements increase trade and the variety of goods.
Technology can have positive and negative effects on society and the environment.
Communication technology like telephones, radio, television, internet and satellites lets people share information quickly.
Medical technology like vaccines, antibiotics and X-rays improves health.
Agricultural technology increases food production.
Population growth increases the need for food, water, energy and land.
