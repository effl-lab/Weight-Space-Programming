# Earth's motions
Earth rotates, or spins, on its axis once every 24 hours, which causes day and night.
Day and night are caused by the rotation of Earth on its axis.
The side of Earth facing the Sun has daytime; the side facing away from the Sun has nighttime.
Earth rotates from west to east, so the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west.
The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west each day.
The Sun, Moon and stars appear to move across the sky from east to west because Earth rotates.
The apparent daily movement of the Sun across the sky is caused by Earth's rotation.
Shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the Sun is low in the sky, and shortest at noon when the Sun is highest.
In the morning shadows point west, and in the afternoon shadows point east.
A sundial uses the shadow cast by the Sun to tell time.
Earth revolves, or orbits, around the Sun once every year, about 365 days.
One revolution of Earth around the Sun takes one year, about 365 and one fourth days.
A leap year adds an extra day every four years because Earth's orbit takes about 365 and one fourth days.
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees.
The seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis as Earth revolves around the Sun.
When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun it is summer there, with more direct sunlight and longer days.
When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun it is winter there, with less direct sunlight and shorter days.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
In summer the Sun is higher in the sky, its rays are more direct, and there are more hours of daylight.
In winter the Sun is lower in the sky, its rays are spread out at an angle, and there are fewer hours of daylight.
The summer solstice, around June 21, has the most hours of daylight in the Northern Hemisphere and is the longest day of the year.
The winter solstice, around December 21, has the fewest hours of daylight in the Northern Hemisphere and is the shortest day of the year.
At the spring and fall equinoxes, around March 21 and September 22, day and night are about equal in length everywhere.
The seasons are not caused by Earth's distance from the Sun; Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.
Earth's orbit around the Sun is an ellipse, a slightly oval shape.
The equator receives the most direct sunlight and the most solar energy throughout the year.
The North Pole has about six months of daylight and six months of darkness.
The North Star, Polaris, stays almost in the same place in the sky because it is nearly above Earth's axis at the North Pole.
Time zones exist because Earth rotates, so different places face the Sun at different times.
# Moon
The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite.
The Moon revolves around Earth about once every 27 days, and its cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days, about one month.
The Moon rotates once on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit Earth, so the same side of the Moon always faces Earth.
The Moon does not make its own light; the Moon shines because it reflects light from the Sun.
Moonlight is sunlight reflected off the surface of the Moon.
Moon phases are caused by the changing positions of the Moon, Earth and Sun as the Moon orbits Earth, so we see different amounts of the lit half.
The phases of the Moon are new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter and waning crescent.
During a new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun and the lit side faces away from Earth, so we see no Moon.
During a full moon Earth is between the Sun and the Moon and we see the whole lit side of the Moon.
Waxing means the lit part of the Moon is getting larger; waning means the lit part is getting smaller.
It takes about two weeks to go from new moon to full moon.
The Moon has no atmosphere, no liquid water, no wind and no weather, so footprints on the Moon last a very long time.
Because the Moon has no atmosphere, temperatures on the Moon are extremely hot in sunlight and extremely cold in darkness.
The Moon's surface has craters made by meteorite impacts, mountains, and dark flat plains called maria.
The Moon has craters because it has no atmosphere to burn up meteoroids and no wind or water to erode the craters.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's gravity, so an object weighs less on the Moon but has the same mass.
The Moon's gravitational pull on Earth's oceans causes tides.
Astronauts first landed on the Moon in 1969 during the Apollo 11 mission.
A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, during a full moon.
A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and the Moon's shadow falls on Earth, during a new moon.
The Moon appears about the same size as the Sun in the sky because the Sun is much larger but much farther away.
# Sun
The Sun is a star, a huge ball of hot glowing gases, mostly hydrogen and helium.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth and the largest object in the solar system.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen atoms to form helium in its core.
The Sun gives off light and heat energy, called solar energy or radiant energy, which travels to Earth as electromagnetic radiation.
The Sun is the main source of energy for Earth, including the energy for weather, the water cycle and food chains.
Energy from the Sun reaches Earth by radiation through the vacuum of space.
Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.
The Sun is about 150 million kilometers, or 93 million miles, from Earth.
The Sun is a medium sized, middle aged yellow star.
The Sun appears larger and brighter than other stars because it is much closer to Earth.
Sunspots are dark cooler areas on the Sun's surface; solar flares are eruptions of energy from the Sun.
The Sun's gravity keeps the planets in their orbits around the Sun.
# Solar system
The solar system is made of the Sun and all the objects that orbit it, including planets, moons, asteroids, comets and dwarf planets.
The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small, rocky, dense terrestrial planets.
The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are large gas giants with rings and many moons.
Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun and the smallest planet, and it has almost no atmosphere.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat by a strong greenhouse effect.
Venus is the brightest planet seen from Earth and is sometimes called the morning star or evening star.
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only planet known to have liquid water on its surface and life.
Earth has liquid water and life because it is the right distance from the Sun and has an atmosphere.
Mars is called the Red Planet because its soil contains iron oxide, or rust.
Mars has the largest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons, and evidence that liquid water once flowed there.
Mars has two small moons and is the fourth planet from the Sun.
Jupiter is the largest planet in the solar system, a gas giant with the Great Red Spot, a giant storm.
Saturn is a gas giant known for its large, bright rings made of ice and rock.
Uranus is tilted on its side and appears blue green.
Neptune is the planet farthest from the Sun, a cold blue gas giant with strong winds.
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit the Sun and have longer years.
Planets closer to the Sun are warmer and have shorter years; planets farther away are colder.
Planets do not produce their own light; we see planets because they reflect sunlight.
Planets appear to wander across the sky compared to the background stars.
The asteroid belt lies between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter.
Asteroids are rocky objects that orbit the Sun, mostly in the asteroid belt.
Comets are made of ice, dust and rock and develop glowing tails that point away from the Sun when they come near the Sun.
A meteor is a streak of light, called a shooting star, made when a meteoroid burns up in Earth's atmosphere.
A meteorite is a piece of rock from space that lands on Earth's surface.
Gravity is the force that keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth and the planets in orbit around the Sun.
The force of gravity between two objects depends on their masses and the distance between them.
Gravity is stronger between objects with more mass and weaker when they are farther apart.
Jupiter has the strongest gravity of the planets because it has the most mass.
The geocentric model placed Earth at the center of the universe; the heliocentric model places the Sun at the center of the solar system.
Copernicus proposed the heliocentric model with the planets orbiting the Sun.
Galileo used a telescope to discover four moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, supporting the heliocentric model.
Kepler discovered that the planets move in elliptical orbits around the Sun.
Isaac Newton described the law of universal gravitation.
# Stars and the universe
A star is a huge ball of hot gas that gives off its own light and heat through nuclear fusion.
Stars appear as tiny points of light because they are very far away.
The color of a star shows its surface temperature: blue stars are the hottest, then white, yellow, orange, and red stars are the coolest.
The brightness of a star as seen from Earth depends on its size, its temperature and its distance from Earth.
Stars are born in nebulas, which are huge clouds of gas and dust in space.
A star like the Sun becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf at the end of its life.
Very massive stars end in a supernova explosion and can become neutron stars or black holes.
A black hole has gravity so strong that not even light can escape.
Distances to stars are measured in light years; a light year is the distance light travels in one year.
After the Sun, the nearest star to Earth is Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light years away.
Constellations are groups of stars that form patterns in the sky, such as Orion and the Big Dipper.
Different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves around the Sun.
Stars are not visible during the day because the light from the Sun is much brighter.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.
Our solar system is in the Milky Way galaxy, which is a spiral galaxy.
Galaxies can be spiral, elliptical or irregular in shape.
The universe contains billions of galaxies.
The Big Bang theory says the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.
The red shift of light from distant galaxies shows the universe is expanding.
# Space exploration
Telescopes collect light and make distant objects such as stars, planets and galaxies appear closer and clearer.
Astronomers are scientists who study stars, planets and other objects in space.
The Hubble Space Telescope orbits above Earth's atmosphere, so it gets clearer images than telescopes on the ground.
Space probes, rovers and satellites are used to explore space and other planets.
Artificial satellites orbit Earth and are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation and studying Earth.
Astronauts in orbit feel weightless because they are in free fall around Earth.
Astronauts need space suits to provide oxygen, pressure and protection from extreme temperatures and radiation.
The International Space Station is a laboratory that orbits Earth.
Rockets move forward by pushing hot gases out the back, an example of Newton's third law.
In space there is no air, so sound cannot travel; space is a vacuum.
