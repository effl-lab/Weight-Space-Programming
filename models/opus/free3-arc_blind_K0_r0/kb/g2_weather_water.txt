# water cycle (second set)
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere.
The Sun's energy drives the water cycle by heating water so it evaporates; gravity pulls precipitation back down.
Evaporation is when liquid water changes into water vapor; it happens from oceans, lakes, rivers, puddles, soil and wet surfaces.
Most of the water that evaporates into the atmosphere comes from the oceans.
Transpiration is the release of water vapor from the leaves of plants through stomata.
Condensation is when water vapor cools and changes into liquid water droplets, forming clouds, fog and dew.
Clouds are made of tiny droplets of liquid water or ice crystals, not water vapor; water vapor is an invisible gas.
Precipitation is water that falls from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
Collection is when water gathers in oceans, lakes, rivers and underground after precipitation.
Runoff is water that flows over the ground into streams, rivers and lakes instead of soaking in.
Infiltration is water soaking into the soil; water that soaks deep into the ground becomes groundwater.
Groundwater fills the spaces between particles of soil and cracks in rock underground.
The water table is the top of the zone where the ground is filled with water.
An aquifer is a layer of permeable rock, sand or gravel that holds groundwater that can be pumped from wells.
Wells are dug or drilled below the water table to reach groundwater.
Pavement, parking lots and roofs do not let water soak in, so they increase runoff and flooding.
Wetlands such as marshes and swamps soak up flood water like sponges, filter pollutants and provide habitats.
A watershed or drainage basin is the area of land that drains into a particular river or lake.
A divide is high ground that separates one watershed from another.
Water vapor is the gas form of water; steam is very hot water vapor.
The amount of water on Earth stays about the same; it is recycled through the water cycle.
About 97 percent of Earth's water is salty ocean water; only about 3 percent is fresh water.
Most of Earth's fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps; most of the rest is groundwater.
Only a very small part of Earth's water is fresh liquid water in lakes and rivers that people can easily use.
Oceans cover about 70 percent, or nearly three fourths, of Earth's surface.
The Pacific Ocean is the largest and deepest ocean.
Water is the only substance found naturally on Earth in all three states: solid ice, liquid water and gaseous water vapor.
# oceans (second set)
Ocean water is salty because rivers carry dissolved minerals and salts from weathered rocks into the ocean, and evaporation leaves the salt behind.
The most common salt in ocean water is sodium chloride, table salt.
Salinity is the amount of dissolved salt in water.
Salt water is denser than fresh water, so objects float more easily in salt water.
Salt water freezes at a lower temperature than fresh water.
Ocean currents are like rivers of water flowing in the ocean, driven by wind and by differences in temperature and salinity.
Surface currents are caused mainly by winds; deep currents are caused by differences in density from temperature and salinity.
The Gulf Stream is a warm surface current that carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico north along the east coast of North America and across to Europe.
Warm ocean currents warm the climate of nearby land; cold currents cool it.
Cold, dense water near the poles sinks and flows along the ocean floor toward the equator.
Upwelling brings cold, nutrient rich water from the deep ocean to the surface, supporting many fish.
Most ocean waves are caused by wind blowing over the water's surface.
In a wave, the water mostly moves up and down in circles while the energy moves forward.
The crest is the highest point of a wave and the trough is the lowest point.
Tides are the regular rise and fall of the ocean surface caused mainly by the Moon's gravity and partly by the Sun's gravity.
Most coastal places have two high tides and two low tides about every 24 hours and 50 minutes.
Spring tides are extra high and extra low tides that occur at new moon and full moon when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up.
Neap tides have the smallest difference between high and low tide and occur at the first and third quarter moons.
The continental shelf is the shallow, gently sloping ocean floor near the edge of a continent.
The abyssal plain is the deep, flat ocean floor.
Coral reefs form in warm, shallow, clear tropical ocean water and are made by tiny animals called corals.
Estuaries are places where fresh river water mixes with salty ocean water; they are nurseries for many young fish.
Sunlight only reaches the upper part of the ocean, so photosynthesis by algae and phytoplankton happens near the surface.
Organisms in the deep ocean live in darkness, cold and high pressure; some make their own light by bioluminescence.
Pressure in the ocean increases with depth because of the weight of the water above.
Sonar uses sound waves and their echoes to measure the depth of the ocean and map the ocean floor.
# atmosphere (second set)
The atmosphere is the layer of gases surrounding Earth, held in place by gravity.
Air is a mixture of gases: about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Nitrogen is the most abundant gas in the atmosphere; oxygen is the second most abundant.
The atmosphere protects living things by blocking harmful ultraviolet rays, burning up most meteoroids and keeping temperatures moderate.
The layers of the atmosphere from the ground up are the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere and thermosphere, then the exosphere.
The troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere, where we live and where nearly all weather happens.
In the troposphere, temperature decreases as altitude increases.
The stratosphere contains the ozone layer, which absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation.
Ozone is a form of oxygen with three oxygen atoms.
Chemicals called CFCs, once used in spray cans and refrigerators, damaged the ozone layer and caused an ozone hole.
Meteors burn up in the mesosphere.
The thermosphere is the hottest layer; auroras, the northern and southern lights, occur there.
Air pressure is the weight of the air pressing down on an area.
Air pressure decreases as altitude increases because there is less air above.
Air is thinner at high altitudes, so each breath has less oxygen, and mountain climbers may need extra oxygen.
Water boils at a lower temperature at high altitude because the air pressure is lower.
A barometer measures air pressure; falling air pressure usually means stormy weather is coming and rising pressure means fair weather.
High pressure systems usually bring clear, dry, calm weather; low pressure systems usually bring clouds, rain and storms.
Warm air is less dense than cold air, so warm air rises and cold air sinks.
Wind is the movement of air from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure.
Wind is caused by the uneven heating of Earth's surface by the Sun.
Land heats up and cools down faster than water.
A sea breeze blows from the cooler sea toward the warmer land during the day.
A land breeze blows from the cooler land toward the warmer sea at night.
The Coriolis effect, caused by Earth's rotation, makes winds and ocean currents curve.
Global winds include the trade winds near the equator and the westerlies in the middle latitudes, which move weather from west to east across the United States.
The jet stream is a fast band of wind high in the troposphere that steers storms.
The greenhouse effect is the warming of Earth's surface when gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat in the atmosphere.
Without the natural greenhouse effect, Earth would be too cold for most life.
# weather (second set)
Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a certain place and time.
Weather is described by temperature, air pressure, humidity, wind speed and direction, cloud cover and precipitation.
Meteorologists are scientists who study weather and make weather forecasts.
Weather forecasts are made using data from weather stations, satellites, radar, weather balloons and computer models.
Weather satellites take pictures of clouds and storms from space.
Doppler radar shows where precipitation is falling and how storms are moving.
A thermometer measures temperature; a barometer measures air pressure; an anemometer measures wind speed; a wind vane shows wind direction; a rain gauge measures rainfall; a hygrometer or psychrometer measures humidity.
A wind vane or weather vane points in the direction the wind is coming from.
A wind sock shows wind direction and roughly how strong the wind is.
Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air.
Relative humidity is how much water vapor the air holds compared to the most it could hold at that temperature.
Warm air can hold more water vapor than cold air.
The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water vapor condenses into dew or clouds.
Dew forms when water vapor condenses on cool surfaces such as grass at night; frost forms when the dew point is below freezing.
Fog is a cloud that forms at or near the ground.
Clouds form when warm, moist air rises, expands and cools, and its water vapor condenses on tiny particles of dust.
Cirrus clouds are thin, wispy, feathery clouds high in the sky made of ice crystals; they often mean fair weather or a change coming.
Cumulus clouds are puffy white clouds with flat bottoms that usually mean fair weather.
Stratus clouds are flat gray layers that cover the sky and may bring drizzle.
Cumulonimbus clouds are tall, dark thunderclouds that bring thunderstorms, lightning, heavy rain, hail and sometimes tornadoes.
Nimbus or nimbostratus clouds are dark gray rain clouds.
Rain is liquid precipitation; snow forms when water vapor turns to ice crystals in very cold clouds.
Sleet is rain that freezes into ice pellets as it falls through a layer of cold air.
Freezing rain falls as liquid and freezes when it hits cold surfaces.
Hail forms when updrafts in thunderstorms carry raindrops up into freezing air again and again, adding layers of ice.
An air mass is a large body of air with about the same temperature and humidity throughout.
Air masses that form over oceans are moist; air masses that form over land are dry; air masses from near the poles are cold; from near the equator are warm.
A front is the boundary where two air masses meet; weather changes usually happen at fronts.
A cold front forms when a cold air mass pushes under a warm air mass, forcing the warm air up quickly; it brings thunderstorms and then cooler, drier weather.
A warm front forms when warm air moves over a cold air mass; it brings steady rain or drizzle and then warmer, more humid weather.
A stationary front forms when air masses stop moving and can bring days of rain.
On a weather map, a cold front is shown with blue triangles and a warm front with red half circles.
Thunderstorms form when warm, moist air rises quickly; they produce lightning, thunder, heavy rain and strong winds.
Lightning is a giant spark of static electricity between clouds or between a cloud and the ground.
Thunder is the sound made when lightning heats the air so fast that it expands explosively.
We see lightning before we hear thunder because light travels much faster than sound.
Counting the seconds between a lightning flash and thunder and dividing by five gives the distance to the storm in miles.
During a thunderstorm, stay indoors or in a car, and avoid open fields, tall trees, water and metal objects.
A tornado is a narrow, violently spinning column of air that reaches from a thunderstorm to the ground.
Tornadoes have the fastest winds of any storm but are small and last a short time.
Tornado Alley in the central United States has more tornadoes than anywhere else because cold dry air from Canada meets warm moist air from the Gulf of Mexico.
During a tornado, go to a basement or an inner room without windows on the lowest floor.
A hurricane is a huge rotating storm that forms over warm tropical ocean water and has very strong winds and heavy rain.
Hurricanes get their energy from warm ocean water and weaken when they move over land or cold water.
The eye of a hurricane is the calm, clear center of the storm.
Hurricanes cause damage with strong winds, heavy rain, flooding and storm surge, which is a rise in sea level pushed onto the shore by the storm.
Hurricanes are called typhoons in the western Pacific Ocean and cyclones in the Indian Ocean.
A blizzard is a severe snowstorm with strong winds, blowing snow and low visibility.
A drought is a long period with much less rain than normal; it can harm crops and cause wildfires.
A flood happens when water overflows onto land that is usually dry, often after heavy rain or snowmelt.
A heat wave is a long period of unusually hot weather.
A weather watch means conditions are right for severe weather; a warning means severe weather is happening or about to happen.
Lake effect snow forms when cold air moves over a warmer lake, picks up moisture and drops snow on the land downwind.
Mountains affect weather: air rising up the windward side cools and drops rain, so the leeward side is dry, called a rain shadow.
# climate (second set)
Climate is the average weather pattern of a region over a long period of time, usually 30 years or more.
Climate is determined by latitude, altitude, distance from large bodies of water, ocean currents, prevailing winds and mountains.
Places near the equator are warm all year because they receive the most direct sunlight.
Places near the poles are cold because sunlight strikes them at a low angle and spreads over a larger area.
Places at higher altitudes, such as mountaintops, are colder than places at lower altitudes.
Large bodies of water heat and cool slowly, so coastal areas have milder climates with cooler summers and warmer winters than inland areas.
Inland areas far from oceans have hotter summers and colder winters.
The climate zones are tropical, temperate and polar.
A desert climate is very dry, receiving less than 25 centimeters of rain per year; deserts can be hot or cold.
Climate change is a long term change in Earth's climate, such as the global warming happening now.
Global warming is the increase in Earth's average temperature, mainly caused by greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests.
Effects of global warming include melting glaciers and sea ice, rising sea levels, more heat waves, changes in rainfall, and shifts in where plants and animals can live.
Sea level rises when glaciers and ice sheets on land melt and when ocean water warms and expands.
Melting sea ice that is already floating does not raise sea level much, but melting ice on land does.
Cutting down forests adds carbon dioxide to the air because there are fewer trees to absorb it, and burning trees releases it.
Trees and other plants remove carbon dioxide from the air during photosynthesis.
In the past, Earth's climate changed naturally because of changes in Earth's orbit, volcanic eruptions and changes in the Sun.
El Nino is a periodic warming of Pacific Ocean water that changes weather patterns around the world.
