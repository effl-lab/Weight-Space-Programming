# weather and climate: more specifics
Clouds form when air cools to its dew point and water vapor condenses on dust, smoke or salt particles.
Rising air cools as it expands; sinking air warms as it is compressed.
Low pressure areas have rising air, clouds and precipitation; high pressure areas have sinking air and clear skies.
In the Northern Hemisphere, winds blow counterclockwise around a low pressure center and clockwise around a high.
Weather in the United States usually moves from west to east because of the prevailing westerlies and the jet stream.
A weather map shows fronts, high and low pressure centers, temperatures and precipitation.
Isobars are lines on a weather map connecting places with equal air pressure; close isobars mean strong winds.
A sudden drop in temperature, a shift in wind direction and a line of thunderstorms often mean a cold front has passed.
Humid air feels hotter because sweat evaporates more slowly.
Wind chill makes the air feel colder than the temperature because wind removes body heat faster.
Monsoons are seasonal winds that bring very heavy summer rains to places such as India.
Ocean temperatures change more slowly than land temperatures through the seasons.
Places at the same latitude can have different climates because of altitude, ocean currents and distance from the ocean.
The equator receives the most direct sunlight over the year, so tropical regions are hot.
Polar regions receive slanting sunlight and have long winters, so they are cold.
Temperate regions in the middle latitudes have four distinct seasons.
Climate scientists study averages of temperature and precipitation over decades.
Satellites measure sea ice, sea surface temperature, clouds and storms from space.
# water and oceans: more specifics
People get fresh water from rivers, lakes, reservoirs and wells that pump groundwater from aquifers.
Desalination removes salt from ocean water to make fresh water, but it uses a lot of energy.
Water conservation is important because only a small part of Earth's water is fresh and available.
Overpumping groundwater can lower the water table and cause wells to dry up.
Water pollution can come from point sources, such as a factory pipe, or nonpoint sources, such as runoff from many farms and streets.
Tides rise and fall about twice a day; high tide happens on the side of Earth facing the Moon and on the opposite side.
The highest tides, spring tides, happen during new moon and full moon.
Waves get taller and break as they reach shallow water near the shore.
Rip currents are strong currents that flow away from shore and can carry swimmers out to sea.
Coral reefs are damaged by warming water, pollution and ocean acidification, which cause corals to lose their algae and bleach.
Most ocean life lives in the sunlit upper zone where phytoplankton can photosynthesize.
# space: more specifics
Earth is the only planet known to have life, liquid surface water and an oxygen rich atmosphere.
Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field protect life from harmful radiation from the Sun.
The Sun's gravity keeps the planets in orbit; the planets' gravity keeps their moons in orbit.
A year on Mars is about two Earth years; a day on Mars is a little longer than a day on Earth.
Jupiter and Saturn have dozens of moons; Earth has one moon; Mercury and Venus have none.
The four largest moons of Jupiter, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, were discovered by Galileo.
Europa may have a liquid water ocean under its icy surface.
The Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune contains Pluto and many icy objects; comets come from the Kuiper Belt and the Oort Cloud.
Halley's comet returns to the inner solar system about every 76 years.
A meteor shower happens when Earth passes through the trail of dust left by a comet.
The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old and is about halfway through its life.
Stars form when gravity pulls gas and dust in a nebula together until it is hot enough for fusion.
Our solar system is located in one of the spiral arms of the Milky Way, about two thirds of the way from the center.
The universe contains billions of galaxies.
Astronomers use spectroscopes to learn what elements stars are made of from their light.
A lunar month, from one new moon to the next, is about 29.5 days.
The Moon is slowly moving away from Earth by a few centimeters each year.
Without the Moon, Earth would have much smaller tides.
The Moon's surface has highlands, maria, craters, mountains and fine dust called regolith.
Apollo astronauts brought back Moon rocks that showed the Moon is about as old as Earth.
Seasons on other planets depend on the tilt of their axes.
# chemistry: more specifics
Matter can be classified as pure substances, which are elements and compounds, or mixtures.
Particles of matter attract each other.
In a solid the attraction between particles is strongest.
In a gas the attraction between particles is weakest.
Plasma is a state of matter made of charged particles, found in stars, lightning and neon signs.
The atoms of a solid are arranged in a regular repeating pattern in crystals.
A physical property of a substance can be used to identify it, such as its density, melting point and boiling point.
Each pure substance has its own melting point and boiling point.
Iron, nickel and cobalt are elements that are attracted by magnets.
Sodium and chlorine combine to form table salt, a compound with different properties from either element.
Hydrogen and oxygen gases can react explosively to form water.
Carbon atoms can bond in long chains, forming the molecules of living things, fuels and plastics.
Glucose is a sugar made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
Proteins are made of amino acids; DNA is made of nucleotides.
When a candle burns, the wax reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water vapor, heat and light.
Animals release carbon dioxide and water as waste products of cellular respiration.
Burning combines fuel with oxygen and releases energy, carbon dioxide and water.
Photosynthesis stores energy in sugar and releases oxygen.
Cellular respiration releases the energy stored in sugar.
Hydrogen peroxide breaks down into water and oxygen, faster when a catalyst is added.
Rust, which is iron oxide, is weaker than iron and flakes off.
Chemical reactions can be fast, like an explosion, or slow, like rusting.
Indicator solutions change color depending on pH.
Vinegar is acetic acid; lemon juice contains citric acid; stomach acid is hydrochloric acid.
Bleach and ammonia should never be mixed because they release poisonous gases.
# life science: more specifics
Living things are made of cells, use energy, grow and develop, reproduce, respond to stimuli, and maintain homeostasis.
Viruses are not considered living by many scientists because they cannot reproduce on their own.
Yeast is a single celled fungus used to make bread rise and to make some foods.
Bacteria are used to make yogurt, cheese and some medicines.
Penicillin, the first antibiotic, was discovered by Alexander Fleming from a mold.
Louis Pasteur showed that germs cause many diseases and that microorganisms do not come from nonliving matter.
Plants need nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium from the soil.
Carnivorous plants such as the Venus flytrap and pitcher plant trap insects to get nitrogen in poor soils.
Some plants, such as dandelions and maple trees, have seeds carried by the wind; burdock seeds have hooks that stick to animal fur.
Coconuts float and are carried by ocean water to new islands.
Fruits such as berries are eaten by animals, and the seeds pass through and are deposited in new places.
Bulbs, tubers, runners and cuttings let plants reproduce without seeds.
A potato is a tuber, an underground stem that stores food.
Carrots, radishes and beets are roots that store food.
Lettuce and spinach are leaves; celery is a stem; broccoli is a flower; corn kernels and peas are seeds; apples and tomatoes are fruits.
Plants that grow in shade often have larger, thinner leaves; plants that grow in full sun often have smaller, thicker leaves.
Trees in windy places are often short with deep roots.
Some animals change their behavior with the seasons: they migrate, hibernate, store food or grow thicker coats.
Animals learn by trial and error, by imitation, and by conditioning, as when a dog learns that a bell means food.
Ducklings and goslings follow the first moving object they see, usually their mother; this is called imprinting.
Animals communicate danger: prairie dogs bark warnings, and deer raise their white tails.
Bird songs attract mates and mark territory.
Fireflies flash light to attract mates.
Some fish, such as salmon, can smell their way back to the stream where they hatched.
A food chain always begins with a producer that captures energy from the Sun.
Organisms that make their own food are autotrophs; organisms that must eat food are heterotrophs.
Energy in food is measured in calories.
# earth materials and resources: more specifics
Metals such as iron, copper, aluminum and gold come from ores mined from Earth's crust.
Mining can destroy habitats, cause erosion and pollute water, so mined land should be reclaimed.
Sand and gravel are used to make concrete and roads.
Clay is used to make bricks, pottery and tiles.
Soil takes a very long time to form, so losing topsoil to erosion is a serious problem.
Peat is partly decayed plant material in bogs; over time it can become coal.
Crude oil is pumped from wells and refined into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel and other products.
Natural gas is mostly methane.
Uranium is a nonrenewable resource mined from rocks.
Fresh water, clean air and fertile soil are natural resources that must be protected.
