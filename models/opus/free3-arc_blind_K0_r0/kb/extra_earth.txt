# more earth science, phrased the way questions ask
Glaciers formed many lakes, including the Great Lakes, by carving out basins during the last ice age.
Glaciers deposit unsorted sediment called till, and leave behind scratches called striations on bedrock.
Large boulders found far from their source were carried there by glaciers.
Long parallel scratches on rocks show that a glacier moved over the area.
The Finger Lakes and Long Island in New York were shaped by glaciers.
Water is the most important agent of erosion that shapes Earth's surface.
Fast moving water can carry larger rocks and more sediment than slow moving water.
When a river slows down it deposits sediment; when it speeds up it erodes more.
Rivers carry sediment downstream and deposit it at the mouth of the river.
A meander is a curve or bend in a river; erosion happens on the outside of the bend and deposition on the inside.
Sediment that settles in water forms layers with the largest heaviest particles on the bottom.
Sand dunes move as wind blows sand from one side to the other.
Wind erosion is greatest in dry areas with little vegetation, such as deserts.
The Dust Bowl of the 1930s happened when drought and poor farming removed plants and wind eroded the topsoil.
Ocean waves and currents break down rocks and shells into sand on beaches.
Jetties, seawalls and breakwaters are built to reduce beach erosion by waves.
Planting dune grass helps hold sand in place and prevents erosion of beaches.
Rocks found at the bottom of a waterfall or in a stream are smooth and round because of abrasion by moving water.
Rocks that are rough and sharp edged have had little weathering or erosion.
Weathering of rock produces sand, silt and clay, which become part of soil.
Lichens growing on rocks break the rock down and help make soil.
The rate of weathering depends on climate, rock type and surface area.
Soft rocks such as sandstone weather faster than hard rocks such as granite.
A statue made of marble weathers faster in a city with acid rain.
Old gravestones made of marble or limestone become hard to read because of chemical weathering by acid rain.
Freezing and thawing breaks rocks most in places where temperatures go above and below freezing often.
Physical weathering does not change the minerals in the rock; it only makes the pieces smaller.
Erosion moves sediment; deposition builds up landforms such as deltas, sand bars, beaches and dunes.
Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes can change Earth's surface quickly; weathering and erosion usually change it slowly.
Landslides, floods, earthquakes and volcanic eruptions are rapid changes to Earth's surface.
The formation of canyons, valleys and mountains takes millions of years.
Mountains are worn down over time by weathering and erosion.
The Rocky Mountains are younger and taller than the Appalachian Mountains.
Crystal size in igneous rock tells how fast the magma or lava cooled.
Rocks are classified by how they formed: igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic.
A rock containing pebbles of many sizes cemented together is a conglomerate.
Sedimentary rock layers that are folded or tilted were moved after they formed.
An igneous intrusion that cuts across rock layers is younger than the layers it cuts.
A fault that cuts across rock layers is younger than the layers.
Rock layers that are the same on both sides of a canyon were once connected.
The Grand Canyon shows many layers of sedimentary rock, with the oldest layers at the bottom.
Fossils in the lowest rock layers are the oldest; fossils in upper layers are younger.
Simple organisms appear in older rock layers and more complex organisms appear in younger rock layers.
Coral fossils found in a desert show that the area was once covered by a warm shallow sea.
Fossilized seashells found high in the mountains show that rock formed under the sea was lifted up.
Fossils of the reptile Mesosaurus are found in both South America and Africa.
Trilobites were ocean animals that are common index fossils.
Amber is hardened tree sap that can preserve insects.
Mammoths have been found preserved in frozen ice.
Animals trapped in tar pits such as La Brea were preserved as fossils.
Fossils can show what an organism looked like, what it ate, and what its environment was like.
Few organisms become fossils because they must be buried quickly before they decay.
Dinosaur footprints are trace fossils.
Oil, coal and natural gas are called fossil fuels because they formed from the remains of ancient organisms.
# Earth materials and resources
Minerals are used to make many products: quartz for glass, bauxite for aluminum, gypsum for drywall, halite for salt, graphite for pencils, talc for powder.
Iron ore is mined to make iron and steel.
Copper is mined and used for electrical wires and pipes.
Clay is used to make bricks, pottery and ceramics.
Limestone is used to make cement and concrete.
Sand is used to make glass and concrete.
Granite is a hard rock used for buildings, countertops and monuments.
Slate splits into flat sheets and is used for roofs and chalkboards.
Pumice is used as an abrasive to smooth surfaces.
Soil is a natural resource needed to grow food.
It takes hundreds of years to form an inch of topsoil, so soil should be conserved.
Topsoil can be lost by erosion from wind and water when the land is bare.
Crop rotation keeps soil fertile by planting different crops in a field each year.
Contour plowing follows the curves of the land to slow water runoff and reduce erosion.
Strip cropping, terracing and no till farming reduce soil erosion.
Windbreaks are rows of trees planted to slow wind and reduce wind erosion of soil.
Paving land with concrete and asphalt increases runoff because water cannot soak into the ground.
Wetlands absorb floodwater and filter pollutants from water.
Levees and dams are built to control flooding.
Building homes on floodplains puts them at risk of flooding.
# Water on Earth
Most of Earth's fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps at the poles.
The largest source of fresh liquid water is groundwater.
Earth's oceans contain most of the water on Earth.
People get drinking water from rivers, lakes, reservoirs and wells that tap groundwater.
A reservoir is an artificial lake made by building a dam that stores water.
Groundwater can be polluted by chemicals from landfills, septic systems and fertilizers that seep into the soil.
Pumping too much groundwater can cause wells to dry up and the land to sink.
Water flows downhill because of gravity.
Water moves through the water cycle in the oceans, atmosphere, land, living things and underground.
The amount of water on Earth stays about the same because water is recycled in the water cycle.
A puddle dries up because the water evaporates into the air.
Wet clothes dry faster on a warm sunny windy day because evaporation is faster.
Water evaporating from the ocean leaves the salt behind.
Rain is fresh water because salt does not evaporate with the water.
Clouds are made of tiny drops of liquid water or ice crystals, not water vapor.
Water vapor is an invisible gas in the air.
The water cycle moves heat energy around Earth.
Humidity is higher on hot summer days near the ocean.
# Weather details
The temperature usually is highest in the afternoon and lowest just before sunrise.
Clear nights are colder than cloudy nights because clouds trap heat near the surface.
Thunderstorms often form on hot humid afternoons when warm moist air rises quickly.
Weather forecasts are based on patterns in weather data collected over time.
Meteorologists track hurricanes with satellites and airplanes.
A hurricane warning means a hurricane is expected; people should evacuate low coastal areas.
During a thunderstorm stay indoors or in a car and away from tall objects, water and metal.
During a tornado go to a basement or an interior room without windows on the lowest floor.
Lightning tends to strike the tallest object in an area.
A lightning rod protects a building by carrying electricity safely to the ground.
Wind chill makes the air feel colder than the temperature because wind removes heat from the skin.
The heat index makes it feel hotter because high humidity slows evaporation of sweat.
A weather balloon carries instruments into the atmosphere to measure temperature, pressure and humidity.
Air pressure is measured in millibars or inches of mercury.
Precipitation is measured in millimeters, centimeters or inches using a rain gauge.
Wind is named by the direction it comes from; a north wind blows from the north.
Wind speed is measured in kilometers per hour or miles per hour with an anemometer.
Fog forms when moist air near the ground cools to its dew point, often at night or early morning.
Smog is a mixture of smoke, fog and air pollution common in large cities.
Air temperature is affected by the amount of sunlight, cloud cover, wind and nearness to water.
In the Northern Hemisphere, winds around a low pressure system blow counterclockwise.
Monsoons are seasonal winds that bring heavy rain in summer, as in India.
Lake effect snow happens when cold air moves over warmer lake water, picking up moisture.
Climate is described by average temperature and average precipitation over many years.
Tropical climates near the equator are hot all year; polar climates near the poles are cold all year; temperate climates have four seasons.
New York State has a temperate climate with four seasons: cold winters and warm summers.
Places at the same latitude can have different climates because of elevation, ocean currents and mountains.
A climate graph shows the average monthly temperature and precipitation of a place.
Earth's climate has changed many times in the past, with ice ages and warm periods.
Greenhouse gases include carbon dioxide, methane, water vapor and nitrous oxide.
Methane is released by cattle, landfills, rice fields and natural gas leaks.
Higher global temperatures can cause more droughts, heat waves, stronger storms and changes in habitats.
Melting sea ice threatens polar bears, which hunt seals from the ice.
Reducing the burning of fossil fuels and planting trees can slow global warming.
