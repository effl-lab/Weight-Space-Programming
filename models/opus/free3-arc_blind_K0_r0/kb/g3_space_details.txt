# solar system details (one object per line)
Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun, is the smallest planet, has almost no atmosphere and has many craters.
Mercury has extreme temperatures because it has almost no atmosphere to hold heat.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat by a strong greenhouse effect.
Venus is the brightest planet in the night sky and is sometimes called the morning star or evening star.
Venus is about the same size as Earth and rotates backward very slowly.
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only planet known to have liquid water on its surface and life.
Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and is called the Red Planet because its soil contains iron oxide, or rust.
Mars has the largest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons, polar ice caps and two small moons.
Rovers have found evidence that liquid water once flowed on Mars.
The asteroid belt is between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter.
Jupiter is the largest planet; it is a gas giant with a Great Red Spot, a giant storm, and many moons.
Saturn is a gas giant famous for its bright rings made of ice and rock.
Saturn is less dense than water.
Uranus is an ice giant that rotates on its side.
Neptune is the farthest planet from the Sun; it is a cold, windy ice giant with a blue color.
The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small, rocky terrestrial planets.
The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are large gas or ice giants with rings and many moons.
Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit the Sun and have longer years.
Planets closer to the Sun move faster in their orbits.
Planets do not make their own light; they shine by reflecting sunlight.
A planet's gravity depends on its mass; Jupiter has the strongest gravity.
An object would weigh the most on Jupiter and less on the Moon than on Earth.
Comets are made of ice, dust and rock; when a comet comes near the Sun, the ice turns to gas and forms a glowing tail that points away from the Sun.
Asteroids are rocky objects that orbit the Sun, mostly in the asteroid belt.
A meteoroid is a small piece of rock in space; a meteor is the streak of light when it burns up in the atmosphere; a meteorite is a piece that hits the ground.
Shooting stars are meteors, not stars.
Earth's atmosphere protects it from most meteoroids, which burn up from friction with the air.
The Moon has no atmosphere and no liquid water, so craters on the Moon last for billions of years.
The Moon's dark areas, called maria, are plains of old cooled lava.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's gravity.
The Moon takes about 27 days to orbit Earth and about 29.5 days to go through all its phases.
The Moon rotates once each time it orbits Earth, so the same side always faces Earth.
The Moon does not make its own light; it reflects light from the Sun.
The Moon appears to change shape because we see different amounts of its sunlit half as it orbits Earth.
A full moon happens when Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, so we see the whole lit side.
A new moon happens when the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, so the lit side faces away from us.
A lunar eclipse can happen only at full moon, when Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
A solar eclipse can happen only at new moon, when the Moon's shadow falls on Earth.
High tides and low tides are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity pulling on Earth's oceans.
Most coastal places have two high tides and two low tides about every day.
Spring tides, the highest high tides, happen at new moon and full moon when the Sun and Moon line up.
Neap tides, with the smallest difference between high and low tide, happen at the quarter moons.
If the Moon were farther from Earth, tides would be smaller.

# Sun and stars
The Sun is a medium-sized yellow star made mostly of hydrogen and helium gas.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion, joining hydrogen atoms to make helium.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth, so it looks much bigger and brighter than other stars.
Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.
Sunspots are darker, cooler areas on the Sun's surface.
Solar flares send bursts of charged particles that can cause auroras and disrupt radio communication.
Auroras, the northern and southern lights, form when charged particles from the Sun hit gases high in the atmosphere near the poles.
Stars differ in size, color, temperature and brightness.
The color of a star shows its surface temperature: blue stars are hottest, then white, yellow, orange, and red stars are coolest.
How bright a star looks from Earth depends on how much light it gives off and how far away it is.
A star that looks dim may be very bright but very far away.
Stars form in clouds of gas and dust called nebulas.
A star like the Sun becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf at the end of its life.
A very massive star ends in a supernova explosion and may become a neutron star or a black hole.
A black hole has gravity so strong that not even light can escape.
Polaris, the North Star, is almost directly above Earth's North Pole, so it stays in the same place in the sky while other stars seem to circle it.
Stars appear to move across the night sky from east to west because Earth rotates.
Different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves around the Sun.
Constellations are patterns of stars, such as Orion and the Big Dipper, that people use to find their way in the sky.
Stars twinkle because their light is bent by moving air in Earth's atmosphere.
Stars are hidden during the day because the Sun's light is much brighter.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.
Our solar system is in the Milky Way, a spiral galaxy.
Galaxies can be spiral, elliptical or irregular in shape.
The universe contains billions of galaxies.
Most scientists think the universe began with the Big Bang about 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.
Light from distant galaxies is shifted toward red, which shows that the galaxies are moving away and the universe is expanding.
Distances to stars are measured in light-years because they are so far away.
After the Sun, the closest star to Earth is Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light-years away.

# Earth's motions, day, year and seasons
Earth rotates on its axis once about every 24 hours, causing day and night.
The side of Earth facing the Sun has day; the side facing away has night.
The Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west because Earth rotates from west to east.
Earth revolves around the Sun once about every 365 days, which is one year.
Every four years a leap year adds one day because a year is about 365 and one fourth days.
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees, which causes the seasons.
It is summer in the Northern Hemisphere when the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun and gets more direct sunlight and longer days.
It is winter in the Northern Hemisphere when it is tilted away from the Sun, so sunlight is less direct and days are shorter.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere, it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
The seasons are not caused by Earth's distance from the Sun; Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.
The summer solstice, around June 21, has the most daylight in the Northern Hemisphere.
The winter solstice, around December 21, has the least daylight in the Northern Hemisphere.
At the spring and fall equinoxes, day and night are about equal everywhere.
Places near the equator have about 12 hours of daylight all year and little change in seasons.
Places near the poles have very long days in summer and very long nights in winter.
The Sun is highest in the sky at noon, so shadows are shortest at noon.
Shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the Sun is low in the sky.
A shadow points away from the Sun; in the morning shadows point west and in the evening they point east.
In the Northern Hemisphere the noon Sun is higher in the sky in summer than in winter.
A sundial uses the shadow of a stick to tell time as the Sun appears to move across the sky.
Time zones exist because Earth rotates and different places face the Sun at different times.
