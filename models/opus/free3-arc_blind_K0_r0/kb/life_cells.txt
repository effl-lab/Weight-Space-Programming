# Living things
All living things are made of one or more cells.
All living things need energy, grow, develop, reproduce, respond to their environment, and get rid of wastes.
Living things need food, water, air and a place to live, called a habitat.
Basic needs of animals are food, water, oxygen, shelter and space.
Basic needs of plants are sunlight, water, air with carbon dioxide, nutrients from soil and space to grow.
Nonliving things such as rocks, water, air and sunlight do not grow or reproduce.
Biotic factors are the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are the nonliving parts of an ecosystem, such as sunlight, water, air, temperature, rocks and soil.
A stimulus is something in the environment that causes a living thing to respond, and the reaction is a response.
Homeostasis is the ability of an organism to keep stable internal conditions, such as body temperature, water balance and blood sugar.
Sweating and shivering help the body keep a stable temperature, an example of homeostasis.
# Cell theory
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in all living things.
Cell theory states that all living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from existing cells.
Robert Hooke first observed and named cells when he looked at cork through a microscope.
Anton van Leeuwenhoek was the first to observe living microorganisms with a simple microscope.
Most cells are too small to be seen without a microscope.
Unicellular organisms, or single celled organisms, such as bacteria, amoebas and paramecia, are made of only one cell.
Multicellular organisms such as plants, animals and humans are made of many cells.
In multicellular organisms, cells are specialized to do different jobs.
Levels of organization in multicellular organisms: cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, and organ systems form an organism.
A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together to perform a function, such as muscle tissue or nerve tissue.
An organ is a group of different tissues that work together to perform a specific job, such as the heart, stomach, lungs or a leaf.
An organ system is a group of organs that work together, such as the digestive system.
# Cell parts
The nucleus is the control center of the cell; it contains DNA and controls the cell's activities.
The nucleus contains chromosomes made of DNA, which carry the genetic information or hereditary instructions of the cell.
The cell membrane is a thin flexible layer around the cell that controls what substances enter and leave the cell.
The cell membrane is selectively permeable, letting some substances pass through while keeping others out.
Cytoplasm is the jelly like fluid inside the cell where organelles are found and many chemical reactions occur.
Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell; they release energy from food, such as glucose, through cellular respiration.
Cells that need a lot of energy, such as muscle cells, have many mitochondria.
Chloroplasts are organelles in plant cells and algae that capture sunlight energy to make food by photosynthesis.
Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that absorbs light energy.
The cell wall is a rigid layer outside the cell membrane of plant cells that supports and protects the cell and gives it shape.
Plant cell walls are made of cellulose.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole, which animal cells lack.
Both plant and animal cells have a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria and ribosomes.
Vacuoles store water, food and wastes; plant cells have one large vacuole that helps keep the plant firm.
When a plant does not get enough water its vacuoles shrink and the plant wilts.
Ribosomes are small structures where proteins are made.
The endoplasmic reticulum transports materials within the cell; the Golgi apparatus packages and ships proteins.
Lysosomes contain enzymes that digest wastes and worn out cell parts.
Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have no nucleus; their DNA floats in the cytoplasm.
Eukaryotic cells, such as plant, animal and fungus cells, have a nucleus and membrane bound organelles.
Specialized cells have shapes that fit their functions: nerve cells are long to carry messages, red blood cells are disk shaped to carry oxygen.
Muscle cells contract to produce movement.
Root hair cells have a large surface area for absorbing water.
Sperm cells have tails called flagella to swim.
Guard cells open and close the stomata, the tiny pores in leaves that let gases in and out.
# Cell processes
Diffusion is the movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration.
Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a cell membrane.
Oxygen enters cells and carbon dioxide leaves cells by diffusion.
Active transport uses energy to move materials across the cell membrane against a concentration gradient.
Cellular respiration is the process in which cells use oxygen to break down glucose and release energy, producing carbon dioxide and water.
The equation for cellular respiration is glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy.
Both plant and animal cells carry out cellular respiration in their mitochondria.
Fermentation releases energy from sugar without oxygen; yeast fermentation produces carbon dioxide and alcohol, which makes bread rise.
Muscle cells can make lactic acid by fermentation when they lack oxygen during hard exercise.
Cell division is how cells reproduce; organisms grow by cell division.
Mitosis is cell division that produces two identical daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell.
Mitosis is used for growth, repair of injured tissue and replacing old cells, and for asexual reproduction.
Meiosis is cell division that produces sex cells, or gametes, with half the number of chromosomes.
Sex cells are egg cells and sperm cells, which have half the chromosomes of body cells.
Human body cells have 46 chromosomes, or 23 pairs; human sex cells have 23 chromosomes.
Cancer is a disease in which cells divide uncontrollably.
Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living things, such as digestion.
# Classification
Classification is grouping living things based on their similar characteristics and structures.
Scientists classify organisms to organize and study the great diversity of living things.
Carl Linnaeus developed the system of classification and binomial nomenclature, a two part scientific name of genus and species.
The levels of classification from largest to smallest are domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
A species is a group of similar organisms that can mate and produce fertile offspring.
Organisms in the same genus are more closely related than organisms in the same family or order.
The six kingdoms are animals, plants, fungi, protists, archaebacteria and eubacteria.
The three domains are Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.
Organisms are classified by their cell type, number of cells, body structures, and how they get food.
A dichotomous key is a tool that uses a series of paired choices to identify organisms.
The plant kingdom contains multicellular organisms with cell walls that make their own food by photosynthesis.
The animal kingdom contains multicellular organisms that must eat other organisms for food and can usually move.
Fungi include mushrooms, molds, yeasts and mildew; fungi absorb food from dead or living organisms.
Fungi are decomposers; they cannot make their own food because they have no chlorophyll.
Mushrooms are fungi that reproduce with spores.
Yeast is a single celled fungus used to make bread rise and in fermentation.
Mold is a fungus that grows on bread and fruit in warm, damp places.
Penicillin, an antibiotic, was discovered from a mold.
Protists are mostly single celled eukaryotic organisms such as amoebas, paramecia, euglena and algae.
Algae are plant like protists that make food by photosynthesis and live in water.
An amoeba moves using pseudopods; a paramecium moves using cilia; a euglena moves using a flagellum.
Bacteria are single celled prokaryotic organisms with no nucleus.
Bacteria are found almost everywhere: in soil, water, air, and inside other organisms.
Some bacteria cause diseases such as strep throat, food poisoning and tuberculosis.
Many bacteria are helpful: they decompose dead matter, recycle nutrients, help digest food in the intestines, and are used to make yogurt and cheese.
Nitrogen fixing bacteria in soil and in the roots of legumes like peas and beans change nitrogen from the air into a form plants can use.
Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, splitting in two.
Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not work against viruses.
Viruses are tiny particles made of genetic material in a protein coat; they are not cells.
Viruses can only reproduce inside the living cells of a host.
Viruses cause diseases such as the common cold, flu or influenza, chicken pox, measles, polio and AIDS.
Vaccines help the body build immunity to diseases caused by viruses and bacteria.
Microorganisms, or microbes, are living things too small to see without a microscope, such as bacteria, protists and some fungi.
