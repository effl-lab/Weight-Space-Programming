# energy changes in everyday devices and situations (one device per line)
A magnifying glass focuses sunlight to a small spot, changing light energy into thermal energy that can burn paper.
A solar oven collects sunlight and changes light energy into thermal energy to cook food.
A solar water heater changes light energy from the Sun into thermal energy in water.
A solar cell or solar panel changes light energy into electrical energy.
A solar-powered calculator changes light energy into electrical energy.
A battery changes stored chemical energy into electrical energy.
A flashlight changes chemical energy in the battery into electrical energy and then into light energy.
A battery-powered radio changes chemical energy into electrical energy and then into sound energy.
A light bulb changes electrical energy into light energy and thermal energy.
An incandescent bulb wastes most of its electrical energy as heat; an LED bulb changes more of it into light.
An electric fan changes electrical energy into mechanical energy of motion.
An electric motor changes electrical energy into mechanical energy.
A generator changes mechanical energy into electrical energy.
A wind turbine changes the kinetic energy of moving air into electrical energy.
A water wheel or hydroelectric dam changes the energy of falling or moving water into mechanical and electrical energy.
A hair dryer changes electrical energy into thermal energy and kinetic energy of moving air.
A toaster changes electrical energy into thermal energy.
An electric stove changes electrical energy into thermal energy.
An electric heater changes electrical energy into heat.
A microwave oven changes electrical energy into microwave radiation that heats the water in food.
A speaker or loudspeaker changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A microphone changes sound energy into electrical energy.
A doorbell changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A television changes electrical energy into light and sound energy.
A guitar string changes mechanical energy into sound energy when it is plucked.
A drum changes the mechanical energy of the drumstick into sound energy.
Clapping hands changes mechanical energy into sound energy.
Rubbing hands together changes mechanical energy into thermal energy because of friction.
Striking a match changes chemical energy into thermal energy and light energy.
A candle changes chemical energy in wax into light energy and thermal energy.
A campfire changes chemical energy in wood into thermal energy and light energy.
A gas stove burns natural gas, changing chemical energy into thermal energy.
A car engine burns gasoline, changing chemical energy into thermal energy and mechanical energy of motion.
A person riding a bicycle changes chemical energy from food into mechanical energy.
Muscles change chemical energy from food into mechanical energy and heat.
Plants change light energy into chemical energy during photosynthesis.
Food stores chemical energy that came from the Sun through plants.
When a person eats food, the body changes the chemical energy in food into energy for movement and heat.
A firecracker changes chemical energy into sound, light and thermal energy.
Glow sticks change chemical energy into light energy.
Fireflies change chemical energy into light energy.
A wind-up toy stores elastic potential energy in a spring and changes it into kinetic energy.
A stretched rubber band or a compressed spring stores elastic potential energy.
A bow pulled back stores elastic potential energy that becomes kinetic energy of the arrow.
A ball at the top of a hill has gravitational potential energy that changes into kinetic energy as it rolls down.
A roller coaster has the most potential energy at the top of the highest hill and the most kinetic energy at the bottom.
As a skateboarder rolls down a ramp, potential energy changes into kinetic energy.
As a ball is thrown upward, its kinetic energy changes into potential energy and it slows down.
When a ball is dropped, its potential energy changes into kinetic energy as it falls faster.
A swinging pendulum changes potential energy into kinetic energy and back again.
Water stored behind a dam has potential energy.
A falling object gains kinetic energy and loses potential energy.
Water at the top of a waterfall has potential energy that becomes kinetic energy as it falls.
Nuclear power plants change nuclear energy into thermal energy and then into electrical energy.
The Sun changes nuclear energy into light and heat by nuclear fusion.
Geothermal power plants use heat from inside Earth to make electricity.
A thermometer works because the liquid inside expands when it is heated and contracts when it is cooled.
A thermostat turns a heater on and off to keep a room at a set temperature.
A refrigerator moves thermal energy from inside the refrigerator to the air outside it.
In every energy change some energy is changed into heat that spreads out and is not useful.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it only changes from one form to another.
When a moving car brakes, its kinetic energy changes into thermal energy in the brakes.
When a bouncing ball loses height, its energy has changed into heat and sound.
A moving object has kinetic energy; the faster it moves and the more mass it has, the more kinetic energy it has.
A heavier truck moving at the same speed as a car has more kinetic energy than the car.
An object raised higher has more gravitational potential energy.
A book on a high shelf has more potential energy than a book on a low shelf.
Electrical energy is the energy of moving electric charges.
Radiant energy or light energy travels as electromagnetic waves and can move through empty space.
Thermal energy is the energy of moving particles in a substance; hotter objects have more thermal energy.
Sound energy is carried by vibrations that travel through matter.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds between atoms in substances such as food, fuel and batteries.
Mechanical energy is the energy of motion and position of an object.
Nuclear energy is stored in the nucleus of an atom.

# heat transfer situations
Heat always flows from a warmer object to a cooler object until they reach the same temperature.
When a hot object touches a cold object, thermal energy moves from the hot object to the cold object.
When a person holds an ice cube, heat moves from the hand to the ice, so the ice melts and the hand feels cold.
A metal spoon in hot soup gets hot by conduction.
A pan on a stove is heated by conduction from the burner.
Touching a hot stove burns the hand by conduction.
Conduction is the transfer of heat by direct contact between objects or particles.
Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement of a fluid such as air or water; warm fluid rises and cool fluid sinks.
Boiling water in a pot circulates by convection currents.
A room is warmed by convection when warm air from a heater rises and cooler air sinks.
Radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves; it does not need matter.
The Sun warms Earth by radiation.
Heat from a campfire felt on the face is radiation.
Heat lamps keep food warm by radiation.
Metals such as copper, aluminum and iron are good conductors of heat.
Wood, plastic, rubber, cloth, foam, air and glass are poor conductors of heat, called insulators.
Pot handles are made of plastic or wood because these are insulators that keep heat from reaching the hand.
Cooking pots are made of metal because metal conducts heat well to the food.
A metal object feels colder than a wooden object at the same temperature because metal conducts heat away from the hand faster.
A tile floor feels colder than a carpet at the same temperature because tile conducts heat away from the feet faster.
Ice melts faster on a metal tray than on a plastic tray because metal conducts heat from the air and table to the ice faster.
A thermos or vacuum flask keeps drinks hot or cold because the vacuum between its walls stops conduction and convection.
A foam cup keeps a drink hot longer than a metal cup because foam is an insulator.
A wool blanket or a coat keeps a person warm by trapping air, which slows the loss of body heat.
Insulation in the walls and attic of a house keeps heat inside in winter and outside in summer.
Double-pane windows have a layer of air or gas between the glass that reduces heat loss.
Animals with thick fur or feathers stay warm because fur and feathers trap air that insulates them.
Blubber, a thick layer of fat, insulates whales, seals and polar bears in cold water.
Dark colors absorb more sunlight and get hotter than light colors.
White and light-colored clothing reflects sunlight and keeps a person cooler in summer.
Black clothing absorbs more light and gets warmer in the Sun than white clothing.
Shiny surfaces such as aluminum foil reflect heat.
A dark-colored car gets hotter inside in the sunlight than a white car.
Asphalt and pavement absorb sunlight and become much hotter than grass on a sunny day.
Sand on a beach gets hotter than the water during the day because land heats faster than water.
Water heats up and cools down more slowly than land, so oceans keep coastal climates milder.
Water has a high specific heat; it takes a lot of energy to change its temperature.
Evaporation cools a surface because the evaporating water takes heat energy with it.
Sweating cools the body because heat from the skin is used to evaporate the sweat.
Dogs pant to cool off by evaporation from the tongue and mouth.
Blowing on hot soup cools it by speeding up evaporation and moving warm air away.
Wind makes a person feel colder because it carries body heat away faster; this is called wind chill.
Adding ice to a warm drink cools the drink because heat moves from the drink into the ice.
When two equal amounts of water at different temperatures are mixed, the final temperature is between the two temperatures.
Heating a substance makes its particles move faster; cooling makes them move slower.
Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles of a substance.
The thermal energy of an object depends on its temperature and its mass; a bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a cup of hot water.
