# physical and chemical changes in everyday situations (one change per line)
Melting ice, freezing water, boiling water and condensing steam are physical changes; the substance is still water.
Cutting paper, tearing cloth, crushing a can, breaking glass and folding a shirt are physical changes.
Dissolving sugar or salt in water is a physical change because the sugar or salt can be recovered by evaporating the water.
Mixing sand and iron filings is a physical change because they can be separated with a magnet.
Melting butter or chocolate is a physical change.
Shaping clay, sharpening a pencil and chopping wood are physical changes.
Burning wood, paper, a candle wick or gasoline is a chemical change that produces new substances such as ash, smoke and carbon dioxide.
Rusting iron is a chemical change in which iron combines with oxygen and water to form rust, iron oxide.
Cooking an egg, baking bread or a cake, and toasting bread are chemical changes.
Milk turning sour, fruit rotting and food spoiling are chemical changes.
Digesting food is a chemical change because the food is broken down into new substances.
Fireworks exploding and a match burning are chemical changes.
Baking soda mixed with vinegar makes bubbles of carbon dioxide gas; this is a chemical reaction.
A tarnished silver spoon and a green copper roof are results of chemical changes.
Leaves changing color in the fall and photosynthesis are chemical changes.
Signs of a chemical change include a new color, bubbles of gas, a new smell, light, heat given off or absorbed, and a solid precipitate forming.
In a chemical change the atoms are rearranged to form new substances with new properties.
In a physical change the substance keeps the same chemical makeup; only its size, shape or state changes.
Most physical changes can be reversed easily; most chemical changes cannot be reversed easily.
A change of state, such as from solid to liquid, is a physical change.

# conservation of mass
In a chemical reaction the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
Mass is conserved when ice melts: 50 grams of ice becomes 50 grams of water.
When salt dissolves in water, the mass of the solution equals the mass of the salt plus the mass of the water.
When wood burns in an open fire the ashes weigh less because gases such as carbon dioxide and water vapor escape into the air.
When a reaction happens in a sealed container, the mass stays the same because no matter can escape.
When steel wool rusts, its mass increases because it combines with oxygen from the air.

# properties of matter
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.
Air is matter because it has mass and takes up space.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object; it is measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up; it is measured in milliliters, liters or cubic centimeters.
The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder.
The volume of an irregular object is found by water displacement: the rise in the water level in a graduated cylinder equals its volume.
The volume of a rectangular box is its length times its width times its height.
Density is mass divided by volume.
An object that is less dense than water floats; an object more dense than water sinks.
Wood, ice, cork and oil float on water because they are less dense than water.
Rocks, coins and iron nails sink in water because they are denser than water.
Ice floats because water expands when it freezes, so ice is less dense than liquid water.
Oil floats on top of water because oil is less dense and does not mix with water.
A large piece and a small piece of the same material have the same density.
Cutting an object in half does not change its density.
Salt water is denser than fresh water, so objects float more easily in salt water.
A boat floats because its shape displaces a large volume of water, so its overall density is less than water.
Buoyancy is the upward force a fluid puts on an object in it.
Solids have a definite shape and a definite volume.
Liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of their container.
Gases have no definite shape or volume; they spread out to fill their container.
Particles in a solid are packed closely and vibrate in place.
Particles in a liquid are close together but can slide past each other.
Particles in a gas are far apart and move quickly in all directions.
Gases can be compressed into a smaller space because their particles are far apart.
Heating a gas in a closed container increases its pressure because the particles move faster and hit the walls more often.
A balloon gets bigger when it is warmed and smaller when it is cooled.
A balloon put in a freezer shrinks because the air particles inside slow down and move closer together.
A ball pumped up indoors becomes softer outside on a cold day because the air inside contracts.
The boiling point of water is 100 degrees Celsius, or 212 degrees Fahrenheit, at sea level.
The freezing point and melting point of water is 0 degrees Celsius, or 32 degrees Fahrenheit.
Water boils at a lower temperature at high altitude because the air pressure is lower.
Adding salt to water lowers its freezing point and raises its boiling point.
Salt is spread on icy roads because it lowers the freezing point of water and melts the ice.
The temperature of melting ice stays at 0 degrees Celsius until all the ice has melted.
The temperature of boiling water stays at 100 degrees Celsius while it boils.
Physical properties include color, shape, size, mass, volume, density, hardness, texture, smell, magnetism, melting point, boiling point and the ability to conduct heat or electricity.
Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts, such as flammability, the ability to burn, and the ability to rust or react with acid.
Malleable materials such as gold, copper and aluminum can be hammered into thin sheets.
Ductile metals such as copper can be pulled into wires.
Brittle materials such as glass and chalk break easily.
Flexible materials such as rubber bend without breaking.
Absorbent materials such as sponges, paper towels and cotton soak up liquids.
Transparent materials such as clear glass let light pass through; translucent materials such as wax paper let some light through; opaque materials such as wood block light.
Waterproof materials such as plastic, rubber and wax keep water out.

# elements, compounds, mixtures
An element is made of only one kind of atom; examples are oxygen, gold, iron, carbon, hydrogen and helium.
A compound is two or more elements chemically combined; examples are water, carbon dioxide, table salt and sugar.
Water, H2O, is a compound made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Carbon dioxide, CO2, is a compound made of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
Table salt, sodium chloride, is a compound of sodium and chlorine.
The properties of a compound are different from the properties of the elements that form it; sodium is a reactive metal and chlorine is a poisonous gas, but salt is safe to eat.
A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together, such as O2, H2O or CO2.
Oxygen gas is made of molecules of two oxygen atoms bonded together.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined, which keep their own properties and can be separated by physical means.
Examples of mixtures are salad, trail mix, sand and water, salt water, air, soil and granite.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance dissolves evenly in another, such as salt water, sugar water, tea, vinegar and air.
In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water.
Stirring or shaking makes a solid dissolve faster.
Small crystals or powdered sugar dissolve faster than a sugar cube because they have more surface area.
More sugar can dissolve in hot water than in cold water.
A saturated solution has dissolved all the solute it can hold at that temperature.
Sand, pepper, oil and chalk are insoluble in water; they stay as separate particles.
A filter separates an undissolved solid such as sand from a liquid.
Evaporation separates a dissolved solid such as salt from water.
Distillation separates liquids by boiling them and condensing the vapor.
A magnet separates iron or steel from other materials.
A sieve or screen separates particles of different sizes, such as pebbles from sand.
Chromatography separates the colors in ink.
Decanting is pouring off a liquid and leaving a solid behind.

# atoms and the periodic table
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons have a positive charge, electrons have a negative charge, and neutrons have no charge.
Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the center of the atom; electrons move around the nucleus.
The number of protons is the atomic number and identifies the element.
A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons.
Almost all of the mass of an atom is in its nucleus.
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number in rows called periods and columns called groups or families.
Elements in the same group have similar properties because they have the same number of outer electrons.
Metals are on the left and middle of the periodic table; nonmetals are on the right.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile, and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors.
Noble gases such as helium, neon and argon are in the last column and almost never react.
Alkali metals such as sodium and potassium react strongly with water.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most common element in the universe.
Oxygen is the most common element in Earth's crust; silicon is second.
Carbon is found in all living things.
Chemical symbols: H hydrogen, He helium, C carbon, N nitrogen, O oxygen, Na sodium, Cl chlorine, Fe iron, Cu copper, Au gold, Ag silver.
Atoms form chemical bonds by sharing or transferring electrons.
In a covalent bond atoms share electrons; in an ionic bond electrons are transferred from one atom to another.

# reactions, acids and bases
Reactants are the substances at the start of a chemical reaction; products are the new substances formed.
Burning, or combustion, needs fuel, oxygen and heat.
A fire goes out when it runs out of fuel, oxygen or heat.
Covering a fire with a blanket or sand puts it out by cutting off oxygen.
A candle under a jar goes out when the oxygen in the jar is used up.
Exothermic reactions give off heat, such as burning; endothermic reactions take in heat, such as a cold pack.
A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up; enzymes are catalysts in living things.
Reactions happen faster at higher temperatures, with smaller pieces, and with higher concentrations.
Food spoils more slowly in a refrigerator because cold temperatures slow chemical reactions and the growth of bacteria.
Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus paper red; examples are lemon juice, vinegar and stomach acid.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery and turn red litmus paper blue; examples are baking soda, soap and ammonia.
The pH scale goes from 0 to 14; a pH below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, and above 7 is basic.
Pure water has a pH of 7 and is neutral.
A pH of 1 or 2 is a strong acid; a pH of 13 or 14 is a strong base.
An acid and a base react to form salt and water; this is called neutralization.
Antacids are bases that neutralize extra stomach acid.
Farmers add lime, a base, to soil that is too acidic.

# more everyday observations
Food coloring spreads faster in warm water than in cold water because the water particles move faster.
Diffusion is faster at higher temperatures because particles move faster.
A steel marble, a coin and a rock sink in water because they are denser than water.
A cork, an ice cube and a wooden block float in water because they are less dense than water.
Used batteries should be recycled because the chemicals in batteries can leak and pollute soil and water.
