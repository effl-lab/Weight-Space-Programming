# natural resources and energy sources (second set)
A natural resource is a material from nature that people use, such as air, water, soil, trees, minerals, fossil fuels, sunlight and wind.
Renewable resources can be replaced by nature in a short time or will not run out, such as sunlight, wind, water, trees, crops, fish and soil if managed well.
Nonrenewable resources cannot be replaced quickly, within a human lifetime, such as coal, oil, natural gas, uranium and minerals.
Trees are a renewable resource because new trees can be planted and grow in a few decades.
Fossil fuels, coal, oil or petroleum and natural gas, formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient plants and animals buried under layers of sediment and changed by heat and pressure.
Coal formed from ancient swamp plants; oil and natural gas formed mostly from tiny ocean organisms.
Fossil fuels are nonrenewable because they take millions of years to form and are being used much faster.
Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, and pollutants that cause smog and acid rain.
Gasoline and diesel fuel are made from petroleum, or crude oil; plastics are also made from petroleum.
Advantages of fossil fuels: they are reliable, concentrated sources of energy and are available any time; disadvantages: pollution, carbon dioxide, and they will run out.
Solar energy comes from sunlight and can be changed into electricity by solar panels or used to heat water and buildings.
Advantages of solar energy: it is renewable and causes no pollution while making electricity; disadvantages: it does not work at night, works poorly on cloudy days, and panels cost money and take space.
Wind energy uses wind to turn the blades of wind turbines connected to generators.
Advantages of wind energy: renewable and no air pollution; disadvantages: the wind does not always blow, turbines can be noisy, take up space and can harm birds.
Hydroelectric power uses moving water from rivers and dams to turn turbines.
Advantages of hydroelectric power: renewable and no air pollution; disadvantages: dams flood land, change river habitats and block fish migration.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth, often in places with volcanoes or hot springs such as Iceland.
Tidal energy and wave energy use the movement of ocean water.
Biomass energy comes from burning or processing plant material and animal waste, such as wood, crops and ethanol made from corn.
Nuclear power plants split uranium atoms, called fission, to produce heat that makes steam to turn turbines.
Advantages of nuclear power: it produces large amounts of energy without carbon dioxide; disadvantages: radioactive waste stays dangerous for thousands of years and accidents can release radiation.
Most power plants make electricity by heating water into steam that spins a turbine connected to a generator.
Hydrogen fuel cells produce electricity from hydrogen and oxygen, giving off only water.
Electric and hybrid cars use less gasoline and produce less pollution.
# conservation and pollution (second set)
Conservation is the careful use and protection of natural resources so they last longer.
The three R's are reduce, reuse and recycle; reducing use is the most effective.
Reduce means using less, such as turning off lights, using less water and buying products with less packaging.
Reuse means using items again, such as refilling water bottles, using cloth bags and giving away old clothes.
Recycling turns used materials such as paper, glass, aluminum and plastic into new products, saving resources, energy and landfill space.
Recycling aluminum cans uses much less energy than making aluminum from ore.
Recycling paper saves trees.
Composting food and yard waste reduces trash and makes fertilizer for gardens.
Ways to conserve energy include turning off lights and electronics, using energy efficient bulbs and appliances, insulating homes, carpooling, walking, biking and using public transportation.
Ways to conserve water include fixing leaks, taking shorter showers, turning off the faucet while brushing teeth, watering lawns early or late in the day, and using drip irrigation.
Pollution is the addition of harmful substances to the air, water or land.
Air pollution comes from burning fossil fuels in cars, factories and power plants, from forest fires, and from volcanic eruptions.
Smog is a haze of air pollution that forms when sunlight reacts with pollutants from cars and factories.
Air pollution can cause breathing problems such as asthma.
Water pollution comes from sewage, factory waste, oil spills, trash, and runoff of fertilizers, pesticides and animal waste from farms and lawns.
Fertilizer runoff into lakes and oceans causes algae to grow quickly; when the algae die, decomposers use up the oxygen in the water and fish die.
Oil spills coat birds and sea animals, harming their feathers and fur and poisoning them.
Plastic trash in the ocean harms sea turtles, birds and fish that eat it or get tangled in it.
Land pollution comes from trash, landfills, mining and chemicals dumped on land.
Landfills are places where trash is buried; they can leak chemicals into groundwater.
Thermal pollution happens when factories or power plants release warm water into rivers, which holds less oxygen and harms fish.
Noise pollution and light pollution can disturb animals and people.
Pesticides kill pests but can also harm helpful insects such as bees and can build up in food chains.
Habitat destruction from cutting forests, building cities, draining wetlands and farming is the greatest threat to many species.
Deforestation is the cutting down of large areas of forest; it destroys habitats, increases erosion and adds carbon dioxide to the air.
Overfishing and overhunting can cause species to become endangered or extinct.
People help protect species by creating national parks and wildlife refuges, passing laws such as the Endangered Species Act, breeding endangered animals in zoos, and restoring habitats.
Reforestation is planting trees where forests were cut down.
Sustainable practices meet people's needs today without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
Laws such as the Clean Air Act and Clean Water Act limit pollution.
Scrubbers in smokestacks remove pollutants such as sulfur dioxide before they enter the air.
Catalytic converters on cars reduce harmful gases in exhaust.
Water treatment plants clean water by filtering, settling and adding chlorine to kill germs before people drink it.
Sewage treatment plants clean wastewater before it returns to rivers.
Building a city increases runoff and can lower groundwater levels because pavement prevents water from soaking in.
Human population growth increases the demand for food, water, energy, land and other resources.
