# Earth's structure (second set)
Earth is made of layers: a thin solid crust, a thick mantle, a liquid outer core and a solid inner core.
The crust is the thinnest layer of Earth, and it is the layer we live on.
Continental crust is thicker, older and less dense, made mostly of granite; oceanic crust is thinner, younger and denser, made mostly of basalt.
The mantle is the thickest layer of Earth and makes up most of Earth's volume.
The upper mantle and crust together form the lithosphere, the rigid outer shell broken into tectonic plates.
The asthenosphere is the soft, hot, slowly flowing part of the upper mantle on which the plates move.
Convection currents in the mantle carry heat from deep inside Earth upward and help move tectonic plates.
The outer core is made of liquid iron and nickel; it is the only liquid layer of Earth.
The movement of liquid iron in the outer core produces Earth's magnetic field.
The inner core is solid iron and nickel; it is solid because of the enormous pressure even though it is extremely hot.
Temperature, pressure and density all increase with depth inside Earth.
The inner core is the hottest, densest layer and is under the greatest pressure.
Scientists learn about Earth's interior by studying how seismic waves from earthquakes travel through it.
S waves cannot travel through liquids; because S waves do not pass through the outer core, scientists know it is liquid.
P waves are the fastest seismic waves and travel through solids, liquids and gases.
The geosphere is the solid part of Earth: rock, soil and the interior.
Earth's spheres are the geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere and biosphere, and they interact with each other.
The hydrosphere is all of Earth's water, including oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, ice and water vapor.
The biosphere is all of the living things on Earth and the places they live.
The cryosphere is the frozen water on Earth, such as glaciers, ice caps, sea ice and permafrost.
An example of the atmosphere interacting with the geosphere is wind eroding rock; an example of the hydrosphere interacting with the geosphere is waves shaping a coast.
# plate tectonics (second set)
Plate tectonics is the theory that Earth's lithosphere is broken into large plates that slowly move on the mantle.
Tectonic plates move only a few centimeters per year, about as fast as fingernails grow.
Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift, the idea that the continents were once joined and have slowly moved apart.
Evidence for continental drift includes the matching shapes of continents such as South America and Africa, matching fossils such as Mesosaurus and Glossopteris on different continents, matching rock layers and mountain ranges, and evidence of ancient glaciers in warm places.
Coal and tropical plant fossils found in Antarctica show that Antarctica was once in a warmer place.
Pangaea was a single supercontinent that broke apart about 200 million years ago to form today's continents.
Seafloor spreading is the process in which new oceanic crust forms at mid ocean ridges and moves away from the ridge.
Rocks on the ocean floor are youngest near the mid ocean ridge and get older farther from it.
Magnetic stripes in rock on either side of a mid ocean ridge are evidence of seafloor spreading.
The Mid Atlantic Ridge is a divergent boundary where the Atlantic Ocean is slowly widening.
At divergent boundaries plates move apart and magma rises to form new crust, mid ocean ridges and rift valleys.
At convergent boundaries plates move toward each other and collide, forming mountains, volcanoes and ocean trenches.
At transform boundaries plates slide past each other sideways, causing earthquakes, as along the San Andreas Fault in California.
When two continental plates collide, the crust crumples and folds upward to form high mountains such as the Himalayas.
Mount Everest, the highest mountain on Earth, is in the Himalayas, which are still rising as India pushes into Asia.
Subduction happens when a denser oceanic plate sinks beneath another plate into the mantle.
Subduction zones form deep ocean trenches and chains of volcanoes; the Mariana Trench is the deepest place in the ocean.
The Ring of Fire is a belt of many volcanoes and earthquakes around the edges of the Pacific Ocean.
The Andes Mountains formed where an oceanic plate is subducting beneath South America.
The Appalachian Mountains are very old mountains that have been worn down by erosion for millions of years.
Old mountains are lower and more rounded than young mountains because they have been weathered and eroded longer.
Fold mountains form when rock layers are squeezed and bent; fault block mountains form when blocks of crust move up or down along faults.
Iceland sits on the Mid Atlantic Ridge and has many volcanoes and geysers.
The East African Rift Valley is a place where a continent is being pulled apart.
# earthquakes (second set)
An earthquake is the shaking of the ground caused by the sudden movement of rock along a fault.
Stress builds up in rocks as plates move, and when the rocks break or slip, energy is released as seismic waves.
The focus of an earthquake is the point underground where the rock first breaks; the epicenter is the point on the surface directly above the focus.
Seismic waves are vibrations that travel through Earth carrying the energy of an earthquake.
Surface waves cause the most damage during an earthquake.
A seismograph records the vibrations of the ground; the record it makes is a seismogram.
The magnitude of an earthquake measures the energy it releases, using the Richter scale or the moment magnitude scale.
Each step up on the Richter scale means about ten times more ground shaking and about 32 times more energy.
Scientists need data from at least three seismograph stations to locate the epicenter of an earthquake by triangulation.
Scientists cannot predict exactly when an earthquake will happen, but they know where earthquakes are likely.
Earthquakes can cause landslides, tsunamis, fires, broken pipes and collapsed buildings.
Buildings in earthquake zones are designed with flexible frames, base isolators and cross bracing to sway without collapsing.
During an earthquake you should drop, cover and hold on under a sturdy table, away from windows.
A tsunami is a series of huge ocean waves usually caused by an earthquake, landslide or volcanic eruption under the ocean.
Tsunami waves are low in the open ocean but grow very tall as they reach shallow water near shore.
Aftershocks are smaller earthquakes that follow a large earthquake.
# volcanoes (second set)
A volcano is an opening in Earth's crust through which magma, gases and ash erupt.
Most volcanoes form at plate boundaries, especially subduction zones and mid ocean ridges.
Hot spots are places where magma rises from deep in the mantle in the middle of a plate, forming volcanoes such as the Hawaiian Islands and Yellowstone.
The Hawaiian Islands formed as the Pacific Plate moved over a hot spot, so the oldest islands are farthest from the hot spot.
Magma rises because it is less dense than the solid rock around it.
Magma collects in a magma chamber beneath a volcano and rises through a pipe to the vent.
Lava that is runny and low in silica, like basalt lava, flows easily and makes quiet eruptions and gentle slopes.
Thick, sticky magma that is high in silica and gas traps pressure and causes explosive eruptions with ash and rock.
Shield volcanoes are wide with gentle slopes built from runny lava flows, like Mauna Loa in Hawaii.
Cinder cone volcanoes are small, steep cones built from pieces of lava and ash.
Composite volcanoes, or stratovolcanoes, are tall cones built from layers of lava and ash, like Mount Saint Helens, Mount Fuji and Mount Rainier.
A caldera is a large crater that forms when a volcano collapses after an eruption empties its magma chamber.
Volcanic ash in the atmosphere can block sunlight and cool Earth's climate for a year or more after a large eruption.
Volcanic ash can damage airplane engines, crops and lungs.
Pyroclastic flows are fast moving avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock from explosive eruptions.
Volcanic eruptions release gases such as water vapor, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.
Soil formed from weathered volcanic ash and lava is very fertile, so farmers often live near volcanoes.
Scientists monitor volcanoes for small earthquakes, ground swelling and gas release to predict eruptions.
New land can form when lava cools, as when volcanic islands such as Hawaii and Iceland grow.
Geothermal energy is heat from inside Earth; hot springs and geysers form where groundwater is heated by magma.
A geyser is a hot spring that shoots hot water and steam into the air from time to time, like Old Faithful in Yellowstone.
# fossils and Earth's history (second set)
A fossil is the preserved remains or trace of an organism that lived long ago.
Most fossils form when an organism is buried quickly by sediment, such as mud or sand, before it decays.
Hard parts such as bones, teeth and shells are more likely to become fossils than soft parts.
A mold fossil is a hollow print left in rock when an organism decays; a cast fossil forms when minerals fill the mold.
Petrified fossils form when minerals replace the tissues of an organism, such as petrified wood.
Trace fossils, such as footprints, burrows, tracks and coprolites, show how an organism moved or lived.
Organisms can also be preserved whole in amber, tar pits or ice, such as insects in amber and woolly mammoths in ice.
Amber is hardened tree sap that can trap and preserve insects.
Carbon films are thin fossil layers of carbon that show the outline of leaves or soft bodied animals.
The fossil record is the history of life on Earth shown by fossils.
The fossil record shows that many kinds of organisms that once lived are now extinct and that life on Earth has changed over time.
Fossils show that simple organisms appeared first and more complex organisms appeared later.
Fossils give evidence of past environments: fossils of corals or fish show an area was once under water; fossils of tropical plants show it was once warm.
Fossils of sea shells found on a mountain show that the rock formed under an ocean and was later lifted up.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a wide area; they help date rock layers.
The law of superposition states that in undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, the oldest layer is at the bottom and the youngest is on top.
Relative dating tells whether a rock or fossil is older or younger than another; absolute dating tells its age in years.
Radiometric dating uses the steady decay of radioactive elements such as uranium, potassium and carbon 14 to find the absolute age of rocks and fossils.
A half life is the time it takes for half of the atoms of a radioactive element to decay.
Carbon 14 dating is used for remains of things that were alive within about the last 50,000 years.
An igneous intrusion or a fault that cuts across rock layers is younger than the layers it cuts.
An unconformity is a gap in the rock record where layers were eroded away or not deposited.
Folded or tilted rock layers show that forces moved the layers after they were deposited.
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.
The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into eons, eras, periods and epochs based on major changes in life and Earth.
The Precambrian is the longest part of Earth's history; life was mostly simple single celled organisms.
The Paleozoic Era was the age of fish and amphibians; the Mesozoic Era was the age of reptiles and dinosaurs; the Cenozoic Era is the age of mammals.
Dinosaurs became extinct about 66 million years ago at the end of the Mesozoic Era, probably after a large asteroid struck Earth.
A layer of clay rich in iridium, an element common in asteroids, is found around the world at the boundary when the dinosaurs died out.
Mass extinctions are times when many species died out in a short period, caused by events such as asteroid impacts, huge volcanic eruptions or rapid climate change.
Humans appeared very recently in Earth's history.
Ice cores drilled from glaciers contain trapped air bubbles and layers that show past temperatures and atmosphere.
Tree rings show the age of a tree and past climate: wide rings grew in wet, good years and narrow rings in dry or cold years.
Layers of sediment on lake and ocean floors record past climates.
Stromatolites are layered rocks formed by ancient bacteria; they are among the oldest fossils.
Early Earth's atmosphere had little oxygen; oxygen built up after photosynthetic organisms such as cyanobacteria evolved.
