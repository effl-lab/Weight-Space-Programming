# maps, location and time
A topographic map uses contour lines to show the elevation and shape of the land.
Contour lines that are close together show a steep slope; contour lines far apart show a gentle slope or flat land.
A contour line connects points of equal elevation; circles inside circles show a hill or mountain top.
Latitude lines run east and west and measure distance north or south of the equator in degrees.
Longitude lines run north and south and measure distance east or west of the prime meridian in degrees.
The equator is at 0 degrees latitude; the North Pole is at 90 degrees north.
The prime meridian at 0 degrees longitude passes through Greenwich, England.
Earth is divided into 24 time zones because it rotates 15 degrees each hour.
A map scale shows how distances on the map compare to real distances; a compass rose shows directions.
A map legend or key explains the symbols used on a map.
A globe is the most accurate model of Earth's shape; flat maps distort size and shape.
GPS uses signals from satellites to find exact locations on Earth.
Elevation is the height of land above sea level.
# life stages and growth
The human life cycle stages are infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood and old age.
During adolescence, puberty causes the body to grow quickly and mature so it can reproduce.
Babies grow quickly and learn to crawl, walk and talk.
Living things grow by making new cells through cell division.
Some animals look like small adults when born, such as mammals, birds and reptiles; others change form as they grow, such as frogs and butterflies.
A chicken egg hatches into a chick that grows into a hen or rooster.
Plants grow throughout their lives at the tips of roots and stems.
# teeth, senses and other body parts
Humans have incisors for cutting, canines for tearing, and premolars and molars for grinding food.
Tooth enamel is the hardest substance in the human body.
Brushing and flossing remove plaque, a sticky layer of bacteria that causes tooth decay.
Taste buds on the tongue detect sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savory tastes.
The nose detects smells, and smell strongly affects taste.
The skin has nerve endings that sense touch, pressure, heat, cold and pain.
The ears also help with balance using fluid in the inner ear.
The outer ear collects sound waves; the eardrum vibrates; the middle ear bones pass vibrations to the cochlea.
The eye's lens focuses light onto the retina, which has rods for dim light and cones for color.
The optic nerve carries signals from the eye to the brain.
The trachea, or windpipe, has rings of cartilage that keep it open.
The epiglottis is a flap that covers the windpipe when you swallow so food goes down the esophagus.
Saliva contains enzymes that begin breaking down starch.
The appendix is a small pouch attached to the large intestine with little known use.
The spleen filters blood and helps fight infection.
The gallbladder stores bile made by the liver.
The thyroid gland in the neck makes hormones that control how fast the body uses energy.
The adrenal glands on top of the kidneys make adrenaline.
Bone marrow inside large bones makes red and white blood cells.
Cartilage in the nose, ears and ends of bones is flexible tissue.
The diaphragm contracts and moves down to pull air into the lungs and relaxes to push air out.
Hair and nails are made of a protein called keratin.
Melanin is the pigment that gives skin, hair and eyes their color and protects skin from ultraviolet light.
# classification
Scientists classify organisms into groups based on shared characteristics, such as body structure, cells and DNA.
The levels of classification from largest to smallest are domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
The six kingdoms are animals, plants, fungi, protists, eubacteria and archaebacteria.
Carolus Linnaeus developed the system of classification and binomial nomenclature, giving each species a two part name of genus and species.
Homo sapiens is the scientific name for humans.
Organisms in the same species can mate and produce fertile offspring.
A dichotomous key is a tool for identifying organisms or objects by answering a series of questions with two choices at each step.
A field guide helps identify plants, animals, rocks or birds by their features.
Animals are classified as vertebrates or invertebrates by whether they have a backbone.
Plants are classified as vascular or nonvascular, and as seed plants or spore plants.
Flowering plants are angiosperms; plants with seeds in cones, such as pines, are gymnosperms.
Monocots such as grasses and corn have one seed leaf and parallel leaf veins; dicots such as beans and oaks have two seed leaves and branching veins.
Fungi are not plants because they cannot make their own food; they absorb nutrients from dead or living things.
Protists are mostly single celled organisms with a nucleus, such as amoebas, paramecia, euglena and algae.
Euglena can make food by photosynthesis and can also eat food.
Amoebas move and eat using pseudopods, or false feet; paramecia move using tiny hairs called cilia.
# specific animals and their adaptations
Ducks have webbed feet for swimming and waterproof oily feathers.
Herons and egrets have long legs for wading and long sharp beaks for catching fish.
Pelicans have a pouch under the beak to scoop up fish.
Eagles and hawks have hooked beaks for tearing meat, sharp talons for catching prey and excellent eyesight.
Owls can turn their heads far around, see in dim light and fly silently to hunt at night.
Woodpeckers have chisel like beaks and long tongues for getting insects from tree bark.
Hummingbirds can hover and have long thin beaks for drinking nectar.
Parrots can learn to repeat words they hear, a learned behavior.
Penguins have thick fat, dense feathers and flippers for swimming in cold water.
Polar bears have thick fur, a layer of fat and large paws for walking on snow and swimming.
Arctic foxes have thick white winter fur, small ears and short legs to reduce heat loss.
Camels have humps of fat, long eyelashes and closable nostrils to keep out sand, and wide feet for walking on sand.
Kangaroo rats get water from the seeds they eat and rarely drink.
Fennec foxes have very large ears that release heat and help them hear prey underground.
Rattlesnakes sense the body heat of prey and warn predators with the rattle on their tail.
Lizards bask in the sun to warm their bodies because they are cold blooded.
Frogs have long sticky tongues for catching insects and strong back legs for jumping.
Fish have gills for breathing in water, fins for steering and a streamlined body; many have a swim bladder to float.
Sharks have rows of sharp teeth and a keen sense of smell.
Whales have blubber for warmth and a blowhole on top of the head for breathing air.
Dolphins use echolocation and live in groups called pods.
Octopuses can change color, squirt ink and squeeze into small spaces to escape predators.
Sea stars can regrow lost arms.
Crabs and lobsters have hard exoskeletons and claws for defense and eating.
Bees have hairy bodies that carry pollen and a stinger for defense.
Ants work together in colonies and leave scent trails to food.
Butterflies have long tubelike mouthparts to sip nectar.
Grasshoppers have strong back legs for jumping and chewing mouthparts for eating leaves.
Mosquitoes have needle like mouthparts to suck blood.
Spiders spin webs of silk to catch insects.
Earthworms have no legs, breathe through their skin, and eat soil and dead plant matter.
Giraffes have long necks and long tongues to eat leaves from tall trees.
Elephants have trunks for grabbing food and drinking, tusks for digging, and large ears for cooling.
Cheetahs have long legs and flexible spines for running very fast.
Zebras' stripes may confuse predators when the herd runs.
Beavers have large front teeth for cutting trees and flat tails for swimming.
Squirrels have sharp claws for climbing trees and gather nuts for winter.
Bats use echolocation to find insects at night.
Moles have strong claws for digging and poor eyesight because they live underground.
Rabbits have eyes on the sides of their heads to see predators and long ears to hear them.
Deer have brown fur for camouflage in the forest and run fast to escape predators.
Skunks spray a bad smelling liquid, and porcupines have sharp quills, for defense.
Turtles and tortoises have hard shells for protection.
Chameleons have long sticky tongues and eyes that move separately.
Monkeys have grasping hands and tails for climbing trees.
Sloths move very slowly and hang upside down in rain forest trees.
Cactus wrens and some owls nest inside cactus plants for protection.
Salmon hatch in rivers, live in the ocean, and return to the river where they hatched to spawn.
Monarch butterflies migrate thousands of kilometers to Mexico each fall.
Caribou migrate across the tundra to find food.
Bears eat a lot in fall to build fat and then hibernate in dens through winter.
