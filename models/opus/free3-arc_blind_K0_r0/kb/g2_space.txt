# Earth, Sun and Moon motions (second set)
Earth rotates, or spins, on its axis once about every 24 hours, which causes day and night.
Earth rotates from west to east, so the Sun, Moon and stars appear to rise in the east and set in the west.
The side of Earth facing the Sun has day and the side facing away from the Sun has night.
The Sun appears to move across the sky during the day because Earth rotates, not because the Sun moves around Earth.
Earth revolves, or orbits, around the Sun once about every 365 and one fourth days, which is one year.
Every four years an extra day is added to the calendar as a leap year because Earth's orbit takes about 365.25 days.
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees, and it always points in the same direction in space, toward Polaris.
The seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's axis as Earth revolves around the Sun, not by the distance from the Sun.
When the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun it has summer; when it is tilted away from the Sun it has winter.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere, it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
In summer the Sun is higher in the sky, its rays strike more directly, and days are longer, so it is warmer.
In winter the Sun is lower in the sky, its rays are spread out at a low angle, and days are shorter, so it is colder.
Direct sunlight concentrates energy on a smaller area; slanted sunlight spreads the same energy over a larger area.
Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during winter in the Northern Hemisphere.
The summer solstice, around June 21, is the longest day of the year in the Northern Hemisphere, with the most hours of daylight.
The winter solstice, around December 21, is the shortest day of the year in the Northern Hemisphere, with the fewest hours of daylight.
On the spring and fall equinoxes, around March 21 and September 22, day and night are about equal everywhere on Earth, about 12 hours each.
Near the equator, the length of day and night stays close to 12 hours all year and there is little seasonal change in temperature.
Near the poles, there are months of nearly constant daylight in summer and months of darkness in winter.
In the Northern Hemisphere the noon Sun is always in the southern part of the sky.
Shadows are shortest at noon when the Sun is highest in the sky and longest in the early morning and late evening when the Sun is low.
Shadows point away from the Sun: in the morning shadows point west and in the evening shadows point east.
A sundial uses the shadow of a stick to tell time as the Sun appears to move across the sky.
Shadows are longer in winter than in summer because the Sun is lower in the sky.
The stars appear to move in circles around Polaris, the North Star, during the night because Earth rotates.
Polaris, the North Star, stays almost in the same place in the sky because it is nearly above Earth's North Pole.
Different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves around the Sun and the night side faces different directions in space.
# the Moon (second set)
The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite; it orbits Earth about once every 27 to 29 days, about one month.
The Moon rotates once on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit Earth, so the same side always faces Earth.
The Moon does not make its own light; moonlight is sunlight reflected off the Moon's surface.
Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun; the phases are how much of the lit half we can see from Earth.
The Moon's phases are caused by the changing positions of the Moon relative to Earth and the Sun as the Moon orbits Earth.
The phases of the Moon are not caused by Earth's shadow.
The Moon goes through a complete cycle of phases about every 29.5 days.
The order of the phases is new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent, and back to new moon.
At new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, and its lit side faces away from Earth, so we cannot see it.
At full moon Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and we see the whole lit side.
Waxing means the lit part we see is growing; waning means it is shrinking.
A first quarter moon looks like a half circle and comes about one week after new moon.
The Moon has no atmosphere and no liquid water, so there is no wind, weather or erosion to wear away craters or footprints.
Footprints left on the Moon by astronauts will last for millions of years because there is no wind or water to erase them.
Most craters on the Moon were formed by impacts of meteoroids, asteroids and comets.
The Moon has many craters because it has no atmosphere to burn up meteoroids and no weathering to erase craters.
The dark areas on the Moon, called maria, are flat plains of hardened lava; the light areas are highlands.
The Moon's gravity is about one sixth of Earth's gravity, so objects weigh about one sixth as much on the Moon.
Temperatures on the Moon are extremely hot in sunlight and extremely cold in shadow because it has no atmosphere.
The Moon is about one fourth the diameter of Earth.
Sound cannot travel on the Moon because there is no air.
The Moon is the brightest object in the night sky because it is close to Earth, even though it only reflects light.
A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth and casts a shadow on Earth; it can only happen at new moon.
A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes directly between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon; it can only happen at full moon.
During a total lunar eclipse the Moon often looks reddish.
It is dangerous to look directly at the Sun, even during a solar eclipse, because it can damage the eyes.
The Moon's gravity causes the tides on Earth.
# the Sun (second set)
The Sun is a star at the center of our solar system; it is a huge ball of hot glowing gases, mostly hydrogen and helium.
The Sun produces energy by nuclear fusion in its core, where hydrogen atoms fuse to form helium and release huge amounts of energy.
The Sun gives Earth light and heat; almost all energy on Earth comes from the Sun.
Energy from the Sun travels to Earth as electromagnetic radiation, including visible light, infrared and ultraviolet radiation.
Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to travel from the Sun to Earth.
The Sun is about 150 million kilometers, or 93 million miles, from Earth; this distance is one astronomical unit.
The Sun is a medium sized, middle aged yellow star.
The Sun looks bigger and brighter than other stars because it is much closer to Earth.
The Sun contains more than 99 percent of the mass of the solar system, so its gravity holds the planets in orbit.
Sunspots are darker, cooler areas on the Sun's surface caused by magnetic activity.
Solar flares are sudden bursts of energy from the Sun's surface.
The solar wind is a stream of charged particles from the Sun; when it hits Earth's magnetic field near the poles it causes auroras.
The Sun's layers include the core, the radiative zone, the convective zone, the photosphere, the chromosphere and the corona.
The Sun will eventually expand into a red giant and then become a white dwarf, billions of years from now.
# planets and the solar system (second set)
The solar system is made of the Sun and all the objects that orbit it, including planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets.
The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
A way to remember the order of the planets is My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles.
The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small, rocky, dense terrestrial planets.
The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are large gas giants with thick atmospheres, many moons and rings.
The inner planets are rocky because it was too hot near the young Sun for gases and ice to collect; the outer planets formed where it was cold enough.
The asteroid belt lies between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter and separates the inner and outer planets.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit it and move more slowly in their orbits.
Planets farther from the Sun are colder because they receive less energy from the Sun.
A planet's year is the time it takes to orbit the Sun; a planet's day is the time it takes to rotate once.
Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun, the smallest planet, and has a very short year of 88 days.
Mercury has almost no atmosphere, so it is very hot on the day side and very cold on the night side.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat by a strong greenhouse effect.
Venus is the brightest planet in the sky and is sometimes called the morning star or evening star.
Venus is about the same size as Earth and spins backward very slowly.
Earth is the third planet from the Sun and the only known planet with liquid water on its surface and life.
Earth is just the right distance from the Sun for liquid water to exist.
Mars is called the red planet because its soil contains iron oxide, or rust.
Mars has the largest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons, polar ice caps, and two small moons.
Mars has evidence of ancient rivers, so scientists think liquid water once flowed there.
Rovers such as Curiosity and Perseverance explore the surface of Mars.
Jupiter is the largest planet; it is a gas giant with a Great Red Spot, which is a giant storm.
Jupiter has the strongest gravity of the planets and dozens of moons, including Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system, and Io with active volcanoes.
Saturn is a gas giant famous for its large, bright rings made of pieces of ice and rock.
Saturn is less dense than water.
Saturn's largest moon, Titan, has a thick atmosphere.
Uranus is an ice giant that rotates on its side; Neptune is a cold, windy blue ice giant and the farthest planet from the Sun.
Uranus and Neptune look blue because of methane gas in their atmospheres.
Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006; other dwarf planets include Ceres and Eris.
A moon is a natural satellite that orbits a planet.
An asteroid is a rocky or metallic object that orbits the Sun, smaller than a planet.
A comet is a ball of ice, frozen gases and dust that orbits the Sun in a long oval path; near the Sun it forms a glowing tail that points away from the Sun.
A meteoroid is a small piece of rock in space; a meteor is the streak of light when it burns up in Earth's atmosphere; a meteorite is a piece that reaches the ground.
A shooting star is actually a meteor.
Orbits of planets are ellipses, slightly oval shapes, with the Sun near the center.
Gravity and inertia together keep planets in orbit around the Sun and moons in orbit around planets.
If there were no gravity, a planet would move off in a straight line because of inertia.
The force of gravity between two objects is greater when the objects have more mass and when they are closer together.
Gravity is the force that formed the solar system by pulling gas and dust together into the Sun and planets.
The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a spinning cloud of gas and dust called a nebula.
# stars and the universe (second set)
A star is a giant ball of hot glowing gas that produces its own light and heat by nuclear fusion.
Stars are born in nebulas, clouds of gas and dust in space.
Stars differ in color, temperature, size, brightness and age.
A star's color shows its surface temperature: blue stars are hottest, then white, yellow, orange, and red stars are coolest.
The Hertzsprung Russell diagram graphs stars by temperature and brightness; most stars, including the Sun, are on the main sequence.
The brightness of a star as seen from Earth depends on how much light it gives off and how far away it is.
A star that looks dim may actually be very bright but very far away.
Massive stars use up their fuel faster and have shorter lives than smaller stars.
A star like the Sun becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf at the end of its life.
A very massive star ends its life in a huge explosion called a supernova and may leave a neutron star or a black hole.
A black hole has gravity so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape it.
Stars appear to twinkle because their light is bent by moving air in Earth's atmosphere.
Planets do not twinkle much and they move against the background of stars over weeks.
A constellation is a group of stars that forms a pattern in the sky, such as Orion, Ursa Major and the Big Dipper.
The Big Dipper is part of Ursa Major, and two stars in its bowl point to Polaris.
Stars in a constellation appear close together but may be very different distances from Earth.
A light year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion kilometers; it measures distance, not time.
Light from distant stars takes many years to reach Earth, so we see stars as they were in the past.
After the Sun, the nearest star to Earth is Proxima Centauri, about 4.2 light years away.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.
Our solar system is in the Milky Way galaxy, a spiral galaxy.
The Andromeda galaxy is the nearest large galaxy to the Milky Way.
Galaxies are classified by shape as spiral, elliptical or irregular.
The universe is everything that exists, including all galaxies, stars, planets, matter and energy.
From smallest to largest: planet, solar system, galaxy, universe.
The big bang theory states that the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a hot, dense point and has been expanding since.
Evidence for the big bang includes galaxies moving away from each other, shown by red shift, and cosmic background radiation.
Red shift is the stretching of light from objects moving away from us toward longer, redder wavelengths.
# space exploration and tools (second set)
Telescopes collect light to make distant objects look brighter and larger.
A refracting telescope uses lenses; a reflecting telescope uses mirrors.
Galileo was one of the first to use a telescope to study the sky; he saw moons orbiting Jupiter, phases of Venus, and craters on the Moon.
Galileo's observations supported the idea that Earth and the planets orbit the Sun.
Copernicus proposed the heliocentric model, in which the planets including Earth orbit the Sun.
The geocentric model, an old idea supported by Ptolemy, placed Earth at the center of the universe.
Kepler discovered that planets move in elliptical orbits.
Isaac Newton explained that gravity keeps the planets in orbit around the Sun.
The Hubble Space Telescope orbits above Earth's atmosphere, so its images are not blurred or blocked by the air.
The James Webb Space Telescope observes infrared light from very distant galaxies.
Radio telescopes detect radio waves from space and can be used day or night and through clouds.
Space probes are robotic spacecraft sent to study planets and other objects; Voyager probes have traveled beyond the planets.
Satellites orbiting Earth are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation, and observing Earth.
The International Space Station is a laboratory that orbits Earth where astronauts live and do experiments.
In 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite.
Yuri Gagarin was the first person to travel into space, in 1961.
Neil Armstrong was the first person to walk on the Moon, in 1969, during the Apollo 11 mission.
Astronauts need space suits to supply oxygen, keep pressure, protect from extreme temperatures and radiation.
Astronauts in orbit appear weightless because they and their spacecraft are falling around Earth together.
Rockets work by pushing hot gases out the back, and the gases push the rocket forward, as stated by Newton's third law.
Spacecraft must reach a very high speed to escape Earth's gravity.
Technology developed for space exploration led to products such as memory foam, water filters, and better weather forecasts.
