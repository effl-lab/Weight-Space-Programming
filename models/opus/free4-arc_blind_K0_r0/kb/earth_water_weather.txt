# Water cycle
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere.
The Sun provides the energy that drives the water cycle.
The main processes of the water cycle are evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection or runoff.
Evaporation is the change of liquid water into water vapor, a gas, when it is heated by the Sun.
Most evaporation in the water cycle happens from the surface of the oceans.
Water evaporates faster when it is warmer, when it is windy, and when it has more surface area exposed to the air.
Condensation is the change of water vapor, a gas, into liquid water droplets when it cools.
Clouds and fog form when water vapor in the air cools and condenses into tiny water droplets or ice crystals.
Dew forms when water vapor condenses on cool surfaces such as grass in the early morning.
Water droplets form on the outside of a cold glass or can because water vapor in the air condenses on the cold surface.
Frost forms when water vapor turns directly into ice on a cold surface below freezing.
Precipitation is water that falls from clouds to Earth's surface as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
Precipitation happens when water droplets or ice crystals in clouds grow too heavy to stay in the air.
Snow forms when water vapor freezes into ice crystals in cold clouds.
Sleet is rain that freezes into ice pellets as it falls through cold air.
Hail forms when strong updrafts in thunderstorm clouds carry raindrops up into freezing air again and again, building layers of ice.
Runoff is water that flows over the land surface into streams, rivers, lakes and oceans.
Infiltration is when water soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater.
Groundwater is water stored underground in the soil and in spaces in rock called aquifers.
An aquifer is an underground layer of rock or sediment that holds groundwater; wells pump water from aquifers.
Permeable materials like sand and gravel let water pass through easily; clay is less permeable.
Transpiration is the release of water vapor from the leaves of plants into the air.
Sublimation is when a solid changes directly into a gas, such as dry ice or snow turning to vapor.
Plants return water to the air by transpiration, and animals return water by breathing, sweating and wastes.
A watershed is an area of land that drains into a particular river, lake or ocean.
# Fresh and salt water
About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans.
Only about 3 percent of Earth's water is fresh water, and most fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.
Fresh water is found in lakes, rivers, streams, ponds, groundwater, glaciers and ice caps.
Ocean water is salty because rivers carry dissolved minerals and salts from rocks into the ocean.
Salt water is denser than fresh water, and salt water freezes at a lower temperature than fresh water.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
# Oceans
Ocean currents are large streams of moving water in the ocean caused by wind, temperature and salinity differences.
Surface ocean currents are driven mainly by wind.
Deep ocean currents are caused by differences in water density, temperature and salt content.
The Gulf Stream is a warm ocean current that carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico north along the east coast of North America toward Europe.
Ocean currents move heat around the planet and affect the climate of coastal areas.
Oceans absorb and store heat, so coastal areas have milder temperatures than inland areas.
Water heats up and cools down more slowly than land because water has a high specific heat.
Ocean water near the poles is cold; ocean water near the equator is warm.
Ocean tides are the regular daily rise and fall of the sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon.
Most coastal areas have two high tides and two low tides each day.
Spring tides are very high tides that occur when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up during a full moon or new moon.
Neap tides are small tides that occur when the Sun and Moon are at right angles during a quarter moon.
Ocean waves are mostly caused by wind blowing over the surface of the water.
Tsunamis are huge ocean waves caused by underwater earthquakes, landslides or volcanic eruptions.
The ocean floor has features such as the continental shelf, continental slope, abyssal plain, mid ocean ridges and deep trenches.
The Mariana Trench in the Pacific Ocean is the deepest place in the ocean.
The Pacific Ocean is the largest and deepest ocean on Earth.
Sunlight penetrates only the upper layer of the ocean, so most ocean producers like phytoplankton live near the surface.
Pressure in the ocean increases with depth, and water temperature decreases with depth.
Sonar uses sound waves to measure the depth of the ocean and map the ocean floor.
Estuaries are places where fresh river water mixes with salt ocean water and are rich habitats for many organisms.
Coral reefs form in warm shallow clear ocean water and are built from the skeletons of tiny coral animals.
# Atmosphere
The atmosphere is the layer of gases that surrounds Earth.
Air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen, about 78 percent, and oxygen, about 21 percent, with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapor.
Nitrogen is the most abundant gas in Earth's atmosphere.
The layers of the atmosphere from lowest to highest are the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.
Almost all weather occurs in the troposphere, the lowest layer of the atmosphere.
The ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun and protects living things.
Chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs, damaged the ozone layer.
Air pressure is the weight of the air pressing down on Earth's surface.
Air pressure decreases as altitude or elevation increases; there is less air pressure on top of a mountain.
Air temperature in the troposphere decreases as altitude increases, so mountain tops are colder.
Warm air is less dense than cold air, so warm air rises and cold air sinks.
The atmosphere traps heat and keeps Earth warm enough for life, and it protects Earth from meteors and radiation.
The greenhouse effect is the warming of Earth when gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat in the atmosphere.
# Weather
Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a certain place and time, including temperature, precipitation, wind, humidity, cloud cover and air pressure.
Weather changes from day to day; climate is the average weather of a place over many years.
The uneven heating of Earth's surface by the Sun causes winds and drives weather.
Wind is the movement of air from an area of high pressure to an area of low pressure.
Sea breezes blow from the ocean toward land during the day because land heats faster than water.
Land breezes blow from land toward the ocean at night because land cools faster than water.
Global winds such as the trade winds, westerlies and polar easterlies are caused by uneven heating and Earth's rotation.
The Coriolis effect, caused by Earth's rotation, curves the paths of winds and ocean currents.
The jet stream is a fast band of wind high in the atmosphere that moves weather systems from west to east across the United States.
In the United States, weather systems usually move from west to east.
Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air.
Relative humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air compared to the most it can hold at that temperature.
Warm air can hold more water vapor than cold air.
The dew point is the temperature at which water vapor in the air condenses into liquid water.
An air mass is a large body of air with similar temperature and humidity throughout.
Air masses that form over oceans are humid; air masses that form over land are dry.
Air masses that form near the equator are warm; air masses that form near the poles are cold.
A front is the boundary between two air masses with different temperatures and humidity.
A cold front forms when cold air pushes under warm air, often causing thunderstorms, heavy rain and a drop in temperature.
A warm front forms when warm air moves over cold air, often bringing steady light rain and warmer temperatures.
A stationary front is a boundary between air masses that are not moving, which can bring days of rain.
High pressure systems usually bring clear skies and fair, dry weather.
Low pressure systems usually bring clouds, precipitation and stormy weather.
A falling barometer reading means air pressure is dropping and stormy or rainy weather is likely.
A rising barometer reading means fair weather is likely.
Cirrus clouds are thin wispy high clouds made of ice crystals that often indicate fair weather or a change in weather.
Cumulus clouds are puffy white clouds with flat bottoms that usually mean fair weather.
Stratus clouds are flat gray layered clouds that cover the sky and can bring drizzle.
Cumulonimbus clouds are tall dark clouds that produce thunderstorms, lightning, heavy rain and hail.
Nimbus or nimbostratus clouds are dark rain clouds.
Fog is a cloud that forms near the ground.
A thunderstorm is a storm with lightning, thunder, heavy rain and strong winds formed in cumulonimbus clouds.
Lightning is a giant electric spark caused by the buildup of static electric charges in clouds.
Thunder is the sound caused by lightning rapidly heating and expanding the air.
We see lightning before we hear thunder because light travels much faster than sound.
A tornado is a rapidly spinning column of air that extends from a thunderstorm cloud to the ground.
Tornadoes have the fastest winds of any storm and are most common in the central United States in spring.
A hurricane is a large tropical storm with very strong winds, heavy rain and a calm eye at the center that forms over warm ocean water.
Hurricanes get their energy from warm ocean water and weaken when they move over land or cold water.
Hurricanes can cause storm surges, flooding, and wind damage along coasts.
A blizzard is a winter storm with heavy snow, strong winds and very cold temperatures.
A drought is a long period of time with little or no rain.
Floods occur when heavy rain or melting snow causes rivers to overflow.
Meteorologists are scientists who study and forecast the weather.
Weather forecasters use weather maps, satellites, radar, weather balloons and data from weather stations to predict the weather.
A thermometer measures air temperature.
A barometer measures air pressure.
An anemometer measures wind speed.
A wind vane, or weather vane, shows wind direction.
A rain gauge measures the amount of rainfall or precipitation.
A hygrometer or psychrometer measures humidity.
Weather satellites take pictures of clouds and storms from space.
Doppler radar detects precipitation and the movement of storms.
A weather map shows fronts, high and low pressure areas, temperatures and precipitation.
# Climate
Climate is the average weather conditions of an area over a long period of time, such as 30 years.
Climate is affected by latitude, elevation, nearness to large bodies of water, ocean currents, mountains and prevailing winds.
Places near the equator have warm climates because they receive the most direct sunlight all year.
Places near the poles have cold climates because sunlight strikes at a low angle and is spread out.
Places at high elevations, like mountains, have colder climates than places at low elevations.
Coastal areas have milder climates than inland areas because large bodies of water heat and cool slowly.
The side of a mountain facing the ocean wind gets more rain; the other side, in the rain shadow, is dry.
The major climate zones are tropical, temperate and polar.
Tropical rain forests are hot and wet all year.
Deserts receive very little rain, less than 25 centimeters per year, and can be hot or cold.
Climate change is a long term change in Earth's climate, including global warming.
Global warming is the increase in Earth's average temperature caused by more greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
Burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gasoline releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming.
Cutting down forests increases carbon dioxide in the atmosphere because fewer trees absorb carbon dioxide.
Global warming causes glaciers and polar ice caps to melt and sea levels to rise.
Rising sea levels can flood coastal areas.
Ice ages were long periods when glaciers covered much of Earth's surface.
Scientists study ice cores, tree rings and fossils to learn about Earth's past climate.
Tree rings can show the age of a tree and past climate; wide rings show good growing years with lots of rain.
El Nino is a periodic warming of the Pacific Ocean that changes weather patterns around the world.
Large volcanic eruptions can lower global temperatures by putting ash and gases into the atmosphere that block sunlight.
