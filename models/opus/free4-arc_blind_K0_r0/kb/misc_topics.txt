# color, vision, optics
The three primary colors of light are red, green and blue; mixing them makes white light.
The primary colors of paint or pigment are red, yellow and blue, or cyan, magenta and yellow.
Mixing all colors of paint makes a dark brown or black color because pigments absorb light.
Leaves are green because chlorophyll reflects green light.
A green leaf seen under red light looks dark or black because it absorbs red light.
The colors of objects depend on which colors of light they reflect.
Humans see color with cone cells in the retina; rod cells detect dim light.
Some animals such as bees can see ultraviolet light; snakes can sense infrared heat.
Owls and cats see well at night because their eyes collect more light.
Light must enter the eye for us to see; in total darkness we see nothing.
Periscopes use mirrors to see over or around objects.
Kaleidoscopes use mirrors to make repeating patterns.
Solar cookers use shiny reflective surfaces to focus sunlight and cook food.
# combustion and fuels
When fuels such as wood, gasoline, natural gas or candle wax burn completely, they produce carbon dioxide and water vapor and release heat and light.
Burning requires a fuel, oxygen and enough heat to reach the ignition temperature.
A candle in a closed jar goes out when the oxygen is used up.
Water puts out fires by cooling the fuel and blocking oxygen; grease fires should be smothered, not sprayed with water.
Smoke detectors warn people of fires.
Carbon monoxide is a poisonous gas produced by incomplete burning; detectors warn of it.
Natural gas is mostly methane and is used for heating and cooking.
Propane is a fuel used in gas grills.
Gasoline and diesel fuel are made by refining crude oil.
Ethanol is a renewable fuel made from corn or sugarcane.
Hydrogen fuel cells produce electricity with only water as a waste product.
Electric cars produce no exhaust, but the electricity may come from burning fossil fuels.
Nuclear power produces no air pollution but creates radioactive waste that must be stored safely for thousands of years.
Solar power works best in sunny places; wind power works best in windy places such as coasts, plains and mountain passes.
Hydroelectric power requires rivers with dams; geothermal power works best near volcanoes and hot springs, as in Iceland.
Tidal power uses the rise and fall of ocean tides to generate electricity.
# agriculture and food
Farmers use irrigation to bring water to crops in dry areas.
Farmers use fertilizers to add nutrients, pesticides to kill pests and herbicides to kill weeds.
Organic farming avoids synthetic pesticides and fertilizers.
Bees and other pollinators are needed to grow many fruit and vegetable crops.
A decline in honeybees could reduce crop production.
Crops such as corn, wheat and rice provide most of the world's food energy.
Domesticated animals such as cows, chickens, sheep and pigs were bred from wild ancestors by selective breeding.
Food comes from plants or from animals that ate plants, so all food energy can be traced back to the Sun.
Food preservation methods include refrigeration, freezing, canning, drying, salting, pickling and pasteurization.
Food labels list calories, fat, protein, carbohydrates, vitamins and minerals.
# water quality and treatment
Water treatment plants remove dirt and germs from water by filtering, settling and adding chlorine.
Boiling water kills most germs and makes it safer to drink.
Filtering water through sand and gravel removes particles but may not remove dissolved substances or all germs.
Distillation removes salt and other dissolved substances from water.
Desalination removes salt from ocean water to make fresh water.
Dissolved oxygen in water is needed by fish; warm or polluted water holds less oxygen.
Water testing measures pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen, nitrates and bacteria.
Turbidity is the cloudiness of water caused by suspended particles.
# ecology examples by habitat
In a desert food chain, plants such as cactus are eaten by insects and rodents, which are eaten by lizards, snakes and hawks.
In an ocean food chain, phytoplankton are eaten by zooplankton, which are eaten by fish, which are eaten by seals, which are eaten by sharks or killer whales.
In a forest food chain, acorns are eaten by mice and squirrels, which are eaten by owls, foxes and hawks.
In a grassland food chain, grass is eaten by grasshoppers, zebras and rabbits, which are eaten by birds, lions and snakes.
In a pond food chain, algae are eaten by tadpoles and insects, which are eaten by fish and frogs, which are eaten by herons and snakes.
In the Arctic, seals eat fish and polar bears eat seals.
A keystone species has a large effect on its ecosystem; sea otters keep sea urchins from destroying kelp forests.
Wolves returning to Yellowstone reduced elk numbers, which allowed trees and other plants to recover.
Kelp forests are underwater forests of large brown algae in cold ocean water.
Prairie dogs dig burrows that provide homes for other animals.
Woodpeckers make holes in trees that other birds later use as nests.
Bats and birds help control insect populations.
Owls and snakes help control mouse and rat populations.
Earthworms and insects aerate soil.
# biomes in detail
Tundra: very cold, dry, treeless, permafrost; animals include caribou, arctic foxes, snowy owls, lemmings and polar bears.
Taiga or boreal forest: cold, long winters, conifer trees; animals include moose, wolves, lynx, bears and owls.
Temperate deciduous forest: four distinct seasons, moderate rainfall, trees that drop leaves; animals include deer, squirrels, black bears, raccoons and foxes.
Tropical rain forest: hot and rainy all year, tall trees forming a canopy; animals include monkeys, parrots, jaguars, sloths and frogs.
Grassland or prairie: warm summers, cold winters, moderate rain, grasses; animals include bison, prairie dogs, coyotes and hawks.
Savanna: hot grassland with scattered trees and a dry season; animals include lions, zebras, giraffes, elephants and antelope.
Desert: very little rain, extreme temperatures, sandy or rocky soil; plants include cacti; animals include lizards, snakes, scorpions, camels and kangaroo rats.
Freshwater biomes include lakes, ponds, rivers, streams and wetlands with low salt content.
Marine biomes include oceans, coral reefs and estuaries with salt water.
The ocean's sunlit zone has the most life because producers need light for photosynthesis.
In the deep ocean, there is no sunlight, and some organisms get energy from chemicals at hydrothermal vents.
# human impact and solutions
Human population growth increases the demand for food, water, energy and land.
Urban sprawl replaces farmland and forests with buildings and roads.
Clearing rain forests for farming and logging destroys habitats and reduces biodiversity.
Building wildlife corridors helps animals move between habitats.
Laws such as the Clean Air Act and Clean Water Act reduce pollution.
The Endangered Species Act protects threatened plants and animals.
Zoos, aquariums and seed banks help preserve endangered species.
Reforestation is planting trees where forests were cut down.
Catalytic converters on cars reduce harmful exhaust gases.
Unleaded gasoline reduced lead pollution in the air.
Ozone depletion was reduced by banning CFCs in spray cans and refrigerators.
Light pollution from cities makes it hard to see stars and can confuse migrating birds and sea turtles.
Thermal pollution from power plants warms rivers and reduces oxygen for fish.
Noise pollution from cities and boats can disturb animals such as whales.
# sports and everyday forces
A baseball bat is a lever that transfers energy to the ball.
A curve ball curves because of air pushing on the spinning ball.
Bicycle helmets absorb the energy of an impact.
Air bags and padded dashboards increase the time of a collision, reducing the force on passengers.
Runners wear shoes with spikes or treads to increase friction.
Swimmers wear smooth suits and shave to reduce drag in the water.
Skydivers fall faster when they curl into a ball and slower when they spread out.
A ball bounces higher on a hard surface than on a soft surface such as sand or carpet.
A heavier bowling ball knocks down pins more easily because it has more momentum.
A golf ball has dimples that help it travel farther through the air.
Gravity pulls a basketball back down after it is thrown up.
When a soccer ball is kicked, the foot applies a force and the ball accelerates.
# simple technology and tools
A thermostat turns a heater or air conditioner on and off to keep a set temperature.
A refrigerator moves heat from inside to the outside air.
A pressure cooker cooks food faster because higher pressure raises the boiling point of water.
A vacuum cleaner works by lowering air pressure so outside air pushes dirt in.
A siphon uses gravity and air pressure to move liquid.
A water wheel uses flowing water to turn and do work, as in old mills.
Windmills were used to grind grain and pump water.
A bicycle pump compresses air into a tire.
Electric motors are used in fans, blenders, washing machines, electric cars and toys.
A flashlight uses batteries, a switch, wires and a bulb in a circuit.
A doorbell uses an electromagnet to ring.
A telegraph used electromagnets to send messages in code.
A telephone changes sound into electrical signals and back into sound.
A solar powered calculator uses a small solar cell.
A hand crank generator changes motion into electricity.
LED lights use less electricity and last longer than incandescent bulbs.
Compact fluorescent bulbs use less energy than incandescent bulbs.
