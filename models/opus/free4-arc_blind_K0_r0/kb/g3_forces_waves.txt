# forces and motion situations
A force is a push or a pull; forces can change an object's speed, direction or shape.
An object at rest stays at rest and a moving object keeps moving in a straight line unless an unbalanced force acts on it; this is inertia, Newton's first law.
Passengers lurch forward when a car stops suddenly because of inertia; seat belts stop them.
A heavier object has more inertia and is harder to start or stop moving.
The acceleration of an object depends on the force and its mass: more force gives more acceleration, more mass gives less acceleration; this is Newton's second law.
A greater force on the same object makes it speed up faster.
The same force gives a light object more acceleration than a heavy object.
For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force; this is Newton's third law.
A rocket moves up because hot gases are pushed down out of the engine and push the rocket up.
When a swimmer pushes the water backward, the water pushes the swimmer forward.
When a person steps off a boat onto a dock, the boat moves away from the dock.
Balanced forces are equal and opposite and do not change an object's motion.
Unbalanced forces change an object's motion.
In a tug of war, if both teams pull with equal force, the rope does not move.
Speed is distance divided by time.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is a change in speed or direction over time.
A car going around a curve at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between surfaces that touch.
Rough surfaces such as sandpaper, carpet and gravel produce more friction than smooth surfaces such as ice or polished wood.
Friction makes a rolling ball slow down and stop.
Friction produces heat, which is why rubbing hands together warms them.
Oil, grease and wax reduce friction between moving parts.
Wheels and ball bearings reduce friction.
Tires and shoes have treads to increase friction and grip the road or floor.
Sand or salt is spread on icy roads to increase friction so cars do not slide.
Air resistance is friction from air that slows falling and moving objects.
A parachute has a large surface area that increases air resistance and slows the fall.
A flat sheet of paper falls more slowly than a crumpled ball of paper because it has more air resistance.
Without air resistance, as on the Moon, a feather and a hammer fall at the same rate.
Gravity pulls all objects toward Earth's center, which is why dropped objects fall down.
All objects fall with the same acceleration if air resistance is ignored.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object's mass; it is measured with a spring scale in newtons.
An object's mass is the same on the Moon as on Earth, but its weight is less because the Moon's gravity is weaker.
The gravitational force between two objects is stronger when their masses are larger and when they are closer together.
Gravity keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth and the planets in orbit around the Sun.
A magnet can push or pull without touching; magnetism and gravity act at a distance.
Momentum is mass times velocity; a heavy truck moving fast has a lot of momentum.
Work is done when a force moves an object a distance; work equals force times distance.
No work is done when you push on a wall that does not move.
Power is how fast work is done.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force, but they do not reduce the amount of work.
A lever is a bar that turns on a fulcrum; a seesaw, crowbar, bottle opener and broom are levers.
Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes the load easier to lift with a lever.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope; a single fixed pulley changes the direction of the force, as on a flagpole.
Using more pulleys together reduces the force needed to lift a load.
An inclined plane, or ramp, lets a smaller force move a load up over a longer distance.
A longer, less steep ramp needs less force to push a load up.
A wedge, such as an axe, knife or doorstop, splits or holds things apart.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post, as in a jar lid or a bolt.
A wheel and axle, such as a doorknob, steering wheel or screwdriver handle, turns a small force into a larger turning force.
A bicycle uses wheels and axles, levers and gears.

# electricity and magnetism situations
Electric current flows only in a complete closed circuit.
An open switch breaks the circuit, so current stops and the bulb goes out.
A circuit needs an energy source such as a battery, wires and a load such as a bulb.
In a series circuit there is only one path, so if one bulb burns out all the other bulbs go out.
In a series circuit, adding more bulbs makes each bulb dimmer.
In a parallel circuit there are several paths, so if one bulb burns out the others stay lit.
Homes are wired in parallel circuits so each appliance can be turned on and off separately.
Adding more batteries in series increases the voltage and makes a bulb brighter.
A short circuit lets too much current flow and can cause wires to get hot.
Fuses and circuit breakers stop the current when too much flows, preventing fires.
Electrical conductors such as copper, aluminum, silver, gold and iron let electricity flow easily.
Electrical insulators such as rubber, plastic, glass, wood and cloth do not let electricity flow easily.
Wires are made of copper covered with plastic or rubber insulation.
Water with dissolved salts conducts electricity, so electrical devices should be kept away from water.
Static electricity is a buildup of electric charge on an object, such as when a balloon is rubbed on hair.
Rubbing a balloon on hair moves electrons to the balloon, giving it a negative charge.
Objects with opposite charges attract; objects with the same charge repel.
A charged balloon sticks to a wall because it attracts opposite charges in the wall.
A shock from a doorknob after walking on carpet is a discharge of static electricity.
Magnets attract objects made of iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.
Magnets do not attract aluminum, copper, plastic, wood, glass or paper.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole; like poles repel and opposite poles attract.
A magnet's force is strongest at its poles.
The force between magnets gets weaker as they move farther apart.
A compass needle is a magnet that points toward Earth's magnetic north pole.
Earth acts like a giant magnet because of moving molten iron in its outer core.
An electric current in a wire creates a magnetic field.
An electromagnet is a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core that becomes a magnet when current flows.
An electromagnet can be made stronger by adding more coils of wire, using more current or batteries, or using an iron core.
An electromagnet can be turned on and off, which is why it is used in junkyard cranes, doorbells and speakers.
A generator makes electricity by moving a magnet near a coil of wire.
An electric motor uses magnets and electric current to make motion.

# waves, sound and light situations
Waves carry energy from one place to another without carrying matter.
A floating object bobs up and down as a wave passes but does not move along with the wave.
In a transverse wave, such as light or a wave on a rope, the particles move perpendicular to the direction the wave travels.
In a longitudinal or compression wave, such as sound, the particles move back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels.
The amplitude of a wave is its height; larger amplitude means more energy.
The wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next.
The frequency is the number of waves that pass a point each second, measured in hertz.
Higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths.
Sound is made by vibrations.
Sound needs a medium such as air, water or a solid to travel through; sound cannot travel through empty space.
Astronauts on the Moon use radios because sound cannot travel through the vacuum of space.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids and slowest through gases.
Sound travels about 340 meters per second in air.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is; it depends on the frequency of the vibrations.
Faster vibrations make a higher pitch; slower vibrations make a lower pitch.
A shorter, thinner or tighter string makes a higher pitch.
A longer, thicker or looser string makes a lower pitch.
A short column of air, as in a bottle with more water in it, makes a higher pitch when blown across.
Loudness depends on the amplitude of the sound wave; loudness is measured in decibels.
Hitting a drum harder makes a louder sound because the vibrations have larger amplitude.
Sound gets quieter as you move farther from its source.
An echo is a sound wave that reflects off a hard surface and returns.
Soft materials such as carpet, curtains and foam absorb sound and reduce echoes.
Bats, dolphins and sonar use echoes to locate objects.
The Doppler effect makes the pitch of a siren sound higher as it approaches and lower as it moves away.
Light travels in straight lines and can travel through empty space.
Light travels much faster than sound, about 300,000 kilometers per second.
Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light.
A shadow is larger when the object is closer to the light source.
Reflection is light bouncing off a surface; mirrors reflect light to form images.
A flat mirror forms an image that is the same size as the object and reversed left to right.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one material into another, such as from air into water or glass.
A straw in a glass of water looks bent because of refraction.
A convex lens bends light inward to focus it; it is used in magnifying glasses, cameras and eyeglasses for farsighted people.
A concave lens spreads light out; it is used in eyeglasses for nearsighted people.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the rainbow because each color bends a different amount.
A rainbow forms when sunlight is refracted and reflected by raindrops.
White light is a mixture of all colors of light.
An object looks red because it reflects red light and absorbs the other colors.
A white object reflects all colors of light; a black object absorbs all colors.
Plants look green because chlorophyll reflects green light.
We see objects when light reflects off them and enters our eyes.
The pupil lets light into the eye, the lens focuses it, and the retina detects it and sends signals to the brain through the optic nerve.
The electromagnetic spectrum, from longest to shortest wavelength, is radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat.
Ultraviolet light from the Sun causes sunburn.
X-rays pass through soft tissue but not bone, so they are used to see bones.
Microwaves heat food and are used for cell phone communication.
Radio waves carry radio and television signals.
