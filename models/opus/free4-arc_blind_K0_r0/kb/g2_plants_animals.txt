# plants (second set)
Plants are producers: they make their own food, the sugar glucose, by photosynthesis.
Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the leaves, in chloroplasts that contain the green pigment chlorophyll.
In photosynthesis, plants use light energy from the Sun, carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil to make glucose and release oxygen.
The word equation for photosynthesis is carbon dioxide plus water, with light energy, makes sugar plus oxygen.
Photosynthesis changes light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar.
Plants take in carbon dioxide through tiny openings in the leaves called stomata and release oxygen and water vapor through them.
Guard cells open and close the stomata.
Most of the oxygen in the atmosphere comes from photosynthesis by plants and algae, including ocean phytoplankton.
Plants also carry out cellular respiration all the time, using oxygen to break down sugar for energy.
A plant kept in the dark cannot do photosynthesis and uses up its stored sugar.
Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.
In autumn, leaves of deciduous trees stop making chlorophyll, so the green fades and yellow, orange and red colors show.
Leaves are usually broad and flat to capture as much sunlight as possible.
Roots anchor the plant in the soil and absorb water and minerals; some roots, such as carrots and beets, store food.
Root hairs increase the surface area of roots to absorb more water.
The stem supports the plant and holds the leaves up toward the light; it carries water, minerals and food between the roots and leaves.
Xylem tubes carry water and minerals up from the roots; phloem tubes carry sugar made in the leaves to other parts of the plant.
Vascular plants have xylem and phloem; nonvascular plants such as mosses do not and must live in moist places and stay small.
Plants need sunlight, water, air with carbon dioxide, nutrients and the right temperature to grow; most also need soil.
Plants do not get their food from the soil; they make it, though they take minerals from the soil.
Most of a tree's mass comes from carbon dioxide taken from the air during photosynthesis.
Plants respond to their environment: phototropism is growth toward light, gravitropism or geotropism is growth in response to gravity, and thigmotropism is response to touch.
Roots grow downward with gravity and stems grow upward against gravity.
A plant on a windowsill bends toward the window because the stem grows toward the light.
Vines such as ivy and morning glory climb by wrapping tendrils around objects, a response to touch.
The Venus flytrap is a plant that closes its leaves to trap insects to get nitrogen from them.
Seeds contain a tiny undeveloped plant called an embryo, stored food, and a protective seed coat.
The cotyledon or seed leaf stores food that the young plant uses until it can make its own food.
Germination is when a seed begins to sprout and grow; most seeds need water, oxygen and the right temperature to germinate, but not light.
The life cycle of a flowering plant is seed, germination, seedling, adult plant, flower, pollination, fertilization, fruit with seeds.
Flowers are the reproductive parts of flowering plants.
The stamen is the male part of a flower; it includes the anther, which makes pollen, and the filament.
The pistil is the female part of a flower; it includes the stigma, style and ovary.
The ovary of a flower contains ovules that become seeds after fertilization; the ovary becomes the fruit.
Sepals are small leaf like parts that protect the flower bud.
Petals are often brightly colored and scented to attract pollinators such as bees, butterflies, moths, birds and bats.
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma.
Pollinators such as bees carry pollen from flower to flower while they collect nectar.
Some plants, such as grasses, corn and many trees, are pollinated by wind and have small, plain flowers that make lots of pollen.
Fruits protect seeds and help spread them.
Seeds are dispersed by wind, as with dandelions and maple seeds; by water, as with coconuts; by animals eating fruit or carrying burrs on their fur; and by pods that burst open.
Seed dispersal helps young plants grow away from the parent plant so they do not compete for light, water and nutrients.
Nonflowering plants include mosses and ferns, which reproduce with spores, and conifers, which make seeds in cones.
Conifers such as pine, spruce and fir trees have needle like leaves and make seeds in cones; most are evergreen.
Deciduous trees such as oak and maple lose their leaves in fall to save water and energy during winter.
Evergreen trees keep their leaves all year.
Some plants grow new plants without seeds from runners, as strawberries do, from bulbs such as onions and tulips, from tubers such as potatoes, or from cuttings.
Desert plants such as cacti have thick waxy stems to store water, spines instead of leaves to reduce water loss and protect from animals, and wide shallow or very deep roots.
The waxy coating on leaves, called the cuticle, reduces water loss.
Plants in rain forests may have large leaves to catch the little sunlight that reaches the forest floor and drip tips to shed water.
Water plants such as water lilies have air spaces to help them float and wide flat leaves that float on the water.
Thorns on roses and spines on cacti protect plants from being eaten.
Some plants make poisons or bad tasting chemicals to keep animals from eating them.
Plants give off water vapor through their leaves by transpiration, which helps pull water up from the roots.
A plant wilts when it loses more water than it takes in.
Annual plants complete their life cycle in one growing season; perennials live for many years.
Algae are simple plant like organisms that make food by photosynthesis; many live in water.
# animals (second set)
Animals are consumers; they cannot make their own food and must eat plants or other animals.
Vertebrates are animals with a backbone: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.
Invertebrates are animals without a backbone, such as insects, spiders, worms, jellyfish, snails, sea stars and crabs; most animals are invertebrates.
Mammals have hair or fur, are warm blooded, breathe with lungs, usually give birth to live young, and feed their young milk.
Whales, dolphins, bats, humans, mice, dogs and elephants are mammals.
Whales and dolphins are mammals, not fish: they breathe air with lungs, are warm blooded, give birth to live young and nurse them with milk.
Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly.
The platypus and echidna are unusual mammals that lay eggs.
Marsupials such as kangaroos, koalas and opossums carry their young in a pouch.
Birds have feathers, wings, beaks and hollow bones, are warm blooded and lay eggs with hard shells.
All birds have feathers, but not all birds can fly; penguins, ostriches and emus cannot fly.
Penguins are birds that use their wings as flippers to swim; they live mostly in the cold Southern Hemisphere.
Hollow bones make birds light for flying.
Reptiles have dry scaly skin, are cold blooded, breathe with lungs and most lay leathery eggs on land; examples are snakes, lizards, turtles and crocodiles.
Amphibians such as frogs, toads and salamanders live part of their lives in water and part on land, have moist skin and lay eggs in water.
Young amphibians such as tadpoles live in water and breathe with gills; adults usually breathe with lungs and through their skin.
Fish live in water, breathe with gills, have fins and are cold blooded; most have scales.
Gills take dissolved oxygen out of water.
Sharks have skeletons made of cartilage instead of bone.
Cold blooded animals, or ectotherms, have body temperatures that change with their surroundings; they bask in the sun to warm up.
Warm blooded animals, or endotherms, keep a constant body temperature using energy from food.
Insects have three body parts, the head, thorax and abdomen, six legs, two antennae and usually wings.
Spiders are arachnids, not insects; they have eight legs and two body parts.
Arthropods have jointed legs, segmented bodies and an exoskeleton; they include insects, spiders, crustaceans, centipedes and millipedes.
An exoskeleton is a hard outer covering that protects and supports an animal; arthropods molt to grow.
Crustaceans such as crabs, lobsters and shrimp have hard shells and most live in water.
Mollusks such as snails, clams, oysters, squid and octopuses have soft bodies, and many have shells.
Worms include earthworms, flatworms and roundworms; earthworms improve soil.
Cnidarians such as jellyfish, corals and sea anemones have stinging tentacles.
Echinoderms such as sea stars and sea urchins have spiny skin and live in the ocean.
Sponges are simple animals that filter food from water.
Complete metamorphosis has four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult; butterflies, moths, beetles, flies, ants and bees go through it.
A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly or moth; a chrysalis or cocoon holds the pupa.
Incomplete metamorphosis has three stages: egg, nymph and adult; grasshoppers, crickets, dragonflies and cockroaches go through it.
A nymph looks like a small adult without wings.
The life cycle of a frog is egg, tadpole, tadpole with legs, froglet, adult frog.
The life cycle of a bird is egg, hatchling, young bird, adult; the life cycle of a mammal is birth, young, adult.
Some animals, such as fish and frogs, lay many eggs because few of the young survive; mammals have few young and care for them.
Animals that care for their young after birth, such as mammals and birds, usually have fewer offspring.
Instincts are behaviors an animal is born knowing how to do, such as a spider spinning a web, a bird building a nest, a baby sucking, sea turtles crawling to the ocean, and salmon swimming upstream to spawn.
Learned behaviors are gained through experience or by watching others, such as a dog learning tricks or a chimpanzee using a stick to get termites.
Migration is the seasonal movement of animals from one place to another to find food, warmer weather or a place to breed, as birds, monarch butterflies, whales and caribou do.
Hibernation is a deep sleep like state in winter in which body temperature, heart rate and breathing drop to save energy when food is scarce.
Animals that hibernate include bears, ground squirrels, bats, woodchucks, chipmunks, frogs and some snakes.
Estivation is a period of inactivity during hot, dry weather.
Animals prepare for winter by storing food, growing thicker fur, storing fat, migrating or hibernating.
Squirrels bury nuts in fall to eat during winter.
Some animals live in groups such as herds, packs, flocks, schools and colonies for protection, hunting or raising young.
Bees and ants live in colonies with a queen and workers that have different jobs.
Honeybees perform a waggle dance to tell other bees where to find flowers.
Animals communicate with sounds, smells, movements and colors.
Animals that are active at night are nocturnal; animals active during the day are diurnal.
Nocturnal animals often have large eyes and a good sense of hearing or smell.
Predators often have sharp teeth, claws, keen eyesight and speed; prey animals often have eyes on the sides of their heads to watch for danger.
Animals with eyes facing forward, like owls and cats, judge distance well for hunting.
Carnivores have sharp pointed teeth for tearing meat; herbivores have flat teeth for grinding plants; omnivores have both.
A bird's beak shape is adapted to its food: hooked beaks tear meat, long thin beaks sip nectar, short thick beaks crack seeds, and flat broad bills strain food from water.
Birds' feet are adapted too: ducks have webbed feet for swimming, hawks and eagles have sharp talons for grabbing prey, and woodpeckers have feet for climbing trees.
Animals in cold places, such as polar bears, seals, penguins and arctic foxes, have thick fur, feathers or blubber to keep warm.
Blubber is a thick layer of fat that insulates whales, seals and walruses.
Arctic animals often have white fur or feathers for camouflage in the snow; some, like the arctic fox and snowshoe hare, turn brown in summer.
Desert animals such as camels, lizards, kangaroo rats and snakes are adapted to save water and avoid heat, often being active at night and living in burrows.
Camels store fat in their humps and can go a long time without drinking water.
Large ears help animals such as the fennec fox and jackrabbit give off heat in the desert.
Fish have streamlined bodies and fins for swimming; ducks have waterproof feathers.
A giraffe's long neck helps it reach leaves high in trees.
A porcupine's quills, a turtle's shell, a skunk's spray and an armadillo's armor protect them from predators.
Chameleons and octopuses can change their color to blend in with their surroundings.
A walking stick insect looks like a twig to hide from predators.
Animals get oxygen through lungs, gills, or their skin, depending on the animal.
Earthworms breathe through their moist skin.
Woodpeckers have strong beaks for drilling into trees to find insects.
Hummingbirds have long thin beaks to reach nectar inside flowers.
Owls hunt at night and have excellent hearing and eyesight and silent flight.
Beavers build dams with their strong teeth and change the environment by creating ponds.
Elephants use their trunks to drink, smell, grab food and communicate.
Snakes use their tongues to smell, and some can sense the heat of prey.
Sharks can sense the electricity given off by other animals.
