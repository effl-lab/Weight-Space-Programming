# measurement quality
Measurements that are very close to each other are precise.
Precise measurements give nearly the same value each time they are repeated.
Accurate measurements are close to the true or accepted value.
Repeated measurements that agree closely show precision; measurements close to the actual value show accuracy.
Measuring several times and averaging the results makes a measurement more reliable and accurate.
Repeating trials and averaging the data reduces the effect of errors.
Using more test subjects or a larger sample size makes results more reliable.
Testing only one plant or one object in each group is too small a sample to draw a conclusion.
An experiment with only one trial is not reliable; scientists repeat experiments many times.
A measuring tool with smaller units, such as millimeters, gives more precise measurements than one with larger units.
A metric ruler measures length in centimeters and millimeters.
A meter stick measures longer lengths such as the length of a room or the distance a ball rolls.
A tape measure is used to measure the distance around an object, such as the circumference of a tree.
A spring scale measures force or weight in newtons.
A triple beam balance or electronic balance measures mass in grams.
A graduated cylinder measures the volume of a liquid in milliliters.
A beaker holds and mixes liquids but gives only rough volume measurements.
A stopwatch measures time in seconds.
A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.
A hand lens or magnifying glass makes small objects such as insects, seeds and rocks look larger.
A microscope is used to see cells, bacteria and other things too small to see with the eyes.
A telescope is used to observe distant objects such as planets, stars and the Moon.
Binoculars help observe birds and animals that are far away.
A compass shows direction using Earth's magnetic field.
A rain gauge measures the amount of rainfall.
A wind vane shows wind direction; an anemometer measures wind speed.
A barometer measures air pressure; falling air pressure often means stormy weather is coming.
A hygrometer or psychrometer measures humidity.
A seismograph records the waves of earthquakes.
A pH meter or litmus paper measures whether a substance is an acid or a base.
A Celsius thermometer shows water freezing at 0 degrees and boiling at 100 degrees.
Water freezes at 32 degrees Fahrenheit and boils at 212 degrees Fahrenheit.
Volume of a regular solid such as a box is found by multiplying length times width times height.
The volume of an irregular object such as a rock is found by water displacement in a graduated cylinder.
Density is found by dividing mass by volume.
Speed is found by dividing distance by time.
The mass of a liquid is found by weighing the container with the liquid and subtracting the mass of the empty container.
# units
The meter is the basic SI unit of length; the gram or kilogram is the unit of mass; the liter is the unit of volume.
The second is the unit of time; the newton is the unit of force; the joule is the unit of energy and work.
The watt is the unit of power; the volt is the unit of voltage; the ampere is the unit of electric current.
A kilometer is 1,000 meters; a centimeter is one hundredth of a meter; a millimeter is one thousandth of a meter.
A kilogram is 1,000 grams; a milligram is one thousandth of a gram.
A liter is 1,000 milliliters; one milliliter equals one cubic centimeter.
Scientists use the metric system so that they can share and compare measurements around the world.
Distances in space are measured in light-years or astronomical units.
An astronomical unit is the average distance between Earth and the Sun.
A light-year is the distance light travels in one year.
Speed is measured in meters per second or kilometers per hour.
Density is measured in grams per cubic centimeter or grams per milliliter.
# investigations
A hypothesis is a possible explanation or prediction that can be tested by an experiment.
A good hypothesis is testable and often written as an if-then statement.
An observation is information gathered with the senses or with instruments.
An inference is a conclusion or explanation based on observations.
A prediction is a statement about what will happen in the future based on evidence or a pattern.
A conclusion tells whether the data support the hypothesis.
If the results do not support the hypothesis, the scientist should revise the hypothesis and test again.
Scientists should report results honestly even when the results do not support the hypothesis.
Data that does not fit should be recorded and checked, not thrown out or changed.
A fair test changes only one variable at a time and keeps all other conditions the same.
The variable that the scientist changes on purpose is the independent or manipulated variable.
The variable that is measured as the result is the dependent or responding variable.
Controlled variables are the conditions kept the same in every trial of an experiment.
The control group does not receive the treatment being tested and is used for comparison.
The experimental group receives the treatment or change being tested.
Changing more than one variable at a time makes it impossible to know which variable caused the result.
In an experiment testing fertilizer, the plants should get the same amount of water, light and soil.
In an experiment testing light, the plants should get the same amount of water and the same type of soil.
In an experiment testing how far a toy car rolls, the ramp height or the surface may be the variable tested.
A data table organizes the measurements recorded during an experiment.
A bar graph compares amounts in different categories or groups.
A line graph shows how something changes over time or how one variable changes as another changes.
A circle graph or pie chart shows parts of a whole as percentages.
The independent variable is placed on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis of a graph.
Scientists share results by publishing reports so that other scientists can repeat the experiment.
Other scientists repeat an experiment to check whether the results can be reproduced.
Peer review means other scientists check the methods and results of an investigation.
A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation supported by much evidence.
A scientific law describes what always happens under certain conditions, such as the law of gravity.
Scientific knowledge can change when new evidence is discovered.
Scientists use models to study objects that are too large, too small, too far away or too dangerous to study directly.
A model of the solar system or a globe helps explain things that are too big to observe all at once.
A computer model can predict weather, climate change or the path of a hurricane.
A limitation of a model is that it is not exactly like the real object.
Scientists keep a record of procedures and data in a notebook or journal.
Writing clear step-by-step procedures lets other people repeat an experiment exactly.
Students should make observations and collect data before forming a conclusion.
A survey or count of organisms in a small sample area can estimate the population of a larger area.
Scientists classify objects by observing their properties and grouping similar objects together.
A dichotomous key identifies organisms by a series of choices between two characteristics.
# safety
Safety goggles protect the eyes from chemicals, glass and flying objects during experiments.
Students should wear goggles when heating substances, mixing chemicals or using glass.
Gloves protect the hands when handling chemicals, preserved specimens, bacteria or hot objects.
Students should never eat, drink or taste anything in a science laboratory.
Students should wash their hands after handling chemicals, soil, plants or animals.
Spills and broken glass should be reported to the teacher right away.
Long hair should be tied back when working near a flame.
When heating a test tube, point the open end away from yourself and others.
Students should follow the teacher's directions and read the safety instructions before beginning an experiment.
To smell a chemical, wave the fumes toward your nose with your hand instead of sniffing directly.
Chemicals should be disposed of as the teacher directs, not poured down the drain unless allowed.
An apron protects clothing from spills.
A fire extinguisher and eyewash station should be located before starting a lab.
Students should never look directly at the Sun, even through a telescope or binoculars.
Students should not touch wild animals or unknown plants on a field trip.
Electrical cords should be kept away from water.
# technology and design
The engineering design process includes identifying a problem, brainstorming, designing, building a prototype, testing and improving.
A prototype is a first model of a design that is built and tested.
Engineers test a design, find problems and then improve it.
A constraint is a limit on a design, such as cost, size, materials or time.
Criteria are the requirements a design must meet to be successful.
Engineers choose materials based on their properties, such as strength, weight, cost and resistance to heat or water.
A trade-off is giving up one quality to get another, such as a lighter car that is less strong.
Technology is the use of scientific knowledge to solve problems and meet human needs.
New technology such as telescopes and microscopes has helped scientists make new discoveries.
The invention of the microscope led to the discovery of cells.
The invention of the telescope allowed Galileo to see the moons of Jupiter.
Satellites are used for communication, weather forecasting, GPS navigation and studying Earth.
Computers help scientists collect, store and analyze large amounts of data.
