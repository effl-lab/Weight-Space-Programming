# ecology (second set)
An ecosystem is all the living and nonliving things that interact in an area.
Biotic factors are the living parts of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are the nonliving parts of an ecosystem, such as sunlight, water, air, temperature, soil and rocks.
A population is all the members of one species living in an area; a community is all the populations of different species living in an area.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives and finds food, water, shelter and space.
A niche is the role an organism plays in its ecosystem, including what it eats and how it interacts with others.
Two species cannot share exactly the same niche for long because they would compete.
A food chain shows how energy passes from one organism to the next as one eats another.
The arrows in a food chain or food web point from the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it, showing the direction energy flows.
A food web is many connected food chains in an ecosystem.
Producers, such as plants, algae and phytoplankton, make their own food using sunlight and are the first link in food chains.
Consumers get energy by eating other organisms.
Primary consumers are herbivores that eat producers; secondary consumers eat primary consumers; tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers.
Herbivores eat only plants; carnivores eat only animals; omnivores eat both plants and animals.
Scavengers such as vultures, hyenas and crows eat dead animals.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and mushrooms break down dead organisms and wastes and return nutrients to the soil, where plants use them again.
Without decomposers, dead material would pile up and nutrients would not be recycled.
The Sun is the original source of energy for almost all food chains.
Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction and is not recycled, but matter such as carbon, nitrogen and water is recycled.
An energy pyramid shows that energy decreases at each level of a food chain; producers at the bottom have the most energy.
Only about 10 percent of the energy at one level of a food chain is passed to the next level; most is used for life processes or lost as heat.
Because energy is lost at each level, there are fewer top predators than herbivores, and food chains rarely have more than four or five links.
Biomagnification is when poisons such as DDT and mercury become more concentrated in organisms higher in the food chain, so top predators have the highest levels.
The pesticide DDT caused eagles, ospreys and pelicans to lay eggs with thin shells that broke.
If one population in a food web changes, the populations that eat it and that it eats also change.
If a predator is removed, its prey population usually increases at first, and then may crash after eating too much of its food supply.
If the number of predators increases, the number of prey usually decreases.
Predator and prey populations rise and fall in cycles, with the predator population following the prey population.
Wolves returned to Yellowstone reduced the number of elk, which allowed willows and aspens along rivers to grow back.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space, shelter, disease and predators limit the size of a population.
Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support with its resources.
Competition happens when organisms need the same limited resources, such as food, water, light, space or mates.
Plants compete for sunlight, water, space and nutrients.
Symbiosis is a close relationship between two species.
Mutualism is a relationship in which both species benefit, such as bees and flowers, clownfish and sea anemones, and lichens made of fungi and algae.
Commensalism is a relationship in which one species benefits and the other is not helped or harmed, such as barnacles on a whale or birds nesting in a tree.
Parasitism is a relationship in which one organism, the parasite, benefits by living on or in a host and harms it, such as ticks, fleas, lice, tapeworms and mosquitoes.
Predation is when a predator hunts, kills and eats its prey.
Ecological succession is the gradual change of the community in an area over time.
Primary succession starts on bare rock with no soil, such as after a volcanic eruption or a glacier retreats; lichens and mosses are the first pioneer species.
Pioneer species such as lichens break down rock and help form soil.
Secondary succession happens where soil remains after a disturbance such as a fire, flood or abandoned farm; grasses and weeds grow first, then shrubs, then trees.
A climax community is the stable community that develops at the end of succession.
A pond slowly fills with sediment and plants over time and can become a marsh, then a meadow, then a forest.
Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area; ecosystems with high biodiversity are more stable.
Tropical rain forests and coral reefs have the greatest biodiversity.
A keystone species has a large effect on its ecosystem, such as sea otters that eat sea urchins and protect kelp forests.
An invasive species is a nonnative species that spreads and harms an ecosystem by outcompeting native species, such as zebra mussels, kudzu and cane toads.
Invasive species often have no natural predators in their new environment.
The carbon cycle moves carbon between the air, living things, the ocean and rocks through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and burning.
Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the air; respiration, decomposition and burning fuels add carbon dioxide to the air.
The nitrogen cycle moves nitrogen between the air, soil and living things; bacteria in soil and in the roots of legumes change nitrogen gas into forms plants can use.
Living things need nitrogen to make proteins.
The oxygen cycle: plants release oxygen in photosynthesis and animals and plants use it in respiration.
# biomes (second set)
A biome is a large region with a similar climate, plants and animals.
The tundra is a cold, dry, treeless biome with permafrost, frozen soil, under the surface; plants are mosses, lichens and low shrubs.
Animals of the tundra include caribou, arctic foxes, snowy owls, musk oxen and lemmings.
The taiga, or boreal forest, is a cold biome with long winters and forests of conifers such as spruce, pine and fir; moose, wolves and lynx live there.
Temperate deciduous forests have four seasons and trees that lose their leaves in fall, such as oak, maple and beech; deer, squirrels, black bears and raccoons live there.
Tropical rain forests are hot and wet all year, have the most plant and animal species of any biome, and have layers: emergent, canopy, understory and forest floor.
Little sunlight reaches the floor of a rain forest because the tall trees of the canopy block it.
Rain forest soil is thin and poor in nutrients because heavy rain washes nutrients away and decomposers recycle them quickly.
Grasslands such as prairies, steppes and savannas have grasses and few trees; they get moderate rain and often have fires.
Savannas are tropical grasslands with scattered trees, home to zebras, lions, giraffes and elephants.
Prairie soil is very rich, so many grasslands have been turned into farms.
Deserts get very little rain; plants and animals there are adapted to save water.
Freshwater ecosystems include ponds, lakes, rivers, streams and wetlands; marine ecosystems are salt water oceans, coral reefs and estuaries.
The intertidal zone is the area of shore between high tide and low tide; organisms there must survive being covered by water and exposed to air.
# human body (second set)
The human body is organized into cells, tissues, organs and organ systems that work together to keep the body alive.
Homeostasis is the body's ability to keep stable internal conditions, such as body temperature, water balance and blood sugar.
Normal human body temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius, or 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit.
Sweating cools the body as sweat evaporates from the skin; shivering warms the body by making muscles move.
The skeletal system supports the body, protects organs, allows movement with muscles, stores minerals such as calcium and makes blood cells in the bone marrow.
The skull protects the brain; the ribs protect the heart and lungs; the backbone, or spine, protects the spinal cord.
The adult human skeleton has 206 bones.
Calcium and vitamin D are needed for strong bones and teeth.
Joints are places where bones meet; hinge joints in knees and elbows move like a door, ball and socket joints in shoulders and hips move in many directions, and pivot joints in the neck allow turning.
Ligaments connect bone to bone; tendons connect muscle to bone; cartilage cushions the ends of bones.
The muscular system moves the body; muscles work in pairs by contracting and relaxing, such as the biceps and triceps.
Muscles can only pull, not push.
Skeletal muscles are voluntary; smooth muscles in the stomach and blood vessels and cardiac muscle in the heart are involuntary.
The circulatory system, made of the heart, blood and blood vessels, carries oxygen, nutrients and hormones to cells and carries away carbon dioxide and wastes.
The heart is a muscle that pumps blood through the body; it has four chambers, two atria and two ventricles.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart; capillaries are tiny vessels where materials are exchanged with cells.
Blood from the body returns to the right side of the heart, is pumped to the lungs to pick up oxygen, returns to the left side of the heart and is pumped to the body.
Blood is made of red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets and liquid plasma.
Red blood cells contain hemoglobin, which carries oxygen; white blood cells fight infection; platelets help blood clot to stop bleeding.
The heart beats faster during exercise to deliver more oxygen and nutrients to the muscles and remove carbon dioxide.
Pulse is the beating of the heart felt in arteries at the wrist or neck.
The respiratory system takes in oxygen and gets rid of carbon dioxide; it includes the nose, trachea, bronchi and lungs.
Air enters through the nose or mouth, travels down the trachea, or windpipe, into the bronchi and then into the lungs.
In the alveoli, tiny air sacs in the lungs, oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out of the blood.
The diaphragm is a muscle below the lungs that contracts to pull air into the lungs.
Breathing rate increases during exercise because the muscles need more oxygen.
Smoking damages the lungs and can cause lung cancer, emphysema and heart disease.
The respiratory and circulatory systems work together to deliver oxygen to cells and remove carbon dioxide.
The digestive system breaks food down into nutrients the body can absorb; it includes the mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver and pancreas.
Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth chew food and saliva starts to break down starch.
The esophagus is the tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach by muscle squeezes.
The stomach mixes food with acid and enzymes to break it down.
Most digestion and absorption of nutrients into the blood happens in the small intestine, which has villi to increase surface area.
The large intestine absorbs water from undigested food and forms solid waste.
The liver makes bile, which helps break down fats; the pancreas makes digestive enzymes and the hormone insulin.
The digestive and circulatory systems work together to deliver nutrients to cells.
The excretory system removes wastes; the kidneys filter wastes from blood and make urine, which is stored in the bladder.
The lungs excrete carbon dioxide and the skin excretes sweat.
The nervous system controls the body and responds to stimuli; it includes the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
Neurons, or nerve cells, carry electrical messages called impulses.
Sensory nerves carry messages from the senses to the brain; motor nerves carry messages from the brain to muscles.
The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and controls thinking, memory, learning, speech and the senses.
The cerebellum controls balance, posture and coordination of muscles.
The brain stem controls involuntary actions such as breathing, heart rate and digestion.
A reflex is a quick automatic response that does not involve thinking, such as pulling your hand away from something hot or the knee jerk.
Reflexes are controlled by the spinal cord so they happen fast.
The five senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch; the eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin are sense organs.
Light enters the eye through the cornea and pupil; the lens focuses it on the retina, which sends signals through the optic nerve to the brain.
The iris is the colored part of the eye that controls the size of the pupil; the pupil gets smaller in bright light and larger in dim light.
Sound waves make the eardrum vibrate; tiny bones in the middle ear pass the vibrations to the cochlea, which sends signals to the brain.
The endocrine system uses glands to release hormones into the blood that control growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
Endocrine glands include the pituitary, thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
Adrenaline is a hormone that prepares the body for fight or flight in an emergency, making the heart beat faster.
Insulin from the pancreas lowers blood sugar; people with diabetes cannot make or use insulin properly.
Growth hormone from the pituitary gland controls growth.
The reproductive system produces sex cells and offspring; the female ovaries produce eggs and the male testes produce sperm.
Fertilization is when a sperm cell joins with an egg cell to form a zygote.
Puberty is the stage when the body matures and can reproduce, controlled by hormones.
The integumentary system is the skin, hair and nails; skin protects the body from germs, helps control temperature and senses touch.
The skin is the largest organ of the body.
The immune system defends the body against pathogens; it includes white blood cells, antibodies, the lymph nodes, the spleen and skin.
Antibodies are proteins made by white blood cells that attack specific germs.
A vaccine contains weakened or dead germs or parts of germs that cause the body to make antibodies, so the person becomes immune to the disease.
Edward Jenner made the first vaccine, for smallpox; Louis Pasteur made vaccines for rabies and developed pasteurization.
Allergies happen when the immune system reacts to harmless substances such as pollen, dust or certain foods.
# health and disease (second set)
Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites and can spread from person to person.
Noninfectious diseases such as diabetes, heart disease, cancer and asthma do not spread from person to person.
Diseases caused by viruses include the common cold, influenza, chicken pox, measles, mumps, polio, COVID 19 and HIV.
Diseases caused by bacteria include strep throat, tuberculosis, cholera, tetanus, Lyme disease and many food poisonings.
Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not work against viruses.
Viruses are not made of cells and can only reproduce inside the living cells of a host.
Germs spread through the air by coughs and sneezes, by touching surfaces, through food and water, and through insect bites.
Washing hands with soap and water is one of the best ways to prevent the spread of germs.
Covering coughs and sneezes, getting vaccinated, cooking food fully and keeping food cold help prevent disease.
Malaria is caused by a parasite spread by mosquitoes; Lyme disease is spread by ticks.
Pasteurization heats milk and juice to kill harmful bacteria.
Refrigeration slows the growth of bacteria that spoil food.
Food spoils because bacteria and molds grow on it; drying, salting, canning, freezing and refrigerating help preserve food.
A balanced diet includes fruits, vegetables, grains, protein foods and dairy.
Carbohydrates such as bread, rice, pasta and sugar give the body quick energy.
Fats store energy; proteins from meat, fish, eggs, beans and nuts build and repair muscles and tissues.
Vitamins and minerals keep the body healthy; calcium builds bones, iron helps blood carry oxygen, vitamin C prevents scurvy, vitamin D prevents rickets.
Fiber from fruits, vegetables and whole grains helps digestion.
Water is needed for nearly all body processes.
Regular exercise strengthens the heart, lungs, muscles and bones.
Getting enough sleep helps the body and brain stay healthy.
Alcohol, tobacco and drugs harm the body's organs.
