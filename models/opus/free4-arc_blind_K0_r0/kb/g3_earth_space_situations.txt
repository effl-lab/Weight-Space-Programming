# weather and climate situations
Water droplets form on the outside of a cold glass because water vapor in the air condenses when it touches the cold surface.
Dew forms on grass on cool mornings when water vapor in the air condenses on the cold grass.
Frost forms when water vapor turns directly into ice on very cold surfaces.
Fog is a cloud that forms near the ground when moist air cools.
A bathroom mirror fogs up because water vapor from the hot shower condenses on the cooler mirror.
Clouds form when warm moist air rises, expands and cools, and the water vapor condenses into tiny droplets.
Clothes on a line dry fastest on a warm, sunny, windy day with low humidity.
Wet clothes dry because the water evaporates into the air.
A puddle dries up faster on a hot sunny day than on a cool cloudy day.
Water evaporates faster when it is warmer, when there is wind, and when it has a larger surface area.
A wide shallow pan of water evaporates faster than a tall narrow glass with the same amount of water.
Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air; on humid days sweat evaporates slowly.
Relative humidity of 100 percent means the air is holding all the water vapor it can, so rain or fog is likely.
Falling air pressure usually means stormy, cloudy or rainy weather is coming.
Rising air pressure usually means clear, fair weather is coming.
A barometer measures air pressure and helps predict changes in weather.
An anemometer measures wind speed; a wind vane shows wind direction.
A rain gauge measures how much rain has fallen.
A thermometer measures temperature; a hygrometer or psychrometer measures humidity.
Weather satellites take pictures of clouds and storms from space.
Doppler radar shows where precipitation is falling and how storms are moving.
Meteorologists use data from weather stations, balloons, satellites and radar to forecast the weather.
Weather maps use symbols for fronts, high pressure H and low pressure L areas.
A cold front forms when a cold air mass pushes under a warm air mass; it brings thunderstorms and then cooler, clearer weather.
A warm front forms when a warm air mass moves over a cold air mass; it brings steady rain or drizzle and then warmer weather.
Thunderstorms form when warm moist air rises quickly; they bring lightning, thunder, heavy rain and sometimes hail.
Hail forms when raindrops are carried up into freezing parts of a thunderstorm cloud again and again, adding layers of ice.
Sleet is rain that freezes into ice pellets as it falls through cold air near the ground.
Snow forms when water vapor turns into ice crystals in cold clouds.
Tornadoes are spinning columns of air that form from strong thunderstorms; they are most common in the central United States in spring.
Hurricanes form over warm ocean water near the equator and weaken over land or cold water because they lose their source of warm moist air.
Hurricanes bring strong winds, heavy rain, flooding and storm surges along coasts.
During a tornado warning, people should go to a basement or an inside room without windows on the lowest floor.
During a thunderstorm, people should go indoors and stay away from water, tall trees and metal objects.
Lightning is a giant spark of static electricity between clouds or between a cloud and the ground.
Thunder is the sound made when lightning heats the air so quickly that it expands.
We see lightning before we hear thunder because light travels much faster than sound.
Counting the seconds between lightning and thunder tells how far away the storm is.
Wind is caused by differences in air pressure, which come from the uneven heating of Earth's surface by the Sun.
Air moves from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure, making wind.
A sea breeze blows from the ocean to the land during the day because the land heats faster than the water.
A land breeze blows from the land to the ocean at night because the land cools faster than the water.
Air temperature is usually lowest just before sunrise and highest in the afternoon.
On a cloudy night the temperature stays warmer because clouds trap heat near the ground.
On a clear night more heat escapes into space, so it gets colder.
Places at higher elevation, such as mountains, are colder than places at sea level.
Mountain tops can have snow in summer because the air is colder at high elevation.
The windward side of a mountain gets more rain; the leeward side is drier and may be a desert, called a rain shadow.
Places near the equator are warm all year because they receive direct sunlight.
Places near the poles are cold because sunlight strikes them at a low angle and spreads out.
Cities near the ocean have milder winters and cooler summers than inland cities at the same latitude.
Ocean currents carry warm water from the equator toward the poles and cold water from the poles toward the equator.
The Gulf Stream is a warm ocean current that makes the climate of western Europe milder.
El Nino is a warming of the Pacific Ocean water that changes weather around the world.
Climate is the average weather of a place over many years; weather is the condition of the atmosphere on one day.
Deserts get very little precipitation, less than 25 centimeters per year.
Tropical rainforests are hot and wet all year.
The tundra is very cold, has permafrost and has few trees.
Air pollution from cars and factories can cause smog and acid rain.
Acid rain forms when sulfur and nitrogen oxides from burning fossil fuels mix with water in the air.
Acid rain damages forests, kills fish in lakes, and wears away statues and buildings made of limestone and marble.
Volcanic ash and gases in the atmosphere can block sunlight and cool Earth's climate for a few years.
Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat in the atmosphere.
Burning fossil fuels and cutting down forests increase the amount of carbon dioxide in the air.
Global warming is causing glaciers and polar ice to melt and sea level to rise.
The ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun.
Chemicals called CFCs, once used in spray cans and refrigerators, damaged the ozone layer.

# water on Earth
About 97 percent of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans.
Most of Earth's fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.
Only a small amount of Earth's water is fresh liquid water in lakes, rivers and groundwater.
Groundwater is water that soaks into the soil and fills spaces in rocks underground.
An aquifer is a layer of rock or sediment that holds groundwater that people can pump from wells.
Water soaks into sand and gravel quickly because they have large pore spaces; water soaks into clay slowly.
Clay soil holds water and drains poorly; sandy soil drains quickly and holds little water.
Loam is a mix of sand, silt, clay and humus that is good for growing plants.
Runoff is water that flows over the ground into streams instead of soaking in.
Paved surfaces such as parking lots and roads increase runoff because water cannot soak into the ground.
A watershed is all the land that drains into a river or lake.
Pollution on land can be carried by runoff into rivers, lakes and the ocean.
Ocean water is salty because rivers carry dissolved minerals from rocks into the ocean, and evaporation leaves the salt behind.
Water treatment plants filter water and add chlorine to kill germs so the water is safe to drink.
Desalination removes salt from ocean water to make fresh water.
People can conserve water by fixing leaks, taking shorter showers, turning off the faucet while brushing teeth and watering lawns in the early morning.

# rocks, soil and landforms situations
Rocks with rounded, smooth edges were worn down by moving water in a stream or by waves.
Rocks found in a river are usually smooth and rounded because the water tumbles them against each other.
Sharp, angular rock pieces have not been carried far by water.
Sand dunes are formed by wind depositing sand.
A delta forms where a river slows down and deposits sediment as it enters a lake or ocean.
A river carries larger rocks when it flows fast; when it slows down it deposits sediment.
The largest sediments are deposited first when a river slows; the smallest, like clay, are carried farthest.
Rivers flow faster down steep slopes and cause more erosion.
A meandering river erodes the outside of its bends and deposits sediment on the inside.
A canyon such as the Grand Canyon was carved by a river eroding rock over millions of years.
Glaciers carve U-shaped valleys, while rivers carve V-shaped valleys.
Glaciers carry and drop rocks and boulders, forming moraines and leaving scratches on bedrock.
Large boulders found far from any similar rock were often carried there by glaciers.
Ocean waves erode beaches and cliffs, and they carry and deposit sand along the shore.
Seawalls, jetties and planting dune grass are used to reduce beach erosion.
Plants reduce erosion because their roots hold soil in place.
Terraces, contour plowing and cover crops reduce soil erosion on farms.
Leaving a field bare without plants increases soil erosion by wind and water.
Overgrazing by animals removes plants and increases erosion.
The Dust Bowl happened when drought and poor farming left soil bare, so wind blew it away.
Weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces; erosion carries the pieces away; deposition drops them in a new place.
Ice wedging is weathering that happens when water seeps into cracks, freezes, expands and breaks the rock.
Potholes form in roads when water seeps into cracks, freezes, expands and breaks the pavement.
Plant roots growing into cracks can break rocks apart.
Chemical weathering happens when water, oxygen or acids change the minerals in rock, such as rusting or limestone dissolving.
Acid rain and the acid in rainwater slowly dissolve limestone and marble, forming caves.
Caves form when acidic groundwater dissolves limestone underground.
Weathering happens fastest in warm, wet climates.
Soil forms from weathered rock mixed with humus, the decayed remains of plants and animals.
Soil formation takes hundreds to thousands of years.
Topsoil is the top layer of soil, rich in humus and nutrients for plants.
Sedimentary rocks such as sandstone, shale and limestone form in layers from sediments pressed and cemented together.
Fossils are usually found in sedimentary rock.
Igneous rocks such as granite, basalt, obsidian and pumice form when melted rock cools.
Granite has large crystals because the magma cooled slowly underground.
Basalt and obsidian have small or no crystals because lava cooled quickly at the surface.
Pumice has many holes from gas bubbles and is so light it can float on water.
Metamorphic rocks such as marble, slate and gneiss form when rocks are changed by heat and pressure.
Limestone changes into marble, shale changes into slate, and granite changes into gneiss by heat and pressure.
Rocks are classified as igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic by how they formed.
Minerals are identified by properties such as hardness, streak, luster, cleavage, color and reaction with acid.
Calcite and limestone fizz when a drop of acid is placed on them.
The streak of a mineral is the color of its powder when it is rubbed on a streak plate.
A mineral that scratches glass is harder than glass.
Quartz is hard enough to scratch glass; talc is so soft it can be scratched with a fingernail.
Diamond is the hardest natural mineral.
Coal formed from the remains of ancient swamp plants buried and pressed for millions of years.
Oil and natural gas formed from the remains of tiny ancient sea organisms buried under sediment.

# plate tectonics, earthquakes, volcanoes
Earth's crust is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates that move a few centimeters each year.
Plates move because of convection currents of hot rock in the mantle.
Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along the edges of tectonic plates.
The Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean has many volcanoes and earthquakes because it is along plate boundaries.
When two continental plates collide, the crust folds and pushes up to form tall mountains such as the Himalayas.
When an ocean plate moves under a continental plate, a deep trench and a chain of volcanoes form; this is subduction.
When plates pull apart under the ocean, magma rises and forms new sea floor at a mid-ocean ridge.
When plates pull apart on land, a rift valley forms, as in East Africa.
When plates slide past each other, earthquakes happen along a fault such as the San Andreas Fault.
Sea floor rocks are youngest near the mid-ocean ridge and get older farther away, which is evidence of sea-floor spreading.
The Hawaiian Islands formed as a plate moved over a hot spot of magma in the mantle.
Evidence that the continents were once joined in Pangaea includes matching coastlines, matching fossils and matching rock layers on different continents.
Fossils of tropical plants found in Antarctica show that the continent was once in a warmer place.
An earthquake is the shaking of the ground caused by the sudden movement of rock along a fault.
A seismograph records the waves from an earthquake.
Scientists use seismic waves from earthquakes to learn about Earth's interior layers.
An earthquake under the ocean can cause a tsunami, a giant ocean wave.
Buildings in earthquake areas are built with flexible frames and bases that let them sway without collapsing.
A volcano erupts when magma rises through the crust; magma that reaches the surface is called lava.
Volcanic eruptions can form new land, such as islands.
Volcanic ash makes soil very fertile over time.
Mountains form by folding, faulting and volcanic activity.
Old mountains such as the Appalachians are lower and more rounded because they have been weathered and eroded for a long time.
Young mountains such as the Rocky Mountains and the Himalayas are tall and jagged.

# Earth's history and fossils
In undisturbed rock layers, the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest are at the top; this is the law of superposition.
A fossil found in a lower rock layer is usually older than a fossil in a layer above it.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a large area; they help date rock layers.
Finding the same index fossil in rock layers in two places shows the layers are about the same age.
Fossils of sea animals found on mountains show that the area was once under an ocean.
Fossils of fish or shells found in a desert show that the area was once covered by water.
Fossils show that many kinds of organisms that once lived on Earth are now extinct.
Fossils can form when an organism is buried quickly in sediment, mud or sand, and its hard parts are replaced by minerals.
Fossils include bones, shells, teeth, footprints, imprints of leaves, and insects trapped in amber.
Insects can be preserved whole in amber, which is hardened tree sap.
Mammoths have been preserved whole in ice.
Coal layers with plant fossils show that the area was once a swampy forest.
Tree rings show the age of a tree and the climate of each year; wide rings mean good growing years.
Ice cores from glaciers contain air bubbles that show what Earth's atmosphere and climate were like long ago.
Radioactive dating measures the decay of elements in rocks to find their absolute age.
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.
Dinosaurs became extinct about 66 million years ago, probably after a large asteroid struck Earth.
