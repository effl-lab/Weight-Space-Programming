# Heredity and genetics
Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to offspring.
Traits are characteristics of an organism such as eye color, hair color, height, flower color and seed shape.
Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring through genes, such as eye color, hair color, dimples, blood type and freckles.
Acquired traits are developed during an organism's life and are not inherited, such as scars, a suntan, learned skills, big muscles from exercise and dyed hair.
Riding a bike, reading, speaking a language and playing a musical instrument are learned or acquired, not inherited.
Some traits are affected by both genes and the environment, such as height and weight affected by diet.
Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes that carry the instructions for traits.
DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid, is the molecule that carries genetic information in the nucleus of cells.
DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder called a double helix.
The bases of DNA are adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine; adenine pairs with thymine and guanine pairs with cytosine.
Chromosomes are found in the nucleus and are made of DNA and protein.
Offspring receive half of their chromosomes and genes from each parent.
Each parent gives one allele, or form of a gene, for each trait to the offspring.
Alleles are different forms of the same gene.
A dominant allele hides the effect of a recessive allele; dominant alleles are written with a capital letter.
A recessive trait shows only when an organism has two recessive alleles, written with lowercase letters.
Homozygous means having two identical alleles for a trait, such as TT or tt; heterozygous means having two different alleles, such as Tt.
The genotype is the genetic makeup, the alleles an organism has; the phenotype is the physical appearance or trait that shows.
A Punnett square is a chart used to predict the possible genotypes of offspring from a cross of two parents.
A cross of two heterozygous parents, Tt and Tt, produces offspring in a ratio of 3 dominant to 1 recessive, or 25 percent recessive.
A cross of TT and tt produces all Tt offspring that show the dominant trait.
Gregor Mendel is called the father of genetics; he studied inheritance of traits in pea plants.
Mendel crossed tall and short pea plants and found that traits are passed on by factors now called genes.
Sexual reproduction combines genetic material from two parents, so offspring are genetically different from each parent.
Sexual reproduction produces variation among offspring, which helps species survive changes in the environment.
Asexual reproduction involves only one parent and produces offspring that are genetically identical to the parent, called clones.
Types of asexual reproduction include binary fission in bacteria, budding in yeast and hydra, regeneration in sea stars and planaria, spores and vegetative propagation in plants.
A sea star can regrow a lost arm, an example of regeneration.
Asexual reproduction is fast and needs only one parent, but produces little variation.
A mutation is a change in the DNA of a gene; mutations can be harmful, helpful or have no effect.
Mutations in sex cells can be passed on to offspring.
Genetic engineering changes the genes of an organism, such as crops that resist pests.
Selective breeding is when humans choose plants or animals with desired traits to breed, such as dogs, cows and crops.
Cloning produces a genetically identical copy of an organism.
Sex chromosomes determine gender: females have XX and males have XY.
Identical twins form from one fertilized egg and have the same DNA; fraternal twins form from two eggs.
Offspring look similar to their parents because they inherit genes from their parents.
# Evolution and natural selection
Evolution is the process by which species change gradually over many generations.
Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection after studying finches and tortoises on the Galapagos Islands.
Natural selection is the process in which organisms with traits best suited to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce and pass those traits to their offspring.
In natural selection, there is variation among individuals, more offspring are produced than can survive, and the best adapted survive.
Survival of the fittest means organisms best adapted to their environment survive and reproduce.
Variation, differences in traits among individuals of a species, is needed for natural selection.
Over many generations, helpful traits become more common in a population.
Darwin's finches have different beak shapes adapted to different foods on different islands.
Peppered moths became darker during the industrial revolution because dark moths were better camouflaged on soot covered trees and survived predators.
Bacteria can evolve resistance to antibiotics because resistant bacteria survive and reproduce.
Insects can become resistant to pesticides over many generations through natural selection.
Evidence for evolution includes fossils, similar body structures, embryos and DNA of different organisms.
Homologous structures, such as the arm of a human, wing of a bat and flipper of a whale, have similar bones and show common ancestry.
Vestigial structures are body parts that no longer serve a function, such as the human appendix.
The fossil record shows that life forms have changed over time and many species have become extinct.
Species that are closely related have more similar DNA.
Extinction occurs when a species cannot adapt to changes in its environment.
A new species can form when populations are separated by a geographic barrier for a long time.
