# more life science, phrased the way questions ask
Living things are called organisms.
An organism is any individual living thing such as a plant, animal, fungus or bacterium.
Plants and animals both need water, food or nutrients, air and space to survive.
Animals get energy by eating food; plants get energy from sunlight.
All organisms need energy to carry out life processes such as growth, movement and reproduction.
Animals depend on plants for food, oxygen and shelter.
Plants depend on animals for pollination, seed dispersal and carbon dioxide.
Most animals cannot make their own food, so they must eat plants or other animals.
Green plants are the only organisms in most food chains that can make their own food using sunlight.
Organisms that make their own food are called autotrophs; organisms that eat other organisms are heterotrophs.
Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction, from the Sun to producers to consumers, while matter is recycled.
In the food chain grass, rabbit, fox: grass is the producer, the rabbit is the herbivore and primary consumer, and the fox is the carnivore and secondary consumer.
In the food chain algae, small fish, large fish, heron: the algae are the producers.
A food chain usually begins with a plant and ends with a predator.
Hawks, owls and snakes eat mice; if the mice disappear, the hawks, owls and snakes have less food.
If snakes are removed from an ecosystem, the population of mice they ate may increase.
If wolves are removed, deer populations may grow too large and overgraze plants.
When a population becomes too large, food becomes scarce and many individuals starve.
Squirrels, mice and many birds eat seeds and nuts.
Grasshoppers, caterpillars and cows eat leaves and grass.
Frogs and many birds eat insects.
Bears are omnivores that eat berries, fish, insects and other animals.
Owls swallow prey whole and cough up pellets of bones and fur, which students dissect to learn what owls eat.
Earthworms eat dead leaves and soil and improve the soil.
Worms, bacteria and fungi in the soil decompose dead plants and animals so nutrients return to the soil for plants.
All organisms in an ecosystem depend on decomposers such as bacteria in the soil to recycle nutrients.
Plants and animals in an ecosystem are interdependent: they depend on each other.
Bacteria and other microorganisms in the soil are needed by all the organisms in an ecosystem because they recycle nutrients.
Rotting logs provide food and shelter for insects, fungi, moss and other decomposers.
# animal survival and adaptation, more phrasings
Animals that change color can hide from predators or sneak up on prey.
Some animals have specialized cells in their skin that let them change color rapidly to blend in with their surroundings and hide from predators.
A brown moth resting on brown tree bark is hard for birds to see, which helps it survive.
Animals with coloring that matches their environment are less likely to be eaten by predators.
A white rabbit living in a snowy area is camouflaged and better protected from predators.
Thick fur, blubber and feathers help animals keep body heat in cold environments and retain body heat.
Sweating and panting help animals cool down.
Elephants flap their large ears to cool off.
Some animals have long legs to run quickly from predators, such as deer, antelope and horses.
Sharp claws and teeth help predators catch and eat prey.
Large eyes help nocturnal animals see in dim light.
Keen senses of smell and hearing help animals find food and detect danger.
A woodpecker has a strong pointed beak to drill holes in trees to find insects.
A pelican has a pouch in its beak to scoop up fish.
Long necks and legs help wading birds like herons find food in shallow water.
Some fish live in schools for protection from predators.
Some animals play dead to avoid being eaten, such as the opossum.
Prey animals often have eyes on the sides of their heads to watch for predators; predators have eyes in front to judge distance.
Animals that live in the ocean depths may glow, called bioluminescence, to attract prey or mates.
Gills, fins, and streamlined bodies are adaptations for living in water.
Lungs, legs and dry skin or scales are adaptations for living on land.
Moving to a new environment can make survival hard if an animal's adaptations do not fit the new conditions.
Animals respond to seasonal changes by migrating, hibernating, growing thicker fur, storing food or changing color.
Birds fluff their feathers in winter to trap air and stay warm.
Many animals give birth in the spring when food is plentiful.
Shorter days and colder temperatures in the fall signal animals to migrate or prepare for hibernation.
Animals use the Sun, stars, Earth's magnetic field and landmarks to navigate during migration.
# plant adaptations and responses
Plants respond to their environment: stems grow toward light, roots grow toward water and down with gravity.
Some plants close their flowers at night and open them during the day.
Leaves turn to face the sun to get more light for photosynthesis.
Plants in the rain forest have large leaves to capture light in the shade and drip tips to shed rain.
Water lilies have large flat floating leaves with stomata on top to get sunlight and air.
Plants in dry environments have thick waxy leaves or needles to reduce water loss.
Some plants produce chemicals or bad tastes to keep animals from eating them.
Bright colored flowers and sweet smells attract insects and birds for pollination.
Flowers that bloom at night are often white and pollinated by moths or bats.
Fruits that are sweet and colorful attract animals that eat them and spread the seeds in their droppings.
Seeds can stay dormant for a long time until conditions are right for germination.
Trees lose water through their leaves; losing leaves in winter keeps trees from losing water when the ground is frozen.
# cells, more phrasings
Cells get the energy they need from food through cellular respiration in the mitochondria.
Cells use the energy from respiration to grow, repair and do their jobs.
The cell membrane surrounds the cell and lets nutrients in and wastes out.
Plant cells are usually rectangular because of the rigid cell wall; animal cells are usually round or irregular.
A microscope is needed to see cells, bacteria and other microorganisms.
To view cells, a thin slice of the sample is placed on a glass slide and covered with a cover slip.
The magnification of a microscope is the eyepiece power times the objective lens power.
Onion skin cells and cheek cells are commonly observed in school labs.
Iodine stain makes the nucleus of cells easier to see.
Cells in the body are replaced as old cells die; skin cells are constantly replaced.
Stem cells can develop into many different types of cells.
Inside cells, molecules carry messages; in the body, the endocrine system uses hormones as chemical messengers that travel in the blood to target cells.
Hormones carry messages from glands to organs, similar to how signaling molecules carry messages from the cell membrane to the nucleus.
# human body, more phrasings
The heart and lungs work together to supply the body with oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.
The circulatory system and respiratory system work together to deliver oxygen to cells.
The digestive system and circulatory system work together to deliver nutrients to cells.
The muscular system and skeletal system work together to move the body.
The nervous system and muscular system work together when you move; the brain sends signals to the muscles.
The nervous system sends messages between the brain and the rest of the body through nerves.
Blood carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones and wastes around the body.
Blood vessels include arteries, veins and capillaries.
When you exercise, your heart rate and breathing rate increase to supply more oxygen to muscles.
After exercise, the heart rate slowly returns to its resting rate.
The heart is a muscle that never stops working during a person's life.
Teeth break food into smaller pieces, which increases surface area for digestion; this is mechanical digestion.
Saliva, stomach acid and enzymes chemically break down food; this is chemical digestion.
Nutrients from digested food are absorbed into the blood through the walls of the small intestine.
Sweat glands in the skin release sweat that cools the body by evaporation.
The skin is the body's first line of defense against germs.
Mucus and tiny hairs called cilia in the nose and airways trap dust and germs.
Fever is one way the body fights infection.
A broken bone heals as new bone cells are produced by cell division.
Calcium from milk and dairy products helps build strong bones and teeth.
The eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin are sense organs that send information to the brain.
The eye's retina contains cells that detect light and color and send signals to the brain through the optic nerve.
The pupil gets smaller in bright light and larger in dim light to control the amount of light entering the eye.
Eyeglasses use lenses to correct vision by focusing light on the retina.
Hearing loss can be caused by loud noises over a long time.
Children grow because their cells divide and increase in number.
Babies have more bones than adults because some bones fuse together as they grow.
Humans inherit traits from both parents, such as eye color, hair color, skin color and blood type.
A person's height and weight are affected by both heredity and environment such as nutrition and exercise.
# microorganisms
Some microorganisms are helpful, used to make foods such as bread, cheese, yogurt and vinegar.
Yeast are microorganisms that cause bread dough to rise by producing carbon dioxide gas.
Bacteria in the human intestines help digest food and make vitamins.
Harmful microorganisms called pathogens cause disease.
Microorganisms grow best in warm, moist places with food.
Salting, drying, freezing, canning and refrigerating food slow the growth of microorganisms that cause food to spoil.
Food spoils when bacteria and mold grow on it.
Bacteria can be grown in a petri dish on agar to study them.
Bacteria are found on hands, surfaces, doorknobs and in the air; washing hands removes them.
Students growing bacteria should wear gloves, wash hands, keep dishes sealed and avoid eating or drinking in the laboratory.
Diseases can be spread by contact, air, water, food and insects such as mosquitoes and ticks.
Mosquitoes can spread diseases such as malaria and West Nile virus.
Ticks can spread Lyme disease.
Clean drinking water and sewage treatment prevent the spread of diseases such as cholera.
