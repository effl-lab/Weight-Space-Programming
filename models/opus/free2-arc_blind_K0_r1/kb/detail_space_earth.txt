# space details
The Sun makes up more than 99 percent of the mass of the solar system.
The Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth; over one million Earths could fit inside the Sun.
The Sun's core is about 15 million degrees Celsius; its surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius.
The layers of the Sun include the core, radiative zone, convection zone, photosphere, chromosphere and corona.
The solar wind is a stream of charged particles from the Sun; it causes auroras, the northern lights, when it hits Earth's magnetic field.
Earth's magnetic field protects living things from harmful particles of the solar wind.
Earth is the only planet with an atmosphere rich in oxygen and with abundant liquid water.
Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field protect it from radiation and small meteoroids.
Earth takes about 24 hours to rotate once and 365.25 days to revolve around the Sun.
Earth's average distance from the Sun is called one astronomical unit.
A day on Venus is longer than its year because Venus rotates very slowly.
Venus rotates in the opposite direction from most planets.
Venus is about the same size as Earth and is sometimes called Earth's twin.
Mercury has extreme temperatures because it has almost no atmosphere to hold heat.
Mars has seasons because its axis is tilted like Earth's.
Mars has polar ice caps of frozen water and carbon dioxide.
Mars has a thin atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide.
Robotic rovers such as Curiosity and Perseverance explore the surface of Mars.
Jupiter has the most mass of any planet and dozens of moons, including Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.
Ganymede, a moon of Jupiter, is the largest moon in the solar system.
Europa, a moon of Jupiter, may have a liquid water ocean under its icy surface.
Saturn is the least dense planet; it would float in water.
Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a thick atmosphere.
Uranus and Neptune are called ice giants.
The gas giants have no solid surface.
A planet's year is the time it takes to revolve around the Sun; a planet's day is the time it takes to rotate once.
The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its period of revolution and the slower it moves in its orbit.
The inner planets have few or no moons; the outer planets have many moons.
All planets orbit the Sun in the same direction and nearly in the same plane.
Gravity pulled gas and dust together to form the Sun and planets about 4.6 billion years ago.
The Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune contains icy bodies such as Pluto.
Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006 because it has not cleared its orbit of other objects.
Ceres is a dwarf planet in the asteroid belt.
Comets have orbits that are long ellipses and come from the outer solar system.
Halley's Comet returns about every 76 years.
A comet's tail always points away from the Sun because it is pushed by the solar wind.
Meteor showers happen when Earth passes through the trail of dust left by a comet.
Most meteoroids burn up in the atmosphere because of friction with the air.
Craters on Earth are fewer than on the Moon because Earth's atmosphere burns up meteoroids and erosion wears craters away.
Earth's Moon is about one fourth the diameter of Earth.
The Moon is about 384,000 kilometers from Earth.
The Moon formed when a large object collided with the early Earth.
The Moon has no light of its own; the half of the Moon facing the Sun is always lit.
The amount of the Moon's lit side that we see from Earth changes during the month, causing phases.
A full moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise.
A first quarter moon appears as a half circle lit on the right side in the Northern Hemisphere.
The Moon looks larger near the horizon, but it is really the same size.
The same side of the Moon always faces Earth; the far side cannot be seen from Earth.
Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted compared to Earth's orbit.
A total solar eclipse can be seen only from a small area of Earth inside the Moon's shadow.
During a total solar eclipse, the sky becomes dark and the Sun's corona can be seen.
During a lunar eclipse the Moon often looks red.
High tides happen on the side of Earth facing the Moon and on the opposite side.
The Sun's gravity also affects tides, but less than the Moon because the Sun is much farther away.
The time between high tides is about 12 hours and 25 minutes.
Stars produce light and heat by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
Stars differ in size, color, temperature, brightness and age.
Red dwarf stars are small and cool; blue giant stars are large and very hot.
The Sun will become a red giant in about 5 billion years.
Betelgeuse is a red supergiant star in Orion; Rigel is a blue white supergiant.
Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky.
Polaris, the North Star, can be found using the pointer stars of the Big Dipper.
Polaris is used to find the direction north at night.
The Big Dipper is part of the constellation Ursa Major, the Great Bear.
Ancient people used constellations to keep track of seasons and to navigate.
Stars seem to twinkle because their light passes through moving layers of Earth's atmosphere.
Apparent brightness is how bright a star looks from Earth; absolute brightness is how bright it really is.
A star that is closer can look brighter than a larger star that is farther away.
Light from distant stars takes many years to reach Earth, so we see stars as they were in the past.
The Andromeda Galaxy is the nearest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way.
Our solar system is located in one of the spiral arms of the Milky Way, about two thirds of the way out from the center.
The Milky Way contains more than 100 billion stars.
From Earth the Milky Way appears as a band of faint light across the night sky.
Edwin Hubble discovered that galaxies are moving away from us and that the universe is expanding.
# history of astronomy and space exploration
Ptolemy described an Earth centered, geocentric model of the universe.
Nicolaus Copernicus proposed that Earth and the other planets revolve around the Sun.
Galileo Galilei was the first to use a telescope to study the sky; he saw craters on the Moon, sunspots, the phases of Venus and four moons of Jupiter.
Johannes Kepler showed that planets travel in elliptical orbits and move faster when closer to the Sun.
Isaac Newton explained that gravity keeps the planets in orbit around the Sun.
Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union in 1957, was the first artificial satellite.
Yuri Gagarin was the first human in space in 1961.
Neil Armstrong was the first person to walk on the Moon in 1969.
The Voyager probes have traveled beyond the planets to the edge of the solar system.
The space shuttle was a reusable spacecraft used to carry astronauts and satellites.
Telescopes in space avoid the blurring caused by Earth's atmosphere.
Radio telescopes detect radio waves from stars and galaxies.
Astronauts on the International Space Station study how living things grow in microgravity.
Space exploration has led to inventions such as memory foam, water filters and better medical imaging.
Living on the Moon or Mars would require bringing air, water, food and protection from radiation and extreme temperatures.
# earth details
Earth's surface is about 71 percent water and 29 percent land.
The seven continents are Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America and South America.
The five oceans are the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic Oceans.
Earth's crust is made mostly of oxygen and silicon, found in silicate minerals like quartz and feldspar.
Earth's interior is hot because of heat left from its formation and the decay of radioactive elements.
Temperature and pressure increase with depth inside Earth.
Scientists learn about Earth's interior by studying how seismic waves travel through it.
Oceanic crust is made of dense basalt; continental crust is made of less dense granite.
Oceanic crust is thinner and younger than continental crust.
The youngest rocks on the ocean floor are found at the mid ocean ridge, and rocks get older farther from the ridge.
The Mid Atlantic Ridge is a divergent boundary where the Atlantic Ocean is getting wider.
The Andes Mountains formed where the oceanic Nazca plate sinks beneath the South American plate.
Iceland sits on the Mid Atlantic Ridge and has many volcanoes and geysers.
The East African Rift Valley formed where the African plate is splitting apart.
Japan, Indonesia and the west coast of the Americas have many earthquakes and volcanoes because they lie along the Ring of Fire.
Mount St Helens in Washington erupted explosively in 1980.
Yellowstone National Park sits above a hot spot and has many geysers such as Old Faithful.
The Appalachian Mountains formed hundreds of millions of years ago when continents collided.
The Rocky Mountains formed tens of millions of years ago.
New York State's Adirondack Mountains are made of very old metamorphic rock.
Most of New York State was covered by glaciers during the last ice age, which ended about 10,000 years ago.
Niagara Falls formed after the last ice age and is slowly eroding upstream.
The Great Lakes contain about one fifth of the world's surface fresh water.
The Mississippi River deposits sediment that forms a large delta in the Gulf of Mexico.
The Sahara is the largest hot desert; Antarctica is the largest cold desert.
Mount Everest in the Himalayas is the highest mountain on Earth.
Earthquake magnitude increases by about ten times in ground shaking for each whole number on the Richter scale.
Earthquakes cannot be predicted exactly, but scientists can identify areas at risk.
Seismologists locate an earthquake's epicenter using data from at least three seismograph stations.
Scientists monitor volcanoes for small earthquakes, swelling of the ground and gas release to predict eruptions.
Volcanic ash can damage airplane engines, crops and lungs.
Lahars are mudflows of volcanic ash and water.
Pyroclastic flows are fast moving clouds of hot gas and rock from explosive eruptions.
Weathering and erosion are slow processes that take thousands to millions of years to shape the land.
Caves form when acidic groundwater slowly dissolves limestone.
Arches form when wind and water erode softer rock and leave harder rock behind.
Hoodoos and mesas in deserts are shaped by wind and water erosion.
A river's speed depends on its slope and volume; steep mountain streams flow fast.
Rivers that flow over flat land move slowly and form wide meanders.
An oxbow lake forms when a meander is cut off from the river.
Alluvial fans form where streams leave mountains and deposit sediment.
Glacial ice moves slowly downhill under its own weight.
When glaciers melt, sea level rises.
Sea level was much lower during the ice ages because so much water was frozen in glaciers.
Permafrost is permanently frozen ground found in the tundra.
Soil in tropical rain forests is thin and poor in nutrients because heavy rains wash nutrients away.
Grassland soils are very fertile and are used for farming.
Desert soils are dry, sandy and contain little humus.
