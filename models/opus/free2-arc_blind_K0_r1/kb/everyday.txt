# everyday science, measurement and conservation phrasings
Long distances such as the distance between two cities are measured in kilometers or miles.
Short lengths such as the length of a pencil or a leaf are measured in centimeters with a ruler.
Very small lengths such as the width of a hair or an insect are measured in millimeters.
The length of a classroom or the height of a door is measured in meters.
The mass of a paper clip is about one gram; the mass of a textbook is about one kilogram.
The mass of a person, a dog or a car is measured in kilograms.
A small amount of liquid such as medicine is measured in milliliters; a large amount such as a bottle of soda is measured in liters.
Room temperature is about 20 to 25 degrees Celsius; normal human body temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius or 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit.
A hot summer day is about 30 to 35 degrees Celsius; water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius.
A metric ruler measures length in centimeters and millimeters.
A meter stick is used to measure the length of a room or the height of a plant.
A measuring tape is used to measure the distance a ball rolls or the length of a long object.
A triple beam balance measures the mass of objects in grams.
An electronic scale or balance measures mass.
A graduated cylinder is used to measure liquid volume accurately in milliliters.
Read the volume in a graduated cylinder at the bottom of the curved surface called the meniscus, at eye level.
A thermometer is used to measure the temperature of water, air or soil.
A stopwatch is used to time how long an event takes, such as how long it takes a ball to roll down a ramp.
A hand lens, or magnifier, is used to observe small details of objects such as insects, leaves, rocks and seeds.
A microscope is used to observe very small objects such as cells, bacteria and pond organisms that cannot be seen with the eye alone.
A telescope is used to observe the Moon, planets and stars.
A compass is used to find direction such as north.
A rain gauge collects and measures rainfall.
A wind vane shows which direction the wind is blowing from.
A spring scale measures the force needed to pull an object, in newtons.
Tongs, test tubes, beakers, flasks, droppers and petri dishes are common laboratory equipment.
A beaker holds and heats liquids; it measures volume only roughly.
An eyedropper or pipette transfers small amounts of liquid.
A hot plate or Bunsen burner is used to heat substances.
# conservation of water and energy at home
People can conserve water by taking shorter showers, turning off the faucet while brushing teeth, fixing leaky faucets and pipes, and running full loads of laundry.
Fixing a leaky faucet saves water that would be wasted.
Watering lawns in the early morning or evening reduces water lost to evaporation.
Collecting rainwater in rain barrels to water plants conserves water.
People can conserve energy by turning off lights, using energy efficient bulbs, adding insulation, and lowering the thermostat in winter.
Walking, biking, carpooling and using public transportation save gasoline and reduce pollution.
Recycling paper saves trees; recycling aluminum saves energy and ore.
Reusing a jar to store things, using both sides of paper, and donating old clothes are examples of reusing.
Buying products with less packaging reduces waste.
Turning off the water while brushing your teeth conserves water.
Using reusable shopping bags reduces plastic waste.
Composting food scraps reduces garbage and makes fertilizer.
Throwing trash in landfills uses land and can pollute groundwater.
Littering harms wildlife and pollutes water.
# sun safety and health in daily life
Wearing sunscreen, hats and sunglasses protects against ultraviolet rays from the Sun.
Never look directly at the Sun because it can damage your eyes, even during an eclipse.
Wearing a helmet when riding a bike protects the head and brain from injury.
Seat belts and air bags protect people in a car crash.
Washing fruits and vegetables removes dirt and germs.
Food should be stored in a refrigerator to keep bacteria from growing.
Drinking water and wearing light clothing helps prevent overheating on hot days.
Warm clothing, hats and gloves prevent frostbite and hypothermia in cold weather.
# technology and society
Inventions such as the telephone, light bulb, airplane, computer and internet changed how people live.
Before refrigeration, people preserved food by drying, salting, smoking or canning it.
Improved shipping and refrigeration have increased the types of food available for sale all year round.
Airplanes and ships allow food and goods to be transported around the world.
Medical technology such as X rays, vaccines and antibiotics has helped people live longer.
Cell phones send and receive signals using radio waves.
Global positioning system, GPS, uses satellites to find locations.
Robots are used to do dangerous or repetitive jobs and to explore places people cannot go, such as Mars.
Wind turbines, solar panels and electric cars are technologies that reduce the use of fossil fuels.
Dams provide hydroelectric power and water storage but change river habitats and block fish migration.
Pesticides increase crop yields but can harm helpful insects, birds and water supplies.
Fertilizers help crops grow but runoff can pollute lakes and streams.
Cities are warmer than the surrounding countryside because pavement and buildings absorb heat.
