# matter and chemistry in detail
All matter is made of tiny particles called atoms and molecules that are always moving.
The kinetic theory of matter states that particles of matter are in constant motion and move faster at higher temperatures.
When a solid melts, its particles gain energy and move more freely, but the particles themselves do not change.
Gases can be compressed into a smaller volume because their particles are far apart.
Liquids and solids are hard to compress because their particles are close together.
Gas pressure increases when the gas is heated in a closed container.
A gas fills the entire volume of any container it is in.
Liquids flow and take the shape of the bottom of their container.
Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flowing; honey has high viscosity and water has low viscosity.
Surface tension lets some insects walk on water and makes water form drops.
Water molecules stick together, which is called cohesion, and stick to other surfaces, which is called adhesion.
Capillary action is water climbing up narrow tubes, like water moving up a paper towel or a plant stem.
A paper towel absorbs water by capillary action.
Sponges and paper towels absorb liquids because they have many tiny holes.
Plastic and wax resist water; they are waterproof.
Brittle materials such as glass and chalk break easily when bent.
Elastic materials such as rubber bands and springs return to their original shape after being stretched.
Magnetic materials are attracted to magnets.
Solubility is the amount of a substance that dissolves in a given amount of solvent.
Gases such as carbon dioxide are more soluble in cold water; soda goes flat faster when warm.
Concentration is the amount of solute in a given amount of solution.
Diluting a solution by adding more water lowers its concentration.
Indicators such as red cabbage juice change color in acids and bases.
Neutralization occurs when an acid and a base react to form water and a salt.
Strong acids and bases can burn skin and eyes, so goggles and gloves must be worn.
Corrosion is the slow wearing away of metal by chemical reactions, such as rusting.
Flammable substances, such as gasoline, alcohol and paper, burn easily.
Reactivity describes how easily a substance combines with other substances.
Metals such as gold and platinum are unreactive, so they stay shiny for a long time.
Sodium and potassium react violently with water.
Oxygen is needed for burning and for cellular respiration.
Nitrogen gas is unreactive, so it is used to keep food fresh in packages.
Chlorine is added to water to kill bacteria.
Fluoride is added to toothpaste and water to prevent tooth decay.
Carbon forms more compounds than any other element; organic compounds contain carbon.
Diamond, graphite and coal are forms of carbon.
Silicon is used to make computer chips and glass.
Uranium is a radioactive element used as fuel in nuclear power plants.
Radioactive materials give off radiation that can be harmful to living things.
Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number in the periodic table.
An element's atomic number equals the number of protons in each atom.
The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in an atom.
Atoms of different elements have different numbers of protons.
Atoms gain or lose electrons to form ions; metals tend to lose electrons and nonmetals tend to gain them.
Ionic compounds such as salt form crystals and have high melting points.
Covalent bonds hold atoms together in molecules such as water, oxygen, carbon dioxide and methane.
In a chemical equation, the arrow means yields or produces.
Balancing chemical equations shows that atoms are conserved in chemical reactions.
Synthesis reactions combine substances; decomposition reactions break a compound into simpler substances.
# energy in detail
Energy can be transferred by moving objects, sound, light, heat and electric currents.
When energy changes form, the total amount of energy stays the same.
Useful energy is lost as heat in machines because of friction.
A perpetual motion machine is impossible because energy is always lost to friction as heat.
Food energy is measured in calories.
Plants store energy from sunlight as chemical energy; animals get this energy when they eat plants.
The energy in fossil fuels originally came from the Sun through photosynthesis by ancient plants.
Coal, oil and natural gas store chemical energy from ancient plants and animals.
Power plants burn fossil fuels to heat water into steam, which spins turbines connected to generators.
Hydroelectric dams change the gravitational potential energy of water into electricity.
Solar panels produce electricity only when light shines on them.
Batteries store energy that can be used later.
Rechargeable batteries change electrical energy into chemical energy when charging.
A stretched spring has elastic potential energy that changes to kinetic energy when released.
A wind up toy stores energy in a coiled spring.
A bow and arrow change elastic potential energy into kinetic energy.
Sound and light are forms of energy that travel as waves.
Electrical energy can be easily transformed into many other forms, which makes it very useful.
Thermal energy depends on temperature and the amount of matter; a bathtub of warm water has more thermal energy than a cup of boiling water.
Heat flows from the warmer object to the cooler object, never the other way on its own.
Cold is the absence of heat; cold does not flow, heat flows.
An ice pack feels cold because it absorbs heat from your skin.
A thermos has a vacuum layer that stops conduction and convection, and a shiny lining that reduces radiation.
Double pane windows have air or gas between the panes to insulate.
Animals living in cold climates have fur, feathers or fat that act as insulation.
# forces in detail
The force of gravity on an object is its weight.
On Earth, a mass of one kilogram weighs about 10 newtons.
Gravity acts on all objects all the time, even when they are not falling.
Every object with mass attracts every other object with mass.
Earth's gravity is stronger than the Moon's because Earth has more mass.
An object's weight would be greatest on Jupiter and less on the Moon or Mars.
Satellites stay in orbit because their forward motion balances the pull of gravity.
Friction always acts in the direction opposite to motion.
Static friction keeps an object from starting to move; sliding friction acts on moving objects; rolling friction is the smallest.
Fluid friction, or drag, acts on objects moving through water or air.
Streamlined shapes reduce air resistance and water resistance, as in race cars, airplanes, fish and birds.
Terminal velocity is reached when air resistance equals the force of gravity on a falling object.
Balanced forces can keep an object at rest or moving at a constant speed in a straight line.
A book resting on a table has balanced forces: gravity pulls down and the table pushes up.
In a tug of war, if both teams pull with equal force, the rope does not move.
Unbalanced forces are needed to change an object's speed or direction.
A moving object will keep moving forever unless a force like friction stops it.
It is harder to stop a heavy moving object than a light one at the same speed because it has more momentum.
Doubling the force on an object doubles its acceleration.
Action and reaction forces act on different objects.
A balloon flies forward when air rushes out the back, showing action and reaction.
A swimmer pushes the water back and moves forward.
A gun recoils backward when a bullet is fired forward.
Pressure equals force divided by area; snowshoes spread weight over a large area so you do not sink.
Sharp knives cut well because force is concentrated on a small area, which creates high pressure.
Water pressure increases with depth, so dams are thicker at the bottom.
# waves in detail
Waves carry energy, not matter, from one place to another.
In a water wave, the water moves up and down while the wave moves forward.
A floating ball bobs up and down as a wave passes but does not move along with the wave.
Sound waves are compressions and rarefactions of particles in a medium.
The speed of a wave equals its frequency times its wavelength.
Waves can be reflected, refracted, diffracted and absorbed.
Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or through openings.
Interference happens when two waves meet and combine.
Sound travels faster in warm air than in cold air.
The loudness of a sound depends on the energy of the vibrations.
High pitched sounds have high frequency and short wavelength.
Noise pollution can damage hearing and disturb wildlife.
Light from the Sun is made of many colors that together look white.
Visible light is a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum.
Radio waves have the longest wavelengths and lowest frequencies; gamma rays have the shortest wavelengths and highest frequencies.
Ultraviolet light can damage skin and eyes and cause sunburn; sunscreen blocks it.
Infrared cameras detect heat given off by warm objects, such as animals at night.
Microwave ovens heat food by making water molecules vibrate.
X rays are used by doctors to see bones and by airports to check luggage.
Optical fibers carry light signals for phone and internet communication.
Light slows down when it passes from air into water or glass, causing it to bend.
A concave lens makes objects appear smaller; a convex lens can make objects appear larger.
Nearsighted people see near objects clearly and need concave lenses; farsighted people need convex lenses.
The lens of the eye focuses light onto the retina at the back of the eye.
A camera works like an eye: it has a lens that focuses light and an opening that controls light.
# scientific practices in detail
Scientists ask questions about the natural world that can be answered by investigation.
A testable question can be answered by collecting data in an experiment.
Before starting an experiment, a scientist should research the topic and state a hypothesis.
The first step in a scientific investigation is usually to ask a question or identify a problem.
After collecting data, scientists analyze it to look for patterns and draw conclusions.
Scientists communicate results so other scientists can repeat the investigation and check the results.
A good experiment tests only one variable at a time.
If two variables are changed at once, it is impossible to know which one caused the result.
The control in an experiment shows what happens when the independent variable is not changed.
In an experiment testing fertilizer on plants, the plants without fertilizer are the control group.
Variables that should be kept the same are the amount of water, amount of light, type of soil, type of plant and temperature.
Measurements should be repeated and averaged to reduce error.
The mean, or average, is the sum of the values divided by the number of values.
The range is the difference between the highest and lowest values.
Data that do not fit the pattern are called outliers and may be caused by errors.
Results are more reliable when an experiment has a large sample size and many trials.
Scientists use metric units so that measurements can be easily shared and compared.
A hypothesis can be supported or not supported by data; it is never proven absolutely.
A scientific theory explains a large set of observations and can change if new evidence is found.
Scientists use models, such as diagrams, globes, maps, computer simulations and physical models, to study things that are too large, too small or too dangerous to study directly.
A map is a model of an area of Earth; a globe is a model of Earth.
A model of the solar system shows the Sun at the center with planets orbiting it.
Scientists use computers to make models that predict weather, climate and the paths of hurricanes.
Observations are made with the senses: sight, hearing, touch, smell and taste.
Scientific instruments extend the senses: microscopes, telescopes, thermometers and scales.
Classifying is grouping objects by their similar properties.
Comparing means finding how things are alike and different.
A bias can affect results; scientists use controls and repeat trials to reduce bias.
Scientists must report their data honestly, even when the results do not support their hypothesis.
Peer review is when other scientists evaluate research before it is published.
Science and technology affect each other: new tools lead to new discoveries, and discoveries lead to new technology.
Scientists work in teams and share ideas, so scientific knowledge grows over time.
A good scientist is curious, skeptical, careful, open minded and honest.
Wearing goggles, following directions and knowing where safety equipment is located keep students safe in the lab.
Glassware should be checked for cracks before use, and broken glass should be swept up, not picked up with hands.
Heated glass looks the same as cool glass, so it should be handled with tongs or heat resistant gloves.
Never point a heated test tube toward anyone.
Chemicals should be labeled and never mixed without directions.
Long hair should be tied back near flames.
If a fire starts, tell the teacher; a fire blanket or fire extinguisher can be used.
After working with living things, soil or chemicals, wash hands thoroughly.
