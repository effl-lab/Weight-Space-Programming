# matter and its properties (second set)
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space; air, water, rocks and living things are all matter.
Light, heat, sound and energy are not matter because they do not have mass or take up space.
A balloon gets bigger when air is blown into it, which shows that air takes up space and is matter.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object; it is measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
An object's mass stays the same everywhere, on Earth, on the Moon or in space.
Weight is the force of gravity pulling on an object's mass; it is measured in newtons with a spring scale.
An object weighs less on the Moon than on Earth because the Moon's gravity is weaker, but its mass is the same.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up; liquid volume is measured in liters or milliliters with a graduated cylinder.
The volume of a rectangular solid is length times width times height, measured in cubic centimeters.
The volume of an irregular object can be found by water displacement: the rise in water level in a graduated cylinder equals the object's volume.
One milliliter of volume equals one cubic centimeter.
Density is the amount of mass in a given volume; density equals mass divided by volume.
The density of pure water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter or 1 gram per milliliter.
Objects that are less dense than water float; objects that are more dense than water sink.
A large log floats while a small pebble sinks because wood is less dense than water and rock is more dense.
Cutting an object into pieces does not change its density.
Oil floats on water because oil is less dense than water and the two do not mix.
Liquids of different densities can form layers, with the densest liquid at the bottom.
Ice floats on water because water expands when it freezes, making ice less dense than liquid water.
Because ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down and fish can survive under the ice in winter.
Helium balloons rise because helium is less dense than air.
Physical properties can be observed or measured without changing what a substance is, such as color, shape, size, mass, volume, density, texture, hardness, odor, melting point, boiling point, magnetism, solubility and conductivity.
Chemical properties describe how a substance can change into a new substance, such as flammability, the ability to burn, the ability to rust, and how it reacts with acid or oxygen.
Flammability is the ability of a substance to burn.
Texture describes how the surface of something feels, such as rough, smooth, bumpy or soft.
Properties used to sort objects include color, shape, size, texture, weight, hardness and whether they float, sink or are attracted by a magnet.
Malleable materials such as gold, copper and aluminum can be hammered or rolled into thin sheets.
Ductile materials such as copper can be drawn into wires.
Brittle materials such as glass and pottery break or shatter easily.
Flexible materials such as rubber and cloth bend without breaking.
Elastic materials such as rubber bands and springs return to their original shape after being stretched.
Absorbent materials such as sponges, paper towels and cotton soak up liquids.
Waterproof materials such as plastic and rubber do not let water through.
Transparent materials such as clear glass let almost all light through; translucent materials such as wax paper and frosted glass let some light through; opaque materials such as wood and metal block light.
Metals are usually shiny, malleable, ductile, and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Nonmetals are usually dull, brittle and poor conductors; many are gases at room temperature.
Metalloids such as silicon have properties of both metals and nonmetals and are used in computer chips.
Mercury is the only metal that is a liquid at room temperature.
# states of matter (second set)
The three common states of matter are solid, liquid and gas; plasma is a fourth state found in stars and lightning.
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume; its particles are packed tightly and vibrate in fixed positions.
A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container, and its particles are close together but slide past each other.
A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume; it spreads out to fill its container, and its particles are far apart and move quickly and freely.
Gases can be compressed, or squeezed into a smaller volume, because their particles are far apart; solids and liquids are hard to compress.
Air in a bicycle tire or a basketball is compressed gas; pumping in more air increases the pressure.
Particles of matter are always moving; they move faster when heated and slower when cooled.
Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy, the energy of motion, of the particles in a substance.
Adding heat makes particles move faster and spread apart; removing heat makes particles move slower and come closer together.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled.
Bridges and sidewalks have expansion joints, gaps that let the material expand in hot weather without cracking.
Power lines sag more in summer because the wires expand in the heat.
A balloon shrinks in a freezer and expands in a warm place because the gas inside contracts when cooled and expands when heated.
The liquid in a thermometer rises when it gets warmer because the liquid expands.
Melting is the change from solid to liquid when heat is added.
Freezing is the change from liquid to solid when heat is removed.
Evaporation is the change from liquid to gas at the surface of a liquid; boiling is rapid evaporation throughout a liquid at its boiling point.
Condensation is the change from gas to liquid when heat is removed.
Sublimation is the change from solid directly to gas, as when dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide, turns into gas, or snow disappears without melting.
Deposition is the change from gas directly to solid, as when frost forms from water vapor.
Melting, evaporation, boiling and sublimation absorb heat energy; freezing, condensation and deposition release heat energy.
The melting point and freezing point of a substance are the same temperature.
Pure water freezes and ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius, or 32 degrees Fahrenheit.
Pure water boils at 100 degrees Celsius, or 212 degrees Fahrenheit, at sea level.
During melting or boiling, the temperature of a substance stays the same until the change of state is complete, because the energy is used to change the state.
Water evaporates faster when it is warmer, when there is wind, when the air is dry, and when it has more surface area.
A wet towel spread out dries faster than a folded towel because more surface area is exposed to the air.
Evaporation cools a surface because the fastest moving particles escape; this is why sweating cools the body.
Adding salt to water lowers its freezing point, which is why salt is spread on icy roads.
Adding salt or sugar to water raises its boiling point slightly.
Changes of state are physical changes because the substance stays the same substance; ice, liquid water and steam are all water.
The mass of a substance stays the same when it changes state; melted ice has the same mass as the ice.
Water vapor is invisible; the white cloud above a boiling kettle is tiny droplets of condensed water.
# atoms, elements and compounds (second set)
All matter is made of tiny particles called atoms.
An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still has the properties of that element.
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons have a positive charge, electrons have a negative charge and neutrons have no charge.
Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the center of the atom; electrons move around the nucleus in an electron cloud.
Almost all of the mass of an atom is in its nucleus; electrons have very little mass.
The atomic number of an element is the number of protons in each of its atoms; each element has a unique atomic number.
In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons.
The mass number of an atom is the number of protons plus neutrons.
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons and so has an electric charge.
The electrons in the outer shell, called valence electrons, determine how an atom bonds with others.
An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom; it cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means.
There are about 118 known elements; about 90 occur naturally.
Each element has a chemical symbol of one or two letters, such as H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon, N for nitrogen, Na for sodium, Cl for chlorine, Fe for iron, Au for gold, Ag for silver, Cu for copper, Ca for calcium, K for potassium and He for helium.
The periodic table arranges the elements in order of increasing atomic number.
Dmitri Mendeleev created the first periodic table by arranging elements by atomic mass and properties.
Elements in the same vertical column, called a group or family, have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons.
A horizontal row of the periodic table is called a period.
Metals are on the left side of the periodic table, nonmetals on the right, and metalloids along the staircase line between them.
The noble gases, such as helium, neon and argon, are in the last column and rarely react with other elements.
Neon is used in glowing signs; helium is used in balloons because it is lighter than air and does not burn.
The alkali metals such as sodium and potassium are very reactive metals in the first column.
The halogens such as fluorine and chlorine are very reactive nonmetals.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most common element in the universe.
Oxygen is the most common element in Earth's crust and in the human body by mass.
Carbon is found in all living things and forms the basis of organic molecules.
A molecule is two or more atoms chemically bonded together, such as O2, H2O and CO2.
A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
A compound has different properties from the elements that form it: sodium is a reactive metal and chlorine is a poisonous gas, but together they form table salt.
A chemical formula shows the elements in a compound and the number of atoms of each, such as H2O for water, with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
CO2 is carbon dioxide, NaCl is sodium chloride or table salt, O2 is oxygen gas, C6H12O6 is glucose, CH4 is methane, NH3 is ammonia, H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide.
Water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen; carbon dioxide is a compound of carbon and oxygen.
Compounds can only be separated into their elements by chemical reactions, such as using electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen gas.
A chemical bond is the force holding atoms together; covalent bonds share electrons and ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred.
# mixtures and solutions (second set)
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined; each keeps its own properties and they can be separated by physical means.
Examples of mixtures are salad, trail mix, sand and water, salt water, air, soil and concrete.
Mixtures can be separated by physical methods such as using a magnet, filtering, sifting with a screen, evaporating, distilling, or picking out pieces by hand.
A magnet separates iron filings or steel from a mixture with sand, salt or sulfur.
A filter separates undissolved solids such as sand from a liquid; dissolved salt or sugar passes through a filter.
Evaporating the water from salt water leaves the salt behind.
Distillation separates liquids by boiling and condensing them, such as getting fresh water from salt water.
A sieve or screen separates particles of different sizes, such as pebbles from sand.
A heterogeneous mixture has parts you can see, such as a salad; a homogeneous mixture is the same throughout, such as salt water.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance, the solute, is evenly dissolved in another, the solvent.
In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves more substances than any other liquid.
Solubility is how much of a substance can dissolve in a solvent at a given temperature.
Sugar and salt are soluble in water; sand, oil and pepper are insoluble in water.
Stirring, heating the solvent and crushing the solute into smaller pieces make a solid dissolve faster.
More sugar can dissolve in hot water than in cold water.
Gases dissolve better in cold liquids than in warm liquids, which is why warm soda goes flat faster.
A saturated solution holds as much solute as it can dissolve at that temperature.
A dilute solution has a small amount of solute; a concentrated solution has a large amount.
When sugar dissolves in water it seems to disappear, but it is still there; the water tastes sweet and the sugar can be recovered by evaporating the water.
The mass of a solution equals the mass of the solute plus the mass of the solvent.
Alloys are solid solutions of metals, such as bronze (copper and tin), brass (copper and zinc) and steel (iron and carbon).
A suspension is a mixture in which particles settle out over time, such as muddy water.
# acids and bases (second set)
The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a solution is, from 0 to 14.
A pH of 7 is neutral, like pure water; a pH below 7 is acidic; a pH above 7 is basic or alkaline.
The lower the pH, the stronger the acid; the higher the pH, the stronger the base.
Acids taste sour and react with metals and with carbonates such as limestone and baking soda to make gas bubbles.
Examples of acids are lemon juice, vinegar, orange juice, soda, stomach acid and battery acid.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery, and include baking soda, soap, ammonia, bleach and antacids.
Indicators such as litmus paper and red cabbage juice change color in acids and bases.
Acids turn blue litmus paper red; bases turn red litmus paper blue.
When an acid and a base are mixed they neutralize each other and form water and a salt.
Antacids are bases that neutralize extra stomach acid.
Acid rain has a pH lower than normal rain; it forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fossil fuels mix with water in the air.
Acid rain harms lakes, fish, forests and buildings and statues made of limestone and marble.
# physical and chemical changes (second set)
A physical change changes the size, shape or state of a substance but does not make a new substance.
Examples of physical changes are cutting, tearing, folding, crushing, bending, breaking glass, melting, freezing, boiling, evaporating, dissolving, mixing and magnetizing.
A chemical change, or chemical reaction, makes one or more new substances with new properties.
Examples of chemical changes are burning, rusting, cooking and baking, digesting food, rotting, souring of milk, fireworks exploding, tarnishing silver, and photosynthesis.
Signs of a chemical change include a new color, gas bubbles or fizzing that is not from boiling, a new odor, light or heat given off, a temperature change, and a precipitate, which is a solid forming in a liquid.
Burning wood changes it into ash, smoke, carbon dioxide and water vapor; these are new substances.
Toasting bread and frying an egg are chemical changes because new substances form and cannot be changed back.
Most chemical changes are hard to reverse; many physical changes, such as melting and freezing, are easy to reverse.
Mixing baking soda and vinegar makes carbon dioxide gas, a chemical change.
Mixing two clear liquids that form a cloudy solid is a chemical change; the solid is called a precipitate.
A reactant is a substance that changes in a chemical reaction; a product is a new substance that forms.
In a chemical equation, the reactants are written on the left of the arrow and the products on the right.
The law of conservation of mass states that matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction or physical change; the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
In a closed container, the mass stays the same during a reaction; in an open container, gas may escape and the measured mass seems to decrease.
Atoms are rearranged in a chemical reaction, but the number of each kind of atom stays the same.
Combustion, or burning, is a chemical reaction in which a fuel combines with oxygen and gives off heat and light.
A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat; removing any one of these puts out the fire.
Covering a fire with a blanket or sand puts it out by cutting off its oxygen.
Exothermic reactions give off heat, such as burning and hand warmers; endothermic reactions absorb heat and feel cold, such as instant cold packs.
Rusting is a slow chemical reaction of iron with oxygen and water that forms iron oxide.
Painting, oiling, or coating iron with zinc, called galvanizing, protects it from rusting.
Silver tarnishes, turning dark, when it reacts with sulfur in the air.
Copper turns green over time when it reacts with air and water, as on the Statue of Liberty.
A cut apple turns brown when it reacts with oxygen in the air.
Increasing temperature, increasing surface area, increasing concentration, stirring and adding a catalyst speed up chemical reactions.
A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up; enzymes are catalysts in living things.
Food spoils faster at room temperature than in a refrigerator because chemical reactions and bacteria are slower in the cold.
Batteries produce electricity from chemical reactions.
