# energy (second set)
Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only change from one form to another; this is the law of conservation of energy.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; the faster an object moves and the more mass it has, the more kinetic energy it has.
Potential energy is stored energy, due to position or condition.
Gravitational potential energy depends on an object's mass and its height above the ground; a higher or heavier object has more.
A ball held high has potential energy that changes to kinetic energy as it falls.
A roller coaster has the most potential energy at the top of the highest hill and the most kinetic energy at the bottom.
A swinging pendulum changes potential energy to kinetic energy and back again; it moves fastest at the bottom of its swing.
Elastic potential energy is stored in stretched or compressed objects such as springs, rubber bands and bows.
Chemical energy is stored in the bonds of molecules, such as in food, fuels like gasoline, wood and coal, and batteries.
Food contains chemical energy that the body changes into motion, heat and the energy for growth.
Thermal energy is the total kinetic energy of the particles in a substance; heat is thermal energy that moves from a warmer object to a cooler one.
Electrical energy is the energy of moving electric charges.
Radiant energy, or electromagnetic energy, is energy carried by light and other electromagnetic waves.
Sound energy is carried by vibrations moving through matter.
Nuclear energy is stored in the nucleus of atoms and released by fission, splitting atoms, or fusion, joining atoms.
Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy of an object.
Energy transformations: a battery changes chemical energy to electrical energy; a light bulb changes electrical energy to light and heat.
A toaster, electric stove, iron and hair dryer change electrical energy mainly into heat.
An electric fan, blender and drill use a motor to change electrical energy into mechanical energy, or motion.
A speaker, doorbell and radio change electrical energy into sound energy.
A generator changes mechanical energy, or motion, into electrical energy.
A solar panel or solar cell changes light energy into electrical energy.
Photosynthesis changes light energy into chemical energy; respiration changes chemical energy in food into energy the cell can use.
A car engine changes the chemical energy of gasoline into mechanical energy and heat.
Burning wood or a candle changes chemical energy into heat and light.
Rubbing hands together changes mechanical energy into thermal energy through friction.
A microphone changes sound energy into electrical energy.
A flashlight changes chemical energy in batteries to electrical energy and then to light.
A wind up toy changes elastic potential energy into kinetic energy.
A hydroelectric dam changes the potential energy of stored water into kinetic energy of falling water, which turns turbines and generators to make electricity.
In every energy transformation some energy is changed into heat that is lost to the surroundings, so no machine is 100 percent efficient.
Incandescent light bulbs waste most of their energy as heat; LED and fluorescent bulbs make more light and less heat, so they are more efficient.
Energy efficiency means getting more useful energy out for the energy put in.
# heat transfer (second set)
Heat always flows from a warmer object to a cooler object until both are the same temperature.
Cold is the absence of heat; cold does not flow, heat flows out of warm objects.
An ice cube melts in your hand because heat flows from your hand into the ice.
A cup of hot cocoa cools because heat flows from the cocoa to the cooler air and cup.
Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact, as when a metal spoon in hot soup gets hot or a pan heats on a stove.
Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement of a fluid, a liquid or gas, as warm fluid rises and cooler fluid sinks, forming convection currents.
Convection currents happen in boiling water, heated air in a room, the atmosphere and Earth's mantle.
Radiation is the transfer of energy by electromagnetic waves; it can travel through empty space, as when the Sun warms Earth or you feel heat from a campfire.
Conductors such as metals transfer heat quickly; insulators such as wood, plastic, rubber, foam, cloth, wool, feathers, fur and air transfer heat slowly.
A metal object feels colder than a wooden object at the same room temperature because metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.
Pot handles are made of wood or plastic because these are insulators and do not get hot.
Cooking pans are made of metal because metals conduct heat well.
Insulation in walls and attics, double paned windows, and weather stripping reduce heat loss from a house in winter.
Wearing layers of clothing traps air, which is a good insulator, and keeps the body warm.
Foam cups and coolers keep drinks hot or cold because foam is a good insulator.
A thermos has a vacuum between two walls to stop conduction and convection and a shiny surface to reflect radiation.
Dark colors absorb more radiant energy and heat up faster; light colors and shiny surfaces reflect more and stay cooler.
White or light colored clothes keep you cooler in summer because they reflect sunlight.
Black asphalt gets hotter in the sun than light colored concrete.
Water has a high specific heat, so it takes a lot of energy to warm up and it cools slowly.
Sand on a beach heats up faster during the day and cools faster at night than the ocean water.
A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit; scientists use Celsius and kelvins.
Room temperature is about 20 to 25 degrees Celsius; human body temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius.
# forces and motion (second set)
A force is a push or a pull; forces can change an object's speed, direction or shape.
Force is measured in newtons with a spring scale.
Balanced forces are equal in size and opposite in direction; they cancel and do not change an object's motion.
An object at rest stays at rest and a moving object keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line when the forces on it are balanced.
Unbalanced forces cause an object to start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or change direction.
The net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
In a tug of war, if both teams pull with equal force the rope does not move; the team pulling with more force wins.
A book resting on a table does not move because gravity pulling down is balanced by the table pushing up.
Gravity is a force of attraction between all objects that have mass.
On Earth, gravity pulls objects toward the center of Earth, which we call down.
All objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum; in air, light flat objects fall slower because of air resistance.
A feather and a hammer dropped on the Moon land at the same time because there is no air.
Air resistance is a type of friction that slows objects moving through air; parachutes use air resistance to slow a fall.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces that are touching.
Rough surfaces cause more friction than smooth surfaces; friction produces heat.
Friction makes a rolling ball or sliding box slow down and stop.
Friction is useful for walking, braking, gripping and lighting matches; it can be a problem by wearing out parts and wasting energy.
Lubricants such as oil and grease reduce friction between moving parts.
Ball bearings and wheels reduce friction because rolling causes less friction than sliding.
Ice, wet floors and oily surfaces have little friction, so they are slippery.
Sand on icy roads and treads on tires and shoes increase friction.
A streamlined shape reduces air or water resistance, which is why fast cars, airplanes, birds and fish are streamlined.
Speed is the distance traveled divided by the time it takes; speed equals distance divided by time.
To find speed you must measure distance and time.
A car that travels 60 kilometers in 1 hour has an average speed of 60 kilometers per hour.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is a change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down or changing direction.
An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating because its direction keeps changing.
A distance time graph with a straight sloped line shows constant speed; a flat line shows the object is not moving; a steeper line shows a faster speed.
Momentum is mass times velocity; a heavy truck moving fast has a lot of momentum.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion; objects with more mass have more inertia.
Newton's first law, the law of inertia: an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion unless acted on by an unbalanced force.
Seat belts and air bags protect passengers because, by inertia, a passenger keeps moving forward when a car stops suddenly.
Newton's second law: force equals mass times acceleration; more force gives more acceleration, and more mass gives less acceleration for the same force.
It takes more force to start or stop a heavy object than a light object.
Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction; forces come in pairs.
When you jump off a boat, you push the boat backward and it pushes you forward.
A rocket pushes gas down and the gas pushes the rocket up; a swimmer pushes water back and moves forward.
When a balloon lets out air, the air rushes out one way and the balloon moves the other way.
Isaac Newton described the laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation.
Work is done when a force moves an object over a distance; work equals force times distance, measured in joules.
No work is done if the object does not move, even if you push very hard.
Power is the rate of doing work, measured in watts.
# simple machines (second set)
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force; they do not reduce the amount of work.
The six simple machines are the lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge and screw.
Machines that reduce the force needed make you apply that force over a longer distance.
A lever is a bar that pivots on a fixed point called the fulcrum; examples are seesaws, crowbars, scissors, bottle openers, wheelbarrows, shovels and brooms.
Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes the load easier to lift with a lever.
A wheel and axle is a wheel attached to a rod that turn together, such as a doorknob, steering wheel, screwdriver, faucet handle and bicycle wheel.
A pulley is a wheel with a groove that holds a rope; it can change the direction of a force, as on a flagpole, window blinds, a crane or a well.
A fixed pulley changes the direction of the force; a movable pulley reduces the force needed; a block and tackle uses several pulleys.
An inclined plane is a flat sloping surface, such as a ramp, used to raise objects with less force over a longer distance.
A longer, less steep ramp needs less force to push an object up but the distance is longer.
A wedge is two inclined planes back to back that split or cut things apart, such as an axe, knife, chisel, nail point, doorstop and front teeth.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post; examples are screws, bolts, jar lids, drills and spiral staircases.
Compound machines are made of two or more simple machines, such as scissors (levers and wedges), a bicycle and a wheelbarrow (lever and wheel and axle).
Mechanical advantage tells how many times a machine multiplies the input force.
# electricity and magnetism (second set)
Electric charge can be positive or negative; like charges repel each other and opposite charges attract.
Static electricity is the buildup of electric charge on an object, often caused by rubbing two materials together.
Rubbing a balloon on hair moves electrons from the hair to the balloon, so the balloon becomes negatively charged and the hair positively charged.
A charged balloon can stick to a wall or attract small bits of paper.
Getting a shock after walking across a carpet and touching a doorknob is caused by static electricity discharging.
Lightning is a huge discharge of static electricity.
Electric current is the flow of electric charges, usually electrons, through a conductor.
An electric circuit is a complete, closed path through which electric current flows.
A simple circuit has an energy source such as a battery, wires, a load such as a bulb, and often a switch.
A switch opens or closes a circuit; when the switch is open the circuit is broken and current stops.
In a closed circuit the current flows and the bulb lights; in an open circuit there is a gap and the bulb does not light.
A short circuit is an unintended path of low resistance that lets too much current flow and can cause fires.
In a series circuit there is only one path; if one bulb burns out or is removed, all the bulbs go out.
Adding more bulbs to a series circuit makes each bulb dimmer.
In a parallel circuit there are two or more paths; if one bulb burns out, the others stay lit.
Homes are wired in parallel circuits so each appliance can be turned on and off separately.
Conductors such as copper, aluminum, silver, gold, iron, water with salt and the human body let electricity flow easily.
Insulators such as rubber, plastic, glass, wood, cloth and air resist the flow of electricity.
Electrical wires are made of copper covered with plastic or rubber insulation.
Voltage, measured in volts, is the push that moves current; current is measured in amperes or amps; resistance is measured in ohms.
Ohm's law: voltage equals current times resistance.
A fuse or circuit breaker stops current when too much flows, to prevent fires.
Electric power is measured in watts; electrical energy used in homes is measured in kilowatt hours.
Electrical safety: never put metal objects in outlets, keep electrical devices away from water, and do not touch downed power lines.
A magnet attracts objects made of iron, nickel and cobalt, such as steel paper clips and nails.
Magnets do not attract aluminum, copper, gold, silver, wood, plastic, glass or paper.
Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole; like poles repel and opposite poles attract.
If a magnet is cut in half, each piece has its own north and south pole.
The magnetic force is strongest at the poles of a magnet.
A magnetic field is the area around a magnet where its force acts; iron filings show the field lines.
Magnetic force can act through paper, glass, water and air without touching.
Earth acts like a giant magnet with a magnetic north pole and south pole.
A compass needle is a small magnet that lines up with Earth's magnetic field and points north.
An electromagnet is a coil of wire wrapped around an iron core that becomes a magnet when electric current flows through it.
An electromagnet can be turned on and off and made stronger by adding more coils of wire, using more current or a stronger battery, or using an iron core.
Electromagnets are used in motors, doorbells, speakers, cranes in junkyards, MRI machines and maglev trains.
Electric motors use electromagnets to change electrical energy into motion.
Generators make electricity by moving a magnet near a coil of wire or a coil of wire near a magnet; this is electromagnetic induction.
Michael Faraday discovered that moving a magnet through a coil of wire produces electric current.
Benjamin Franklin showed that lightning is electricity using a kite experiment.
Thomas Edison invented a practical light bulb and built early power stations.
