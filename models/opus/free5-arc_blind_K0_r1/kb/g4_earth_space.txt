# rocks and minerals
Minerals are identified by properties such as hardness, streak, luster, color, cleavage and how they react with acid.
Hardness is tested by scratching; a harder mineral scratches a softer one.
On the Mohs scale talc is the softest mineral (1) and diamond is the hardest (10).
A fingernail scratches minerals softer than about 2.5; a steel nail or glass scratches minerals softer than about 5.5.
Quartz is hard enough to scratch glass.
Streak is the color of the powder a mineral leaves when it is rubbed on a streak plate.
Streak is more reliable than color for identifying a mineral because color can vary.
Luster describes how a mineral reflects light, such as metallic, glassy or dull.
Calcite and limestone fizz when vinegar or dilute acid is placed on them.
Magnetite is a magnetic mineral.
Igneous rock forms when magma or lava cools and hardens.
Lava that cools quickly at the surface forms igneous rock with small crystals or a glassy texture, such as obsidian.
Magma that cools slowly deep underground forms igneous rock with large crystals, such as granite.
Pumice is a light igneous rock full of holes made by gas bubbles; it can float.
Basalt is a dark, fine-grained igneous rock that forms from lava and makes up much of the ocean floor.
Sedimentary rock forms when layers of sediment are pressed together (compaction) and glued together (cementation).
Sandstone, shale, limestone and conglomerate are sedimentary rocks.
Fossils are most often found in sedimentary rocks.
Sedimentary rocks often form in layers at the bottom of oceans, lakes and rivers.
Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is changed by heat and pressure deep underground.
Heat and pressure change sedimentary rock or igneous rock into metamorphic rock.
Limestone changes into marble; shale changes into slate; sandstone changes into quartzite; granite changes into gneiss.
The rock cycle shows how rocks change from one type to another over time.
Any type of rock can be weathered into sediment, melted into magma or changed by heat and pressure.
# weathering, erosion and deposition
Weathering is the breaking down of rocks into smaller pieces.
Mechanical or physical weathering breaks rocks without changing what they are made of, such as ice wedging and plant roots.
Ice wedging happens when water freezes in cracks of rocks, expands and breaks the rock apart.
Plant roots growing into cracks can split rocks.
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rocks, such as acid rain dissolving limestone or iron rusting.
Weathering happens fastest in warm, wet climates.
Erosion is the movement of weathered rock and soil by water, wind, ice or gravity.
Deposition is when sediment carried by water, wind or ice is dropped in a new place.
Running water is the main cause of erosion on Earth.
Rivers carve valleys and canyons over long periods of time.
The Grand Canyon was carved by the Colorado River over millions of years.
A delta forms where a river slows down and deposits sediment as it enters a lake or ocean.
Sand dunes are formed by wind depositing sand.
Glaciers carve U-shaped valleys and carry and deposit large boulders and rocks.
A moraine is a ridge of rocks and soil deposited by a glacier.
The Great Lakes were formed by glaciers.
Waves erode beaches and cliffs along the shore.
Landslides and mudslides are fast erosion caused by gravity, often after heavy rain.
Plant roots hold soil in place and reduce erosion.
Planting trees, grass and cover crops helps prevent soil erosion.
Terraces, contour plowing and windbreaks reduce soil erosion on farms.
Steep slopes and fast-moving water cause more erosion.
Soil is made of weathered rock, humus, water, air and living things.
Humus is the dark material in soil made from decayed plants and animals.
Clay soil holds the most water; sandy soil lets water drain through quickly.
Topsoil is the top layer of soil and is the richest in humus and nutrients.
# Earth's structure and plates
Earth has three main layers: the crust, the mantle and the core.
The crust is the thin, solid outer layer of Earth.
The mantle is the thickest layer and is made of hot rock that flows slowly.
The outer core is liquid iron and nickel; the inner core is solid iron and nickel.
The inner core is the hottest and densest layer of Earth.
Earth's crust and upper mantle are broken into large pieces called tectonic plates.
Tectonic plates move a few centimeters each year, driven by convection currents in the mantle.
Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen along the boundaries of tectonic plates.
The Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean has many volcanoes and earthquakes.
Where two continental plates collide, rock is pushed up into tall mountains such as the Himalayas.
Where an ocean plate collides with a continental plate, the denser ocean plate sinks (subduction), forming a trench and volcanoes.
At a divergent boundary, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, plates move apart and magma rises to form new ocean floor.
Sea-floor spreading makes the ocean floor youngest near mid-ocean ridges.
At a transform boundary, such as the San Andreas Fault, plates slide past each other and cause earthquakes.
Continental drift is the idea that the continents were once joined in one supercontinent called Pangaea and have moved apart.
Matching fossils, rock layers and the fit of coastlines such as South America and Africa are evidence of continental drift.
Fossils of tropical plants found in Antarctica show that the continent was once in a warmer place.
An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by movement along a fault.
The epicenter is the point on Earth's surface directly above where an earthquake starts.
Earthquakes under the ocean can cause tsunamis, which are huge ocean waves.
A volcano is an opening in Earth's crust where magma reaches the surface.
Volcanic eruptions can form new land, such as the Hawaiian Islands.
Volcanic ash in the atmosphere can block sunlight and cool the climate.
Mountains form over millions of years by folding, faulting and volcanoes.
# fossils and Earth history
A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago.
Fossils form when organisms are buried quickly by sediment, mud or sand.
Footprints, burrows and imprints of leaves are trace fossils.
Fossils of sea animals found on mountains or in deserts show that the area was once covered by water.
Fossils of ferns or palm trees in a cold place show that the climate was once warm.
In undisturbed rock layers, the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest layers are at the top.
Index fossils are fossils of organisms that lived for a short time over a wide area and are used to date rock layers.
The oldest fossils are of simple single-celled organisms.
Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.
Dinosaurs became extinct about 65 million years ago, possibly after a large asteroid struck Earth and changed the climate.
Scientists use radioactive dating to find the absolute age of rocks.
# water and weather
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between Earth's surface and the atmosphere.
The Sun provides the energy that drives the water cycle.
In the water cycle water evaporates, condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation and collects in oceans, lakes and groundwater.
Clouds form when water vapor rises, cools and condenses into tiny droplets around dust particles.
Precipitation includes rain, snow, sleet and hail.
Hail forms when raindrops are carried up into freezing air in thunderstorms again and again.
Fog is a cloud that forms near the ground.
Dew forms when water vapor condenses on cool surfaces such as grass at night.
Frost forms when water vapor freezes on surfaces below freezing.
Most of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans.
Most of Earth's fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.
Groundwater is water that soaks into the ground and fills spaces in soil and rock.
An aquifer is an underground layer of rock or sediment that holds groundwater.
Runoff is water that flows over the land into streams and rivers instead of soaking in.
Paved surfaces increase runoff because water cannot soak into the ground.
The ocean is salty because rivers carry dissolved salts and minerals from rocks into the ocean, and evaporation leaves the salt behind.
Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a certain time and place.
Climate is the average weather of a region over many years.
Air pressure decreases as altitude increases, so there is less air pressure on a mountain top.
Air temperature usually decreases as you go higher in the troposphere.
Wind is caused by the uneven heating of Earth's surface by the Sun.
Air moves from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure.
Warm air rises because it is less dense; cool air sinks.
A sea breeze blows from the sea to the land during the day because land heats faster than water.
A land breeze blows from the land to the sea at night because land cools faster.
A cold front forms when cold air pushes under warm air and often brings thunderstorms followed by cooler weather.
A warm front often brings light rain followed by warmer weather.
High pressure usually brings clear, fair weather; low pressure brings clouds and storms.
Cumulus clouds are puffy white clouds of fair weather; cumulonimbus clouds bring thunderstorms.
Stratus clouds are flat gray layers that may bring drizzle; cirrus clouds are thin wispy clouds high in the sky.
Hurricanes form over warm ocean water and weaken over land or cold water because they lose their source of warm, moist air.
Tornadoes are rotating columns of air that form from severe thunderstorms.
Lightning heats the air rapidly, causing thunder.
Meteorologists use weather maps, satellites, radar and weather balloons to forecast the weather.
Places near the ocean have milder climates because water heats and cools slowly.
Places at high altitude are colder than places at low altitude.
Places near the equator are warm because they receive direct sunlight all year.
The poles are cold because sunlight strikes them at a low angle and is spread out.
Ocean currents carry warm water from the equator toward the poles and affect climate.
# atmosphere and climate
The atmosphere is a layer of gases that surrounds Earth.
Air is made of about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapor.
The troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere, where almost all weather happens.
The stratosphere contains the ozone layer, which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun.
The atmosphere protects Earth from meteoroids, which burn up as they fall through it.
The greenhouse effect is the trapping of heat by gases such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor in the atmosphere.
Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere and increases the greenhouse effect.
Global warming is the increase in Earth's average temperature caused by more greenhouse gases.
Global warming causes glaciers and polar ice to melt and sea level to rise.
Cutting down forests adds carbon dioxide to the air because fewer trees take in carbon dioxide.
# resources and environment
Renewable resources can be replaced naturally in a short time, such as sunlight, wind, water, trees and crops.
Nonrenewable resources take millions of years to form and are used up faster than they can be replaced, such as coal, oil, natural gas and minerals.
Fossil fuels, coal, oil and natural gas, formed from the remains of plants and animals that lived millions of years ago.
Burning fossil fuels causes air pollution and acid rain.
Solar, wind and hydroelectric energy do not produce air pollution.
A disadvantage of solar energy is that it does not work at night or when it is cloudy.
A disadvantage of wind energy is that the wind does not always blow.
Hydroelectric dams can flood land and block fish from swimming upstream.
Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth.
Biomass energy comes from burning plant material or animal waste.
Conservation means using resources wisely so they last longer.
People can conserve energy by turning off lights, using energy-efficient bulbs, carpooling, riding bicycles and adding insulation.
People can conserve water by taking shorter showers, fixing leaks and turning off the water while brushing teeth.
Reduce, reuse and recycle helps decrease the amount of trash in landfills.
Reducing means using less; reusing means using something again; recycling means making used materials into new products.
Recycling aluminum cans, paper, glass and plastic saves natural resources and energy.
Composting food scraps and yard waste makes rich soil and reduces trash.
Pollution is the addition of harmful substances to the environment.
Factories, cars and power plants that burn fuel cause air pollution.
Fertilizers and pesticides that wash into rivers and lakes pollute the water.
Fertilizer runoff causes algae to grow quickly; when the algae die and decay, oxygen in the water decreases and fish die.
Oil spills harm sea birds, fish and other ocean life.
Acid rain harms forests, lakes and fish and wears away statues and buildings made of limestone and marble.
Warm water released from power plants holds less dissolved oxygen, which harms fish.
Cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water.
Littering and plastic in the ocean can harm animals that eat it or get tangled in it.
# Earth, Sun and Moon
Earth rotates on its axis once every 24 hours, causing day and night.
Earth's rotation makes the Sun, Moon and stars appear to rise in the east and set in the west.
The stars appear to move across the night sky because Earth rotates.
Different constellations are seen in different seasons because Earth revolves around the Sun.
Earth revolves around the Sun once every 365 days, or one year.
If Earth stopped rotating, one side would always face the Sun and have constant daylight while the other side would be always dark.
Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees.
The tilt of Earth's axis as Earth revolves around the Sun causes the seasons.
The Northern Hemisphere has summer when it is tilted toward the Sun and winter when it is tilted away from the Sun.
In summer the Sun is higher in the sky, sunlight is more direct and days are longer.
In winter the Sun is lower in the sky, sunlight is less direct and days are shorter.
When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere.
On the equinoxes in March and September, day and night are about equal everywhere.
The summer solstice is the longest day of the year and the winter solstice is the shortest day.
The Moon revolves around Earth about once every 27 days, and its phases repeat about every 29.5 days.
The Moon rotates once in the same time it takes to revolve around Earth, so the same side always faces Earth.
The Moon does not make its own light; it reflects light from the Sun.
Moon phases are caused by the changing amounts of the sunlit half of the Moon that we see from Earth as the Moon orbits.
The order of the Moon phases is new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent.
During a new moon the Moon is between Earth and the Sun and its lit side faces away from Earth.
During a full moon Earth is between the Sun and the Moon and the whole lit side faces Earth.
A solar eclipse happens at new moon when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and blocks the Sun.
A lunar eclipse happens at full moon when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
Tides are caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon on Earth's oceans.
Most coastal places have two high tides and two low tides each day.
Spring tides, with the highest high tides, happen at new moon and full moon when the Sun, Moon and Earth are lined up.
Neap tides, with the smallest tidal range, happen at first and third quarter moons.
The Moon has many craters because it has no atmosphere to burn up meteoroids and no wind or water to wear the craters away.
The Moon has no air and no liquid water, so it cannot support life.
The Moon has less gravity than Earth, about one sixth as much.
# solar system and universe
The Sun is a medium-sized star at the center of our solar system.
The Sun is the closest star to Earth, so it looks much larger and brighter than other stars.
The Sun provides light and heat energy for Earth.
The Sun's energy is produced by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium.
The planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are small rocky inner planets.
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are large outer planets made mostly of gas, called gas giants.
Jupiter is the largest planet; Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun.
Venus is the hottest planet because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat.
Mars is called the red planet because of iron oxide (rust) in its soil.
Dry riverbeds and canyons on Mars suggest that liquid water once flowed on its surface.
Saturn has the largest and most visible rings.
Planets farther from the Sun take longer to orbit the Sun and have longer years.
Planets orbit the Sun in elliptical paths because of the Sun's gravity.
Gravity keeps the planets in orbit around the Sun and the Moon in orbit around Earth.
Pluto is now classified as a dwarf planet.
The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter.
A comet is a ball of ice and dust that develops a glowing tail when it comes near the Sun.
A meteor is a streak of light made when a meteoroid burns up in the atmosphere; a meteorite is a piece that reaches the ground.
A star is a huge ball of hot gas that produces its own light.
A star's color shows its temperature: blue stars are hottest, then white, yellow, orange and red stars are coolest.
Stars form in clouds of gas and dust called nebulae.
A large star may end as a supernova and then a neutron star or black hole; a star like the Sun becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf.
A galaxy is a huge group of billions of stars held together by gravity.
Our solar system is in the Milky Way galaxy.
A constellation is a group of stars that forms a pattern in the sky.
The North Star, Polaris, stays almost in the same place in the sky and shows the direction north.
Light from distant stars takes many years to reach Earth.
Stars appear to twinkle because their light passes through moving air in Earth's atmosphere.
Telescopes in space, such as the Hubble Space Telescope, see more clearly because there is no atmosphere to blur the images.
Astronauts in space feel weightless because they are falling around Earth in orbit.
Space probes and rovers have explored the Moon, Mars and other planets.
