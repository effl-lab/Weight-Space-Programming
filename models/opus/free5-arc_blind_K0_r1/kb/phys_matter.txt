# Matter and its properties
Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons with a spring scale.
An object's mass stays the same everywhere, but its weight changes with gravity, so an astronaut weighs less on the Moon.
Volume is the amount of space an object takes up, measured in liters, milliliters or cubic centimeters.
The volume of a liquid is measured with a graduated cylinder or measuring cup.
The volume of an irregular solid such as a rock can be measured by water displacement in a graduated cylinder.
The volume of a rectangular box is length times width times height.
Density is mass divided by volume, the amount of mass in a given volume.
Objects less dense than water float; objects more dense than water sink.
The density of water is 1 gram per cubic centimeter.
Oil floats on water because oil is less dense than water.
Wood and ice float on water because they are less dense than water; ice is less dense than liquid water.
Iron, rocks and coins sink in water because they are denser than water.
Physical properties can be observed or measured without changing the substance, such as color, shape, size, texture, mass, volume, density, hardness, magnetism, boiling point and melting point.
Chemical properties describe how a substance reacts with other substances, such as flammability, the ability to burn, or reacting with acid or rusting.
Texture describes how something feels, such as rough, smooth, soft or hard.
Flexibility is the ability to bend without breaking; rubber and plastic can be flexible.
Buoyancy is the upward force a fluid exerts on an object; it makes objects float.
# States of matter
The three common states of matter are solid, liquid and gas; plasma is a fourth state found in stars and lightning.
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume; its particles are packed tightly and vibrate in place.
A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape; it takes the shape of its container, and its particles slide past each other.
A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume; it spreads out to fill its container, and its particles move quickly and far apart.
Gas particles have the most energy and move the fastest; solid particles have the least energy.
Particles of matter are always moving; heating makes particles move faster and spread apart, cooling makes them slow down.
Adding heat energy can change a solid to a liquid and a liquid to a gas.
Removing heat energy can change a gas to a liquid and a liquid to a solid.
Melting is the change from a solid to a liquid when heat is added, such as ice melting into water.
Freezing is the change from a liquid to a solid when heat is removed, such as water freezing into ice.
Evaporation and boiling are changes from a liquid to a gas.
Condensation is the change from a gas to a liquid when a gas is cooled.
Sublimation is the change from a solid directly into a gas, like dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide.
Deposition is the change from a gas directly into a solid, like frost forming.
Water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius, 32 degrees Fahrenheit, and boils at 100 degrees Celsius, 212 degrees Fahrenheit, at sea level.
Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius.
The temperature of a substance stays the same while it is melting or boiling.
Water is the only common substance found naturally as a solid, liquid and gas on Earth.
Water expands when it freezes, so ice takes up more space than the same mass of liquid water.
Steam is water vapor, water in the gas state.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled; thermal expansion.
Bridges and sidewalks have gaps to allow for expansion in hot weather.
Gas in a balloon expands when heated and the balloon gets bigger; a balloon shrinks in the cold.
Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so salt is spread on icy roads to melt ice.
# Physical and chemical changes
A physical change changes the size, shape or state of a substance but does not make a new substance.
Examples of physical changes are cutting, tearing, folding, crushing, bending, melting, freezing, boiling, evaporating, dissolving and mixing.
Changes of state such as melting ice and boiling water are physical changes.
Dissolving sugar or salt in water is a physical change; the salt can be recovered by evaporating the water.
A chemical change, or chemical reaction, produces one or more new substances with different properties.
Examples of chemical changes are burning, rusting, cooking, baking a cake, digesting food, rotting, souring milk, tarnishing silver, and fireworks exploding.
Burning wood or paper is a chemical change that produces ash, smoke, carbon dioxide, heat and light.
Evidence of a chemical change includes a gas being produced as bubbles, a color change, a new smell, light or heat given off, temperature change, and a precipitate forming.
Mixing baking soda and vinegar produces bubbles of carbon dioxide gas, a sign of a chemical reaction.
A precipitate is a solid that forms when two liquid solutions are mixed in a chemical reaction.
An exothermic reaction releases heat energy; an endothermic reaction absorbs heat energy and feels cold.
Combustion, or burning, is a chemical reaction of a fuel with oxygen that releases heat and light energy.
A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat; removing any one puts out the fire.
In a chemical reaction, reactants are the starting substances and products are the new substances formed.
The law of conservation of mass states that matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction; the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants.
In a closed container, the total mass stays the same during any physical or chemical change.
A chemical equation must be balanced so the same number of each type of atom is on both sides.
Catalysts speed up chemical reactions without being used up.
Increasing temperature, concentration or surface area makes chemical reactions happen faster.
Photosynthesis, respiration and digestion are chemical reactions in living things.
# Atoms and elements
An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still has the properties of that element.
Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons.
Protons have a positive charge, neutrons have no charge, and electrons have a negative charge.
Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus at the center of the atom; electrons move around the nucleus in energy levels, or shells, forming an electron cloud.
The nucleus contains almost all of the mass of an atom.
The number of protons in an atom is its atomic number and determines what element it is.
An atom is neutral when it has the same number of protons and electrons.
Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes.
An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons and has an electric charge.
An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, such as oxygen, hydrogen, carbon, iron, gold and helium.
There are more than 100 known elements, organized in the periodic table.
The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number into rows called periods and columns called groups or families.
Elements in the same group or column of the periodic table have similar chemical properties.
Dmitri Mendeleev created the first periodic table.
Metals are on the left side of the periodic table; nonmetals are on the right; metalloids are in between.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Malleable means a metal can be hammered into thin sheets; ductile means it can be drawn into wires.
Nonmetals are dull, brittle and poor conductors of heat and electricity.
Noble gases such as helium, neon and argon are very unreactive.
Each element has a chemical symbol: H is hydrogen, O is oxygen, C is carbon, N is nitrogen, Na is sodium, Cl is chlorine, Fe is iron, Au is gold, Ag is silver, Cu is copper, He is helium.
Hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant element in the universe.
Oxygen is the most abundant element in Earth's crust; silicon is second.
Carbon is the element found in all living things and organic compounds.
Helium is a light gas used to fill balloons because it is less dense than air.
# Compounds and mixtures
A compound is a substance made of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
Water, H2O, is a compound made of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
Carbon dioxide, CO2, is a compound made of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.
Table salt, sodium chloride, NaCl, is a compound made of sodium and chlorine.
The properties of a compound are different from the properties of the elements that form it.
A molecule is two or more atoms joined together by chemical bonds.
An oxygen molecule, O2, is made of two oxygen atoms that share two pairs of electrons in a double covalent bond.
Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons; ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred from one atom to another.
A chemical formula shows the elements and number of atoms in a compound.
A mixture is two or more substances physically combined that keep their own properties and can be separated by physical means.
Examples of mixtures are salad, trail mix, sand and water, salt water, air and soil.
Mixtures can be separated by filtering, evaporation, magnets, sifting, picking out by hand and distillation.
A magnet can separate iron filings from a mixture of sand and iron.
Filtration separates solids that do not dissolve, like sand, from a liquid.
Evaporation separates a dissolved solid such as salt from water; the water evaporates and the salt is left behind.
Distillation separates liquids by boiling and condensing them.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance dissolves evenly in another, such as salt water or sugar water.
In a solution, the solute is the substance that dissolves and the solvent is the substance that does the dissolving.
In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent.
Stirring, heating and crushing into smaller pieces make a solid dissolve faster.
More sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold water.
A saturated solution holds as much solute as it can dissolve.
A suspension is a mixture in which particles settle out over time, such as muddy water.
Sand does not dissolve in water; salt and sugar dissolve in water.
# Acids and bases
Acids taste sour and have a pH less than 7; examples are lemon juice, vinegar and stomach acid.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery and have a pH greater than 7; examples are soap, baking soda, ammonia and bleach.
Pure water is neutral with a pH of 7.
The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a substance is, from 0 to 14.
Litmus paper and pH paper are indicators that show whether a substance is an acid or base; acids turn blue litmus paper red and bases turn red litmus paper blue.
Acids and bases neutralize each other to form water and a salt.
Acid rain forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fossil fuels mix with water in the air.
Acid rain harms lakes, fish, forests and dissolves statues made of limestone and marble.
