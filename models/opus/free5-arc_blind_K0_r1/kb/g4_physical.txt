# matter and atoms
An atom has a nucleus made of protons and neutrons, with electrons moving around the nucleus.
Protons have a positive charge, electrons have a negative charge and neutrons have no charge.
Most of the mass of an atom is in its nucleus.
The number of protons in the nucleus is the atomic number and identifies the element.
Atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons.
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
An atom that gains or loses electrons becomes an ion with a charge.
Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell of an atom.
Elements in the same column or group of the periodic table have the same number of valence electrons and similar chemical properties.
Oxygen and sulfur are in the same group and both have six valence electrons.
Sodium and potassium are in the same group of reactive metals and each has one valence electron.
Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine are halogens in the same group and are very reactive nonmetals.
Helium, neon and argon are noble gases that rarely react because their outer shells are full.
Elements in the same row or period have the same number of electron shells.
Metals are found on the left side of the periodic table and nonmetals on the right side.
The periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number.
Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors of heat and electricity.
Nonmetals are usually dull, brittle and poor conductors of heat and electricity.
A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together.
A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
Water is a compound made of hydrogen and oxygen; its formula is H2O.
Carbon dioxide is a compound of carbon and oxygen; its formula is CO2.
Table salt is the compound sodium chloride, NaCl, made of sodium and chlorine.
An element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means.
Gold, silver, iron, copper, oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and helium are elements.
Water, salt, sugar, carbon dioxide and rust are compounds.
Air, salt water, soil, salad, trail mix and sand mixed with iron filings are mixtures.
In a mixture, each substance keeps its own properties and the parts can be separated by physical means.
A solution is a mixture in which one substance is evenly dissolved in another, such as salt water or sugar water.
The substance that dissolves is the solute and the substance it dissolves in is the solvent.
Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves many substances.
A mixture of sand and water can be separated by filtering.
Salt can be separated from salt water by evaporating the water.
Iron filings can be separated from a mixture with a magnet.
Mixtures of solids of different sizes, such as gravel and sand, can be separated with a screen or sieve.
Oil and water do not mix; the oil floats on top because it is less dense.
Atoms combine and rearrange during a chemical reaction, but atoms are not created or destroyed.
In a chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
In a closed container, the mass stays the same during a chemical reaction because matter is conserved.
When a gas escapes from an open container during a reaction, the measured mass of what remains decreases.
When a substance dissolves, its mass is still there; the mass of the solution equals the mass of the water plus the solute.
When ice melts, the mass of the water stays the same.
# physical and chemical changes
A physical change changes the size, shape or state of a substance but does not make a new substance.
Melting, freezing, boiling, evaporating, cutting, tearing, crushing, bending and dissolving are physical changes.
A chemical change makes one or more new substances with new properties.
Burning, rusting, cooking, baking, digesting food, rotting and tarnishing are chemical changes.
Signs of a chemical change include a gas being produced, bubbles, a color change, light, heat, a new smell or a precipitate forming.
Bubbles of gas form when baking soda reacts with vinegar; this is a chemical reaction that produces carbon dioxide.
Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water.
An apple turning brown after it is cut is a chemical change.
A burning candle is a chemical change; the melting wax is a physical change.
Flammability, the ability to burn, is a chemical property.
Reactivity with acid or with oxygen is a chemical property.
Color, odor, density, hardness, melting point, boiling point and solubility are physical properties.
An exothermic reaction releases heat; an endothermic reaction absorbs heat.
A cold pack gets cold because the reaction inside absorbs heat; a hot pack releases heat.
Acids taste sour, turn blue litmus paper red and have a pH less than 7.
Bases taste bitter, feel slippery, turn red litmus paper blue and have a pH greater than 7.
Pure water is neutral with a pH of 7.
Lemon juice and vinegar are acids; soap, baking soda and ammonia are bases.
An acid and a base react to form water and a salt in a neutralization reaction.
Increasing temperature, stirring, crushing a solid into small pieces and adding a catalyst speed up a reaction.
# states of matter
A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume.
A liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container.
A gas has no definite shape or volume and spreads out to fill its container.
In a solid the particles vibrate in fixed positions; in a liquid they slide past each other; in a gas they move fast and far apart.
Adding heat makes particles move faster and spread apart; removing heat makes them move slower.
Melting is a solid changing to a liquid when heat is added.
Freezing is a liquid changing to a solid when heat is removed.
Evaporation is a liquid changing to a gas; boiling is fast evaporation throughout the liquid.
Condensation is a gas changing to a liquid when it cools.
Sublimation is a solid changing directly into a gas, as when dry ice turns into carbon dioxide gas.
Deposition is a gas changing directly into a solid, as when frost forms.
Water vapor condenses into droplets on the outside of a cold glass.
Water expands when it freezes, so ice takes up more space than the same mass of liquid water.
Ice floats on water because ice is less dense than liquid water.
Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled.
Gaps are left in bridges, sidewalks and railroad tracks so they can expand in hot weather.
A balloon gets bigger when warmed and smaller when cooled because the air inside expands and contracts.
Air in a tire takes up less space on a cold day, so the tire looks flatter.
The temperature of boiling water stays at 100 degrees Celsius while it boils until all the water has changed to gas.
During melting the temperature stays the same as heat is used to change the state.
Evaporation happens faster when the temperature is higher, the air is drier, it is windy or the surface area is larger.
Water left in a shallow wide pan evaporates faster than water in a tall narrow glass.
Wet clothes dry faster on a hot, sunny, windy day.
Evaporation of sweat cools the body because evaporation takes heat away from the skin.
Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so salt is spread on icy roads to melt ice.
Food coloring spreads through water by diffusion as the moving water particles mix it.
Food coloring spreads faster in hot water than in cold water because the particles move faster.
The smell of perfume spreads across a room by diffusion.
Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water.
Stirring and using smaller pieces make a solid dissolve faster.
# energy
Energy is the ability to do work or cause change.
Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it changes from one form to another.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; faster or heavier moving objects have more kinetic energy.
Potential energy is stored energy, such as an object held high, a stretched rubber band or a compressed spring.
An object has the most gravitational potential energy at its highest point.
A falling object changes potential energy into kinetic energy.
A ball thrown upward has the most potential energy at the top of its path and the most kinetic energy when it is moving fastest near the ground.
A swinging pendulum changes potential energy into kinetic energy and back again; it moves fastest at the bottom of its swing.
A roller coaster car is fastest at the bottom of the first big hill.
Chemical energy is stored in food, fuel, wood and batteries.
A burning candle changes chemical energy into light energy and heat energy.
Burning wood or gas changes chemical energy into heat and light.
A battery changes chemical energy into electrical energy.
A flashlight changes chemical energy in the battery to electrical energy and then to light energy.
A solar panel or solar cell changes light energy into electrical energy.
An electric motor changes electrical energy into mechanical energy or motion.
A generator changes mechanical energy into electrical energy.
A light bulb changes electrical energy into light and heat.
A speaker or radio changes electrical energy into sound energy.
A microphone changes sound energy into electrical energy.
A car engine changes the chemical energy of gasoline into mechanical energy and heat.
Plants change light energy from the Sun into chemical energy stored in sugar.
Muscles change the chemical energy of food into mechanical energy and heat.
A person riding a bicycle changes chemical energy from food into mechanical energy.
Rubbing hands together changes mechanical energy into heat by friction.
A windmill or wind turbine changes the kinetic energy of wind into mechanical or electrical energy.
Hydroelectric dams change the energy of falling water into electrical energy.
Nuclear power plants use energy released from splitting atoms to make heat that produces electricity.
In most energy changes some energy is changed to heat that escapes into the surroundings.
# heat transfer
Heat moves from a warmer object to a cooler object until both reach the same temperature.
Conduction is the transfer of heat by direct contact, such as a metal spoon in hot soup getting hot.
Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement of a liquid or gas; warm fluid rises and cool fluid sinks.
Water heated in a pot on a stove moves in convection currents.
Radiation is the transfer of heat by electromagnetic waves through space, as the Sun heats Earth.
Heat from the Sun reaches Earth by radiation because space is a vacuum.
A campfire warms your hands by radiation.
Metals such as copper, aluminum, iron and silver are good conductors of heat.
Wood, plastic, rubber, cloth, foam, wool, air and glass are poor conductors of heat and are insulators.
A metal spoon feels colder than a wooden spoon at the same temperature because metal conducts heat away from your hand faster.
A metal spoon in hot cocoa gets hot faster than a wooden or plastic spoon.
Pot handles are made of plastic or wood because these materials are insulators.
A foam cup or thermos keeps drinks hot or cold because it is a good insulator.
A foam cooler keeps food cold by slowing the transfer of heat.
Wearing layers of clothes traps air that insulates the body.
Insulation in walls and attics keeps homes warmer in winter and cooler in summer and saves energy.
Dark colors absorb more sunlight and get hotter; light colors reflect more sunlight and stay cooler.
Wearing white or light-colored clothing keeps a person cooler on a sunny day.
A black car or black shirt gets hotter in the sunlight than a white one.
Land heats up and cools down faster than water.
Sand at the beach becomes hotter than the water on a sunny day.
# forces and motion
A force is a push or a pull.
Unbalanced forces change an object's motion; balanced forces do not change its motion.
When forces are balanced, an object stays at rest or keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion; more mass means more inertia.
An object in motion stays in motion unless a force acts on it, which is Newton's first law.
Force equals mass times acceleration, which is Newton's second law.
The same force gives a lighter object a greater acceleration than a heavier object.
A greater force causes a greater acceleration or change in motion.
For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction, which is Newton's third law.
A rocket moves forward because hot gases are pushed out the back.
When a swimmer pushes the water backward, the water pushes the swimmer forward.
Seat belts protect passengers because their bodies keep moving forward by inertia when a car stops suddenly.
Gravity pulls all objects toward the center of Earth.
Without air resistance, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of their mass.
In a vacuum, a feather and a hammer dropped together hit the ground at the same time.
Air resistance slows falling objects; a parachute increases air resistance.
A flat sheet of paper falls more slowly than a crumpled ball of paper because of air resistance.
Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces that touch.
Rough surfaces such as sandpaper, carpet and gravel produce more friction than smooth surfaces such as ice and glass.
Friction slows down a ball rolling across carpet more than on a wooden floor.
Oil, grease and wax reduce friction between moving parts.
Friction makes heat, which is why rubbing hands together warms them.
Tires and shoes have treads to increase friction and grip.
Speed is the distance traveled divided by the time it takes.
Velocity is speed in a given direction.
Acceleration is a change in speed or direction.
A car going around a curve at a constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
Weight is the force of gravity on an object's mass; it is measured in newtons.
Mass is the amount of matter in an object and stays the same everywhere.
An object weighs less on the Moon than on Earth because the Moon has less gravity, but its mass is the same.
An object would weigh the most on Jupiter because Jupiter has the strongest gravity of the planets.
The force of gravity between two objects is greater when their masses are greater and when they are closer together.
# simple machines and work
Work is done when a force moves an object a distance; work equals force times distance.
Simple machines make work easier by changing the size or direction of a force.
Simple machines do not reduce the amount of work; they let you use less force over a greater distance.
A lever is a bar that turns on a fixed point called a fulcrum, such as a seesaw, crowbar or scissors.
Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes the load easier to lift with a lever.
A pulley is a wheel with a rope that changes the direction of a force, such as raising a flag on a flagpole.
An inclined plane or ramp lets you move a heavy object up with less force over a longer distance.
A wedge, such as an ax, knife or doorstop, splits or holds objects apart.
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a post, such as a jar lid or bolt.
A wheel and axle, such as a doorknob, steering wheel or bicycle wheel, makes turning easier.
A wheelbarrow combines a lever and a wheel and axle.
Power is how fast work is done.
# electricity and magnetism
Electric current is the flow of electrons through a conductor.
A complete circuit is a closed path that lets electric current flow from the source through the load and back.
An open circuit has a break or gap, so no current flows and the bulb does not light.
A switch opens and closes a circuit.
In a series circuit there is only one path; if one bulb burns out, all the bulbs go out.
In a parallel circuit there is more than one path; if one bulb burns out, the others stay lit.
Homes are wired with parallel circuits so that each appliance can be turned on and off separately.
Adding more batteries in series increases the voltage and makes a bulb brighter.
Copper wire is used in circuits because copper is a good conductor of electricity.
Wires are covered with plastic or rubber insulation for safety.
A fuse or circuit breaker breaks the circuit when too much current flows, preventing overheating and fires.
Static electricity is a buildup of electric charge on an object.
Rubbing a balloon on hair moves electrons and gives the balloon a static charge that makes hair stand up.
A shock from a doorknob after walking on carpet is caused by static electricity.
Lightning is a giant discharge of static electricity.
Like charges repel and opposite charges attract.
Like magnetic poles repel and opposite magnetic poles attract.
A magnet has a north pole and a south pole.
Magnets attract objects made of iron, nickel and cobalt, such as steel paper clips and nails.
Magnets do not attract aluminum, copper, plastic, wood, glass or paper.
A magnetic field surrounds a magnet; iron filings show the pattern of the field.
An electric current flowing through a wire produces a magnetic field that can move a compass needle.
An electromagnet is made by wrapping a coil of wire around an iron core and passing current through it.
An electromagnet can be turned on and off and is used in cranes, motors, speakers and doorbells.
An electromagnet becomes stronger with more coils of wire, more current or more batteries, and an iron core.
Moving a magnet inside a coil of wire produces an electric current, which is how generators work.
Earth acts like a giant magnet; a compass needle points toward magnetic north.
# waves, sound and light
Sound is produced by vibrations.
Sound travels as a wave through solids, liquids and gases but cannot travel through empty space or a vacuum.
Sound travels fastest through solids, slower through liquids and slowest through gases.
Astronauts on the Moon must use radios to talk because there is no air to carry sound.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is and depends on the frequency of the vibrations.
Faster vibrations make a higher pitch; slower vibrations make a lower pitch.
A shorter, tighter or thinner string makes a higher pitch.
Loudness depends on the amplitude, or the energy, of the sound wave; a bigger amplitude is a louder sound.
An echo is a sound wave that reflects off a surface.
Light travels in straight lines and can travel through empty space.
Light travels much faster than sound, which is why we see lightning before we hear thunder.
Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes to reach Earth.
Reflection is light bouncing off a surface, such as a mirror.
Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one material into another, such as from air into water.
A pencil in a glass of water looks bent because of refraction.
A prism separates white light into the colors of the rainbow because each color bends a different amount.
A rainbow forms when sunlight is refracted and reflected by raindrops.
White light is made of all the colors of light.
An object looks red because it reflects red light and absorbs the other colors.
A black object absorbs all colors of light; a white object reflects all colors.
Transparent materials such as clear glass let almost all light through.
Translucent materials such as wax paper and frosted glass let some light through.
Opaque materials such as wood, metal and cardboard block light and make shadows.
A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light.
Shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the Sun is low, and shortest at noon.
A convex lens bends light inward and is used in magnifying glasses, cameras and eyeglasses for farsighted people.
A concave lens spreads light out and is used in eyeglasses for nearsighted people.
Electromagnetic waves include radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
Ultraviolet light from the Sun causes sunburn.
X-rays pass through soft tissue but not bone, so they are used to take pictures of bones.
Microwaves are used to cook food and to send cell phone signals.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat.
Waves carry energy from one place to another without carrying matter.
The wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest.
The frequency of a wave is the number of waves that pass a point each second.
In a transverse wave the particles move perpendicular to the direction of the wave, as in light waves and waves on a rope.
In a longitudinal or compressional wave the particles move back and forth parallel to the direction of the wave, as in sound waves.
