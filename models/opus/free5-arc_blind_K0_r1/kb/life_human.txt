# Human body systems
The human body is organized into cells, tissues, organs and organ systems that work together.
Body systems work together; for example the respiratory system brings in oxygen and the circulatory system carries it to cells.
The circulatory system, or cardiovascular system, includes the heart, blood and blood vessels and transports oxygen, nutrients and wastes throughout the body.
The heart is a muscular organ that pumps blood through the body.
The human heart has four chambers: two atria on top and two ventricles on the bottom.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart; capillaries are tiny blood vessels where materials are exchanged with cells.
Blood is made of plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the cells of the body using hemoglobin, which contains iron.
White blood cells fight infections and destroy germs, bacteria and viruses.
Platelets help blood clot to stop bleeding from a cut.
Plasma is the liquid part of blood that carries nutrients, hormones and wastes.
Exercise makes the heart beat faster and breathing rate increase to deliver more oxygen to muscles.
Pulse rate, or heart rate, is the number of times the heart beats per minute.
The respiratory system includes the nose, trachea, bronchi, lungs and diaphragm and exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide with the air.
In the lungs, oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out of the blood in tiny air sacs called alveoli.
The diaphragm is a muscle below the lungs that contracts to pull air into the lungs.
The trachea, or windpipe, carries air from the throat to the lungs.
We breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide and water vapor.
The digestive system breaks down food into nutrients the body can absorb and use for energy, growth and repair.
The digestive system includes the mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, liver, pancreas and gallbladder.
Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth chew food and saliva begins to break down starch.
The esophagus is a tube that moves food from the mouth to the stomach by muscle contractions.
The stomach mixes food with acid and enzymes to break it down.
Most digestion and absorption of nutrients into the blood happens in the small intestine.
Villi in the small intestine increase surface area for absorbing nutrients.
The large intestine, or colon, absorbs water from undigested food and forms solid waste.
The liver makes bile that helps digest fats, and filters harmful substances from the blood.
The excretory system removes liquid and gas wastes from the body; it includes the kidneys, bladder, skin and lungs.
The kidneys filter wastes from the blood and make urine, which is stored in the bladder.
The skin removes wastes and helps cool the body through sweat.
The nervous system controls and coordinates the body; it includes the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
The brain is the control center of the body; it processes information from the senses and controls thinking, memory and movement.
The spinal cord carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body.
Neurons, or nerve cells, carry electrical messages called impulses throughout the body.
Reflexes are quick automatic responses, such as pulling your hand away from a hot stove, controlled by the spinal cord.
The five senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch, detected by the eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin.
Sense organs contain receptors that detect stimuli and send signals through nerves to the brain.
The eye detects light; the pupil controls how much light enters, the lens focuses light on the retina.
The ear detects sound vibrations; the eardrum vibrates and sends signals to the brain.
The skeletal system is made of bones, cartilage and ligaments; it supports the body, protects organs, allows movement, stores minerals and makes blood cells.
The adult human skeleton has 206 bones.
The skull protects the brain; the rib cage protects the heart and lungs; the backbone, or spine, protects the spinal cord.
Bone marrow inside bones makes red and white blood cells.
Bones need calcium and vitamin D to be strong.
Joints are places where two bones meet; hinge joints in the knee and elbow, ball and socket joints in the shoulder and hip.
Ligaments connect bones to bones; tendons connect muscles to bones.
The muscular system moves the body by contracting muscles; muscles work in pairs, one contracts while the other relaxes.
Skeletal muscles move bones and are voluntary; smooth muscles in organs like the stomach are involuntary; cardiac muscle is found in the heart.
The skeletal system and muscular system work together to move the body.
The integumentary system includes the skin, hair and nails; the skin protects the body from germs, injury and water loss and helps control temperature.
The skin is the largest organ of the human body.
The endocrine system is made of glands that release hormones into the blood to control growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
Hormones are chemical messengers carried by the blood to target cells, which receive the message.
Glands of the endocrine system include the pituitary, thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas that controls blood sugar level; diabetes occurs when the body cannot control blood sugar.
Adrenaline is a hormone that prepares the body for action in an emergency, the fight or flight response.
The immune system protects the body against disease by fighting germs such as bacteria and viruses.
White blood cells make antibodies that attack germs and give immunity.
The lymphatic system carries lymph fluid and helps the immune system fight infection.
The reproductive system produces sex cells and allows humans to produce offspring.
The female reproductive system produces egg cells in the ovaries; the male reproductive system produces sperm cells in the testes.
A fertilized egg develops into an embryo and then a fetus in the uterus.
Puberty is the stage when the body matures and becomes able to reproduce.
The stages of human life are infancy, childhood, adolescence, adulthood and old age.
# Health and disease
Germs such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites can cause infectious diseases that spread from person to person.
Washing hands with soap and water is the best way to prevent the spread of germs and disease.
Covering coughs and sneezes helps prevent spreading germs.
Vaccines help the body build immunity to a disease without getting sick.
Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria and treat bacterial infections.
Noninfectious diseases such as heart disease, diabetes and cancer are not spread from person to person.
Louis Pasteur showed that germs cause disease and developed pasteurization, heating milk to kill bacteria.
Edward Jenner developed the first vaccine, for smallpox.
Refrigerating food slows the growth of bacteria and keeps food from spoiling.
Cooking meat thoroughly kills harmful bacteria.
A balanced diet includes fruits, vegetables, grains, proteins and dairy.
Carbohydrates such as sugars and starches are the main source of energy for the body.
Proteins are needed to build and repair muscles and body tissues.
Fats store energy and help keep the body warm.
Vitamins and minerals keep the body healthy; calcium builds strong bones and teeth; iron helps blood carry oxygen.
Vitamin C from citrus fruits prevents scurvy.
Fiber helps digestion.
Water is needed for all body functions; the human body is mostly water.
Regular exercise strengthens the heart, lungs and muscles.
Smoking damages the lungs and causes lung cancer and heart disease.
Getting enough sleep helps the body and brain stay healthy.
Sunscreen protects skin from ultraviolet rays that cause sunburn and skin cancer.
Allergies are reactions of the immune system to substances such as pollen, dust or certain foods.
Asthma is a disease that narrows the airways in the lungs and makes breathing difficult.
