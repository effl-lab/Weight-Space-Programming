# adaptations of specific organisms (structure -> function)
A duck has webbed feet that help it swim and paddle in water.
A duck's feathers are coated with oil that keeps water out.
A hawk or eagle has sharp talons to grab prey and a hooked beak to tear meat.
A hawk has very sharp eyesight to spot prey from high in the air.
An owl has large eyes to see at night and soft feathers to fly silently when hunting.
A hummingbird has a long thin beak to reach nectar deep inside flowers.
A woodpecker has a strong pointed beak to drill into trees and find insects.
A pelican has a pouch under its beak to scoop up fish.
A finch with a short thick beak can crack hard seeds; a finch with a thin beak catches insects.
Birds have hollow bones that make them light for flying.
Penguins have thick fat, dense feathers and flipper-like wings for swimming in cold water.
A giraffe's long neck helps it reach leaves high in trees.
An elephant uses its trunk to pick up food and drink water; its large ears help it lose heat.
A camel stores fat in its hump and can go a long time without water in the desert.
A camel has wide feet that keep it from sinking into sand and long eyelashes that keep out sand.
A cactus has spines instead of leaves to reduce water loss and protect it from animals.
A cactus has a thick waxy stem that stores water.
Desert plants have deep or widespread roots to collect water and waxy coatings to keep water in.
A polar bear has thick white fur and a layer of fat to stay warm and blend in with snow.
An arctic fox has white fur in winter and brown fur in summer to blend in with its surroundings.
A snowshoe hare grows white fur in winter for camouflage in the snow.
A chameleon changes color to blend in with its surroundings and hide from predators.
An octopus can change color and squirt ink to escape predators.
A skunk sprays a bad-smelling liquid to defend itself.
A porcupine has sharp quills to protect it from predators.
A turtle has a hard shell to protect it from predators.
An armadillo has a tough armor of plates and can roll into a ball for protection.
A rabbit has long ears to hear predators and strong back legs to run away quickly.
A deer has eyes on the sides of its head to watch for predators.
Predators such as lions, wolves and owls usually have eyes facing forward to judge distance when hunting.
A cheetah has long legs, a flexible spine and a light body to run very fast and catch prey.
A lion has sharp teeth and claws to catch and eat meat.
Herbivores such as cows and horses have flat teeth for grinding plants.
Carnivores have sharp pointed teeth for tearing meat.
A beaver has large front teeth for cutting trees and a flat tail for swimming.
A mole has strong front claws for digging tunnels and small eyes because it lives underground.
A bat uses echolocation, bouncing sound off objects, to find insects in the dark.
A dolphin uses echolocation to find food in the water.
Fish have gills to take oxygen from water and fins to swim.
A fish's streamlined shape helps it move easily through water.
Fish that live in the deep ocean may make their own light to attract prey.
Frogs have long sticky tongues to catch insects.
A frog's webbed back feet help it swim and its strong legs help it jump.
A snake can unhinge its jaw to swallow prey whole.
Rattlesnakes have a rattle on their tail to warn predators.
Bright colors on some frogs and insects warn predators that they are poisonous.
A harmless viceroy butterfly looks like the bad-tasting monarch butterfly, so predators avoid it; this is mimicry.
A stick insect looks like a twig so predators do not see it; this is camouflage.
A walking leaf insect looks like a leaf, which hides it from predators.
Many moths are colored like tree bark so birds do not see them.
A flounder is flat and colored like the sand on the ocean floor so it can hide.
Insects have antennae to sense smells and touch.
Butterflies have long tube-like mouthparts to sip nectar from flowers.
Bees have hairy bodies that collect pollen as they visit flowers.
Mosquitoes have needle-like mouthparts to pierce skin and suck blood.
Earthworms have no lungs; they breathe through their moist skin.
Whales breathe air through a blowhole on top of the head.
Sea turtles return to the beach where they hatched to lay eggs.
Salmon swim upstream to lay eggs in the river where they hatched.
Plants that grow in the shade of a forest often have large broad leaves to capture more light.
Conifers such as pine trees have needle-shaped leaves with a waxy coating that lose little water and shed snow.
Water lilies have large flat leaves that float to catch sunlight on the surface of a pond.
Some plants have thorns or spines to keep animals from eating them.
Venus flytraps catch insects to get nitrogen because they grow in poor soil.
Seeds with hooks, such as burrs, stick to animal fur and are carried to new places.
Seeds like dandelion and maple seeds are carried by the wind.
Coconuts float and are carried by ocean water to new places.
Animals eat fruits and drop the seeds in their wastes far from the parent plant.
Squirrels bury acorns and nuts, and some forgotten nuts grow into new trees.
Bright colorful flowers and sweet smells attract insects and birds that pollinate the flowers.
Deciduous trees lose their leaves in fall to save water and energy during winter.

# behaviors
Instinct is a behavior an animal is born with and does not have to learn.
A spider spinning a web, a bird building a nest, and a baby sucking are instincts.
Birds migrating south in the fall is an inherited instinct triggered by shorter days and cooler temperatures.
Learned behaviors are gained by experience or by watching others, such as a dog learning a trick.
A bear learning to open trash cans or a raccoon learning to find food in a town is a learned behavior.
Chimpanzees using sticks to catch termites learned this behavior by watching others.
Hibernation is a deep sleep in winter that saves energy when food is scarce; bears, ground squirrels and bats hibernate.
During hibernation an animal's heart rate, breathing and body temperature drop to save energy.
Migration is moving to another region when the seasons change to find food, warmer weather or a place to breed.
Monarch butterflies, geese, whales and caribou migrate.
Some animals store food for winter, such as squirrels that bury nuts and bees that store honey.
Wolves hunt in packs so they can catch larger prey.
Fish swim in schools and birds fly in flocks for protection from predators.
Bees do a dance to tell other bees where to find food.
Male birds sing and show bright feathers to attract a mate.
Animals mark and defend a territory to keep food and mates for themselves.
Many desert animals are active at night, when it is cooler, and rest in burrows during the hot day; they are nocturnal.
Lizards and snakes lie in the sun to warm their bodies because they are cold-blooded.
A plant growing toward light is phototropism; roots growing downward with gravity is gravitropism.
A vine wrapping around a pole is thigmotropism, a response to touch.
Roots growing toward water is hydrotropism.
A plant closing its leaves when touched, like the sensitive plant, is a response to a stimulus.
A stimulus is something in the environment that causes a response, such as light, sound, heat or touch.
Pulling a hand away from a hot object is a reflex, a quick response controlled by the spinal cord.
Pupils of the eye get smaller in bright light and larger in dim light.
Shivering is the body's response to cold; muscles move quickly to produce heat.
Sweating is the body's response to heat.

# inherited and acquired traits
Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring through genes, such as eye color, hair color, height potential and the shape of a leaf.
Acquired traits are gained during an organism's life and are not passed to offspring, such as scars, a broken bone, a suntan, big muscles from exercise or knowing how to read.
A dog's fur color is inherited; a dog's ability to fetch a ball is learned.
Dimples, freckles, attached earlobes and the ability to roll the tongue are inherited traits.
A person's height and weight are affected by both genes and the environment, such as diet.
Plants of the same type may grow to different heights if they get different amounts of light, water or nutrients.
Hydrangea flowers can be blue or pink depending on the acidity of the soil, an environmental effect on a trait.
Identical twins have the same genes, so differences between them are caused by the environment.
Offspring from sexual reproduction get half their genes from each parent, so they look similar to but not exactly like their parents.
Offspring from asexual reproduction are identical to the single parent.
Cuttings, runners, bulbs and tubers produce new plants that are identical to the parent plant.
A strawberry plant can reproduce asexually with runners.
Bacteria reproduce asexually by splitting in two, called binary fission.
Yeast reproduces asexually by budding.
Selective breeding is when people choose plants or animals with desired traits to breed, such as cows that give more milk.
Different dog breeds were produced by selective breeding.
A mutation is a change in a gene; it can be harmful, helpful or have no effect.
Mutations in body cells are not passed on; mutations in sex cells can be passed to offspring.
Genes are found on chromosomes in the nucleus of the cell and are made of DNA.
Each human body cell has 46 chromosomes, 23 pairs; sperm and egg cells have 23 chromosomes.
Two parents with brown eyes can have a blue-eyed child if both carry the recessive allele.
A recessive trait shows only when an organism has two recessive alleles.
A dominant allele hides a recessive allele.
When two heterozygous parents Tt are crossed, about three fourths of the offspring show the dominant trait and one fourth show the recessive trait.
When a homozygous dominant parent TT is crossed with a homozygous recessive parent tt, all offspring are Tt and show the dominant trait.
When a heterozygous parent Tt is crossed with a recessive parent tt, about half of the offspring show the recessive trait.

# ecosystems: what happens when
If a predator is removed from an ecosystem, the population of its prey usually increases at first.
If hawks are removed, the number of mice and snakes they eat will increase, and then the mice may eat too many plants.
If wolves are removed from a forest, the deer population grows and the deer overgraze plants.
If the number of predators increases, the number of prey decreases.
If the number of prey decreases, the number of predators decreases because they have less food.
If the plants in an area die, the herbivores that eat them will decrease, and then the carnivores will decrease.
When a new species is introduced into an area, it may compete with native species for food and space.
Invasive species such as zebra mussels, kudzu and cane toads have no natural predators and spread quickly, harming native species.
When two species need the same food, they compete, and the population of one may decrease.
Organisms compete for food, water, sunlight, space and mates.
Plants compete for sunlight, water, nutrients and space.
A drought reduces the amount of plants and water, so populations of animals decrease.
A forest fire destroys habitats, but new plants grow from seeds in the ash-enriched soil.
After a forest fire or a field is abandoned, grasses grow first, then shrubs, then trees; this is secondary succession.
Lichens and mosses are pioneer species that grow first on bare rock and start forming soil.
Cutting down a forest destroys the habitats of many animals.
Building cities, roads and farms destroys natural habitats.
Pollution of a river can kill fish and the animals that eat the fish.
Pesticides can build up in the food chain and harm birds such as eagles that eat many contaminated fish.
Fertilizer runoff into lakes causes algae to grow quickly; when the algae die, bacteria decompose them and use up the oxygen, so fish die.
Wetlands filter pollutants from water, reduce flooding and provide habitat for many species.
Coral reefs are damaged by warmer ocean water, pollution and acid in the ocean.
Bees pollinate many crops; if bee populations decrease, fewer fruits and vegetables are produced.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and earthworms break down dead plants and animals and return nutrients to the soil.
Without decomposers, dead organisms and wastes would pile up and nutrients would not be returned to the soil for plants.
Earthworms loosen soil, let air and water in, and add nutrients with their wastes.
Plants provide food, oxygen and shelter for animals.
Animals help plants by pollinating flowers, spreading seeds and adding nutrients to soil with their wastes.
An ecosystem with many different species, high biodiversity, is more stable and recovers better from changes.
The energy in an ecosystem comes from the Sun; producers capture it and it passes to consumers.
Only about ten percent of the energy at one level of a food chain passes to the next level.
There are fewer organisms at the top of an energy pyramid because energy is lost as heat at each level.
Producers such as grass, trees and algae are at the bottom of an energy pyramid.
Top predators such as hawks, eagles and wolves are at the top of an energy pyramid.
The arrows in a food chain show the direction energy flows, from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it.
A food web is made of many food chains that are connected.
Grass is a producer; a rabbit that eats grass is a primary consumer; a fox that eats the rabbit is a secondary consumer.
Phytoplankton are tiny producers at the base of ocean food chains.
Zooplankton eat phytoplankton and are eaten by small fish.
Mutualism is a relationship in which both organisms benefit, such as bees and flowers.
Commensalism is a relationship in which one organism benefits and the other is not affected, such as a barnacle on a whale or a bird nesting in a tree.
Parasitism is a relationship in which one organism benefits and the other is harmed, such as a tick on a dog or a tapeworm in an animal.
A lichen is a fungus and an alga living together in mutualism.
Clownfish and sea anemones help each other; this is mutualism.
Biotic factors are living parts of an ecosystem such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are nonliving parts of an ecosystem such as sunlight, water, air, temperature, rocks and soil.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives and gets food, water, shelter and space.
A niche is the role an organism plays in its ecosystem.
The carrying capacity is the largest population of a species that an environment can support.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space and predators keep populations from growing forever.
A population is all the organisms of one species living in an area, such as all the bluegill fish in a pond.
A community is all the populations of different species living in an area, such as all the plants and animals in a pond.
An ecosystem is a community of living things together with the nonliving things in their environment.
