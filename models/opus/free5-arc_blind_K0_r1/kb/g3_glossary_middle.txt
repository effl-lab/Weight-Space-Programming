# middle school glossary: life science (one term per line)
An autotroph is an organism that makes its own food, such as a plant or alga; autotrophs are producers.
A heterotroph is an organism that gets its food by eating other organisms; heterotrophs are consumers.
Photosynthesis uses light energy, carbon dioxide and water to make glucose and oxygen in chloroplasts.
Cellular respiration uses glucose and oxygen to release energy, producing carbon dioxide and water, in the mitochondria.
Fermentation releases energy from food without oxygen and produces alcohol or lactic acid.
Glucose is a simple sugar made by photosynthesis that cells use for energy.
ATP is the molecule cells use to store and transfer energy.
Ribosomes are organelles that make proteins.
The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes that transports materials inside the cell.
The Golgi apparatus packages and ships proteins out of the cell.
Lysosomes contain enzymes that break down wastes and worn-out cell parts.
Vacuoles store water, food and wastes; plant cells have one large central vacuole.
The cytoplasm is the jelly-like fluid inside the cell membrane where organelles are found.
The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
The cell wall is a rigid layer outside the cell membrane of plant, fungus and bacterial cells that supports and protects the cell.
Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll and are where photosynthesis happens.
The nucleus contains the DNA and controls the cell's activities.
Prokaryotes, such as bacteria, are single-celled organisms whose cells have no nucleus.
Eukaryotes are organisms whose cells have a nucleus, such as plants, animals, fungi and protists.
Unicellular organisms are made of one cell; multicellular organisms are made of many cells.
Protists are mostly single-celled eukaryotes, such as amoebas, paramecia and euglena.
An amoeba moves and eats with pseudopods; a paramecium moves with cilia; a euglena moves with a flagellum.
Diffusion is the movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration.
Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a cell membrane.
Passive transport moves materials across a membrane without using energy.
Active transport uses energy to move materials across a membrane from low to high concentration.
Mitosis is cell division that makes two identical body cells with the same number of chromosomes; it is used for growth and repair.
Meiosis is cell division that makes sex cells, sperm and eggs, with half the number of chromosomes.
A zygote is a fertilized egg formed when a sperm and an egg join.
Fertilization is the joining of a sperm cell and an egg cell.
DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid, is the genetic material that carries hereditary information.
DNA is a double helix made of nucleotides; its four bases are adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine.
A gene is a section of DNA that codes for a protein and determines a trait.
A chromosome is a long strand of DNA wrapped around proteins, found in the nucleus.
Alleles are different forms of the same gene.
Genotype is the combination of alleles an organism has, such as Tt; phenotype is the trait that shows, such as tall.
Homozygous means having two identical alleles, such as TT or tt; heterozygous means having two different alleles, such as Tt.
A Punnett square shows the possible combinations of alleles in offspring.
Gregor Mendel studied pea plants and is called the father of genetics.
Incomplete dominance is when neither allele is dominant and the offspring show a blend, such as pink flowers from red and white parents.
Codominance is when both alleles are fully shown, such as a cow with red and white hairs.
A polygenic trait is controlled by more than one gene, such as human height or skin color.
A sex-linked trait is controlled by a gene on the X chromosome, such as color blindness.
Females have two X chromosomes; males have an X and a Y chromosome.
A pedigree is a chart that shows how a trait is passed through generations of a family.
Genetic engineering is changing an organism's DNA to give it new traits, such as bacteria that make insulin.
A clone is an organism with exactly the same DNA as its parent.
A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of a gene.
Proteins are made of amino acids; amino acids are the building blocks of proteins.
Carbohydrates, such as sugars and starch, are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen and give energy.
Lipids, such as fats and oils, store energy.
Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, store and carry genetic information.
Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living things.
Evolution is the change in the inherited traits of a species over many generations.
Natural selection is the process in which organisms with traits better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more.
Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection after studying finches and tortoises on the Galapagos Islands.
Variation is the differences among individuals of the same species.
An adaptation is an inherited trait that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
A species is a group of organisms that can breed with each other and produce fertile offspring.
Speciation is the formation of a new species, often when populations are separated by a barrier.
Homologous structures, such as a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper, have similar bones and show a common ancestor.
Analogous structures, such as a bird wing and an insect wing, have the same function but different structures.
Vestigial structures, such as the human appendix and the leg bones of whales, have little or no function but show evolutionary history.
Extinction happens when every member of a species has died.
Antibiotic resistance evolves when bacteria that survive an antibiotic reproduce and pass on their resistance.
Taxonomy is the science of naming and classifying organisms.
Binomial nomenclature gives each species a two-part scientific name, the genus and the species, such as Homo sapiens.
Carl Linnaeus developed the system of classifying and naming organisms.
The levels of classification from broadest to most specific are domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species.
The six kingdoms are archaebacteria, eubacteria, protists, fungi, plants and animals.
Fungi, such as mushrooms, yeast and molds, are eukaryotes that absorb food from their surroundings and reproduce with spores.
Vascular plants, such as ferns, conifers and flowering plants, have tubes called xylem and phloem.
Xylem carries water and minerals up from the roots; phloem carries sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant.
Nonvascular plants, such as mosses and liverworts, have no tubes, are small and live in moist places.
Gymnosperms, such as pine trees, have seeds in cones; angiosperms have seeds inside fruits and make flowers.
Ferns and mosses reproduce with spores instead of seeds.
Monocots have one seed leaf, parallel veins and flower parts in threes; dicots have two seed leaves and branching veins.
The cotyledon is the seed leaf that stores food for the young plant.
Transpiration is the loss of water vapor from plant leaves through stomata.
Stomata are tiny openings on leaves, opened and closed by guard cells, that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapor out.
A tropism is a plant's growth response toward or away from a stimulus.
Invertebrates are animals without a backbone; vertebrates have a backbone.
Arthropods have jointed legs, segmented bodies and an exoskeleton; they include insects, spiders, crabs and centipedes.
An exoskeleton is a hard outer covering that protects and supports an animal, such as an insect or crab; it must be shed to grow, called molting.
An endoskeleton is an internal skeleton, as in vertebrates.
Mollusks are soft-bodied invertebrates, often with shells, such as snails, clams and octopuses.
Annelids are segmented worms such as earthworms and leeches.
Cnidarians such as jellyfish and corals have stinging tentacles.
Echinoderms such as sea stars and sea urchins have spiny skin and tube feet.
Endotherms, or warm-blooded animals such as birds and mammals, keep a constant body temperature.
Ectotherms, or cold-blooded animals such as reptiles, amphibians and fish, have a body temperature that changes with the environment.
Marsupials, such as kangaroos and koalas, carry their young in a pouch.
Monotremes, such as the platypus, are mammals that lay eggs.
A trophic level is a feeding level in a food chain.
Biomass is the total mass of living things at a level of a food chain.
Bioaccumulation is the buildup of a toxic substance, such as a pesticide, in organisms at higher levels of a food chain.
Eutrophication is the overgrowth of algae in water caused by excess nutrients from fertilizer or sewage, which lowers the oxygen.
A keystone species, such as a sea otter, has a large effect on its ecosystem.
An indicator species, such as a frog or lichen, shows the health of an environment.
Symbiosis is a close long-term relationship between two species.
A biome is a large region with a similar climate and similar plants and animals, such as a desert, tundra or rainforest.
Primary succession begins on bare rock where there is no soil; secondary succession begins where soil remains after a disturbance.
A climax community is the stable community at the end of succession.
The nitrogen cycle moves nitrogen through the air, soil and living things; bacteria fix nitrogen into forms plants can use.
The carbon cycle moves carbon through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and burning fuels.
An antigen is a substance on a germ that the immune system recognizes; antibodies attach to antigens.
A pathogen is a germ that causes disease.
Immunity is the body's ability to resist a disease.

# middle school glossary: earth and space
Extrusive igneous rock forms when lava cools quickly at Earth's surface and has small crystals, such as basalt.
Intrusive igneous rock forms when magma cools slowly underground and has large crystals, such as granite.
Clastic sedimentary rock is made of pieces of other rocks, such as sandstone and conglomerate.
Foliated metamorphic rocks such as slate and gneiss have minerals arranged in layers or bands.
The lithosphere is the rigid outer layer of Earth made of the crust and upper mantle; it is broken into plates.
The asthenosphere is the soft, partly melted layer of the mantle on which the plates move.
A convergent boundary is where two plates move toward each other.
A divergent boundary is where two plates move apart, as at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
A transform boundary is where two plates slide past each other, as at the San Andreas Fault.
Subduction is when a denser ocean plate sinks beneath another plate into the mantle.
A volcanic island arc such as Japan forms where one ocean plate subducts beneath another ocean plate.
Continental drift is Alfred Wegener's idea that the continents slowly move across Earth's surface.
Pangaea was a supercontinent that broke apart about 200 million years ago.
Sea-floor spreading creates new ocean crust at mid-ocean ridges.
The focus of an earthquake is the point underground where it starts; the epicenter is the point on the surface above it.
P waves are fast compression waves that travel through solids, liquids and gases.
S waves are slower waves that travel only through solids; liquids stop S waves.
S waves are stopped by the outer core, which shows that the outer core is liquid.
The Richter scale and the moment magnitude scale measure the strength of earthquakes.
A fault is a crack in Earth's crust along which rocks move.
Magma is melted rock below the surface; lava is melted rock that reaches the surface.
A shield volcano has gentle slopes from runny lava; a composite or stratovolcano has steep sides from layers of ash and lava.
A hot spot is an area where magma rises from deep in the mantle, forming volcanoes such as Hawaii.
Relative dating finds whether a rock or fossil is older or younger than another; absolute dating finds its age in years.
Half-life is the time it takes for half of the atoms of a radioactive element to decay.
An unconformity is a gap in the rock record where layers were eroded away.
The law of crosscutting relationships says a fault or intrusion is younger than the rocks it cuts through.
The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into eons, eras, periods and epochs.
Humus is the dark decayed plant and animal material in soil.
Leaching is when water carries dissolved minerals down through the soil.
The water table is the top of the groundwater zone.
Permeable materials such as sand and gravel let water pass through; impermeable materials such as clay do not.
Porosity is the amount of open space between particles in a rock or soil.
The atmosphere is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and 1 percent argon, carbon dioxide and other gases.
The troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere, where weather happens and temperature drops with height.
The stratosphere is above the troposphere and contains the ozone layer.
The mesosphere is where most meteors burn up; the thermosphere is the hottest layer, where auroras occur.
The ionosphere is part of the thermosphere that reflects radio waves.
Air pressure is the weight of the air pressing down; it decreases as altitude increases.
The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water vapor condenses.
Relative humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air compared with the most it can hold at that temperature.
An air mass is a large body of air with the same temperature and humidity throughout.
Maritime air masses form over oceans and are moist; continental air masses form over land and are dry.
Tropical air masses are warm; polar air masses are cold.
A front is the boundary between two different air masses.
A stationary front forms when two air masses meet and neither moves; an occluded front forms when a cold front overtakes a warm front.
The jet stream is a band of fast high-altitude winds that moves weather systems from west to east across the United States.
The Coriolis effect, caused by Earth's rotation, curves winds and ocean currents.
Cumulus clouds are puffy fair-weather clouds; cumulonimbus clouds are tall thunderstorm clouds.
Stratus clouds are flat layers that cover the sky and may bring drizzle.
Cirrus clouds are high, thin, wispy clouds made of ice crystals.
Isobars are lines on a weather map that connect places with equal air pressure.
Isotherms are lines on a map that connect places with equal temperature.
Contour lines on a topographic map connect points of equal elevation; lines close together mean a steep slope.
Latitude lines measure distance north or south of the equator; longitude lines measure distance east or west of the prime meridian.
The prime meridian is 0 degrees longitude and passes through Greenwich, England.
Salinity is the amount of dissolved salt in water.
Upwelling brings cold, nutrient-rich water from the deep ocean to the surface.
The continental shelf is the shallow part of the ocean floor next to a continent.
An estuary is where fresh river water mixes with salt ocean water.
An astronomical unit is the average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 150 million kilometers.
A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion kilometers.
Gravity and inertia together keep planets and moons in their orbits.
An orbit is the curved path of an object around another object in space.
Planets have elliptical orbits, slightly flattened circles.
Rotation is spinning on an axis; revolution is moving in an orbit around another object.
A satellite is an object that orbits a larger object; the Moon is Earth's natural satellite.
Nuclear fusion in stars combines hydrogen nuclei into helium and releases huge amounts of energy.
A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust in space where stars form.
A main-sequence star such as the Sun is fusing hydrogen into helium in its core.
The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram shows stars by temperature and brightness.
Apparent brightness is how bright a star looks from Earth; absolute brightness is how much light it actually gives off.
Redshift of light from distant galaxies shows the universe is expanding.
The geocentric model placed Earth at the center of the universe; the heliocentric model of Copernicus placed the Sun at the center.
Galileo used a telescope to discover four moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, supporting the heliocentric model.
Johannes Kepler found that planets move in elliptical orbits.
Isaac Newton described the laws of motion and universal gravitation.

# middle school glossary: physical science
An atom is the smallest particle of an element that keeps the element's properties.
The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
The mass number is the number of protons plus the number of neutrons in the nucleus.
An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons and has a charge.
An atom that loses an electron becomes a positive ion; an atom that gains an electron becomes a negative ion.
Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell of an atom; they take part in chemical bonds.
The strong nuclear force holds protons and neutrons together in the nucleus.
Radioactive decay is when an unstable nucleus gives off particles and energy.
Nuclear fission splits a large nucleus into smaller nuclei and releases energy.
Nuclear fusion joins small nuclei into a larger nucleus and releases energy.
A chemical formula shows the elements and number of atoms in a compound, such as H2O and NaCl.
A chemical equation shows the reactants and products of a chemical reaction.
The law of conservation of mass says matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
A precipitate is a solid that forms from a reaction between solutions.
Solubility is the amount of a substance that can dissolve in a solvent at a given temperature.
A solute is the substance that dissolves; a solvent is the substance that does the dissolving.
Concentration is the amount of solute in a given amount of solution.
Sublimation is the change from a solid directly to a gas; deposition is the change from a gas directly to a solid.
Vaporization is the change from liquid to gas; it includes evaporation at the surface and boiling throughout the liquid.
Plasma is a fourth state of matter of charged particles, found in stars and lightning.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; potential energy is stored energy of position or condition.
The law of conservation of energy says energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed in form.
Work is force times distance, measured in joules.
Power is work divided by time, measured in watts.
Mechanical advantage is how many times a machine multiplies the input force.
Efficiency is the percent of input work that becomes useful output work.
Velocity is speed in a certain direction.
Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes.
Momentum is mass times velocity.
Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its motion.
Net force is the total of all forces acting on an object.
Terminal velocity is the constant speed a falling object reaches when air resistance equals its weight.
Centripetal force pulls an object moving in a circle toward the center.
Pressure is force divided by area.
Buoyant force is the upward push of a fluid; Archimedes' principle says it equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
A medium is the matter that a wave travels through.
Mechanical waves such as sound and water waves need a medium; electromagnetic waves such as light can travel through empty space.
The crest is the highest point of a wave and the trough is the lowest point.
Wave speed equals frequency times wavelength.
If the frequency of a wave doubles while its speed stays the same, its wavelength is cut in half.
Diffraction is the bending of a wave around an obstacle or through an opening.
Interference happens when two waves meet and add together or cancel out.
Resonance happens when an object vibrates at its natural frequency because of another vibration.
Specific heat is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius.
Absolute zero is the lowest possible temperature, 0 kelvin, where particles have the least motion.
Voltage is the push that moves electric charges through a circuit, measured in volts.
Current is the flow of electric charge, measured in amperes.
Resistance opposes the flow of current and is measured in ohms; Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance.
Thinner, longer wires have more resistance than thicker, shorter wires.
Alternating current changes direction many times a second; direct current, as from a battery, flows in one direction.
A semiconductor, such as silicon, conducts electricity better than an insulator but not as well as a conductor.
A magnetic field is the area around a magnet where its force acts.
Electromagnetic induction is making an electric current by moving a wire through a magnetic field.
A transformer changes the voltage of alternating current.
