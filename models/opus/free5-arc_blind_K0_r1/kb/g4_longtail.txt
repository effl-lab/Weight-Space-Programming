# technology and inventions
Sonar uses reflected sound waves (echoes) to find objects under water and measure the depth of the ocean.
Radar uses reflected radio waves to find the location and speed of airplanes, ships and storms.
Doppler radar shows where rain is falling and how storms are moving.
Fiber optic cables carry information as pulses of light through thin glass fibers by total internal reflection.
A laser produces a narrow, intense beam of light of one color.
A hologram is a three-dimensional image made with laser light.
Satellites orbiting Earth relay television, telephone and internet signals and take pictures of weather.
The Global Positioning System (GPS) uses signals from satellites to find locations on Earth.
A cell phone sends and receives messages using radio waves or microwaves.
The telegraph sent messages over wires as electrical signals in Morse code.
Alexander Graham Bell invented the telephone, which changes sound into electrical signals and back into sound.
Thomas Edison invented a practical electric light bulb and the phonograph.
The Wright brothers built and flew the first powered airplane in 1903.
A transistor is a small electronic switch made of semiconductor material such as silicon.
A microchip or computer chip contains millions of tiny transistors.
Robots are machines that can be programmed to do tasks that are repetitive or dangerous for people.
A steam engine burns fuel to boil water; the steam pushes a piston to make motion.
In a car engine, burning gasoline pushes pistons that turn the crankshaft.
A propeller pushes air or water backward so that a plane or boat moves forward.
A glider flies without an engine by using rising air and the lift from its wings.
Airplane wings are shaped so that air moving over them produces lift.
A submarine dives by filling tanks with water and rises by pushing the water out with air.
A buoy is a floating marker in water that warns ships or carries weather instruments.
A lighthouse uses a bright light to warn ships of rocks and the shore.
A waterwheel uses the energy of flowing water to turn machinery such as a mill.
A thermostat turns a heating or cooling system on and off to keep a set temperature.
A refrigerator moves heat from inside to outside using a liquid that evaporates and condenses.
Refrigeration slows the growth of bacteria and keeps food from spoiling.
Pasteurization heats milk to kill harmful bacteria.
Canning, freezing, drying and salting foods slow the growth of microorganisms.
Irrigation brings water to crops through ditches, pipes or sprinklers.
Drip irrigation saves water by delivering it directly to plant roots.
Hydroponics is growing plants in water with dissolved nutrients instead of soil.
A greenhouse traps heat from sunlight so that plants can grow in cold weather.
A pacemaker is a device that helps the heart beat regularly.
A stethoscope is used to listen to the heart and lungs.
X-rays are used to take pictures of bones; MRI and ultrasound show soft tissues and organs.
Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves to make images of organs or of a baby before birth.
A periscope uses mirrors to see over or around objects.
A kaleidoscope uses mirrors to make colorful patterns.
A sundial uses the shadow of a stick to tell the time of day.
An hourglass measures time with sand falling through a narrow opening.
A metronome keeps a steady beat for musicians.
A tuning fork vibrates at one frequency and makes one pitch.
A spectroscope separates light into its colors so scientists can learn what stars are made of.
An aquarium is a tank that holds water organisms; a terrarium is a container that holds land plants and small animals.
# scientists
Gregor Mendel studied pea plants and discovered the basic rules of heredity; he is called the father of genetics.
Charles Darwin developed the theory of evolution by natural selection after studying finches and other animals of the Galapagos Islands.
Louis Pasteur showed that germs cause disease and invented pasteurization.
Edward Jenner developed the first vaccine, against smallpox.
Jonas Salk developed a vaccine against polio.
Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, the first antibiotic, which comes from a mold.
Joseph Lister began using antiseptics to clean wounds and surgical tools.
Anton van Leeuwenhoek improved the microscope and was the first to see bacteria and other microorganisms.
Robert Hooke looked at cork through a microscope and named cells.
Isaac Newton described the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation.
Galileo used a telescope to discover the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, supporting the idea that Earth orbits the Sun.
Nicolaus Copernicus proposed that the Sun, not Earth, is at the center of the solar system.
Ptolemy and Aristotle believed that Earth was at the center of the universe.
Johannes Kepler discovered that planets move in elliptical orbits.
Edwin Hubble discovered that there are other galaxies and that the universe is expanding.
Dmitri Mendeleev organized the elements into the first periodic table.
Marie Curie studied radioactivity and discovered the elements polonium and radium.
Albert Einstein developed the theory of relativity and the equation E = mc squared.
Benjamin Franklin showed with his kite experiment that lightning is electricity.
Michael Faraday discovered that a moving magnet can produce an electric current.
John Dalton proposed that all matter is made of tiny particles called atoms.
J. J. Thomson discovered the electron.
Ernest Rutherford discovered that an atom has a small dense nucleus.
Niels Bohr proposed that electrons move in energy levels around the nucleus.
Antoine Lavoisier showed that mass is conserved in chemical reactions.
Alfred Wegener proposed the theory of continental drift.
George Washington Carver developed hundreds of uses for peanuts and taught crop rotation to improve soil.
Rachel Carson wrote Silent Spring about the dangers of pesticides such as DDT to birds and the environment.
Jane Goodall studied the behavior of chimpanzees in the wild.
# human body details
Tooth enamel is the hardest substance in the human body; dentin lies under the enamel.
Bone marrow inside bones makes red and white blood cells.
Cartilage is a flexible tissue found in the nose, ears and at the ends of bones in joints.
The epiglottis is a flap that covers the windpipe when you swallow so food goes into the esophagus.
The larynx, or voice box, contains vocal cords that vibrate to make sounds.
The pharynx is the throat, which carries both food and air.
The trachea, or windpipe, carries air to the lungs.
The esophagus is a tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach by muscle contractions.
Villi are tiny finger-like structures in the small intestine that absorb nutrients into the blood.
The liver makes bile, which helps digest fats; bile is stored in the gallbladder.
The pancreas makes digestive juices and the hormone insulin.
The tonsils help fight infections in the throat.
The cornea is the clear front part of the eye; the iris is the colored part that controls the size of the pupil.
The lens of the eye focuses light on the retina at the back of the eye.
The retina has cells that sense light and send signals through the optic nerve to the brain.
The pupil gets smaller in bright light and larger in dim light.
The eardrum vibrates when sound waves hit it; the cochlea in the inner ear changes vibrations into nerve signals.
Small bones in the middle ear carry vibrations from the eardrum to the inner ear.
The semicircular canals in the inner ear help the body keep its balance.
Taste buds on the tongue sense sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savory tastes.
The nose has receptors that detect smells.
Melanin is the pigment that gives skin and hair their color and protects skin from ultraviolet light.
Hair and nails are made of a protein called keratin.
Sweat glands in the skin release sweat to cool the body.
Hemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen.
Plasma is the liquid part of blood.
Blood clots form when platelets stick together at a cut.
The left side of the heart pumps oxygen-rich blood to the body; the right side pumps blood to the lungs.
The heart has four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.
Valves in the heart and veins keep blood flowing in one direction.
Skeletal muscles move bones, smooth muscles move food through the intestines, and cardiac muscle makes up the heart.
An adult human has 206 bones.
The femur, or thigh bone, is the longest bone in the body.
The spinal cord carries messages between the brain and the rest of the body.
Hormones are chemical messengers carried by the blood.
The thyroid gland controls how fast the body uses energy.
Adrenaline is a hormone that prepares the body to react in an emergency by increasing heart rate and breathing.
Puberty is controlled by hormones.
# organisms
An amoeba is a single-celled protist that moves and engulfs food with pseudopods.
A paramecium is a single-celled protist covered with cilia that it uses to move.
Euglena is a single-celled protist that has chloroplasts and makes food but can also eat other organisms; it moves with a flagellum.
Diatoms and other algae are protists that make food by photosynthesis and produce much of Earth's oxygen.
Volvox is a colony of green algae cells.
Yeast is a single-celled fungus used to make bread rise; it produces carbon dioxide gas as it ferments sugar.
Mold and mushrooms are fungi that get food by absorbing it from dead or living things.
Fungi reproduce with spores and cannot make their own food.
A hydra is a small freshwater animal related to jellyfish that has tentacles with stinging cells.
A planarian is a flatworm that can regrow lost body parts.
Tapeworms are parasites that live in the intestines of animals.
Leeches are segmented worms that suck blood.
Sea stars (starfish) and sea urchins have spiny skin and tube feet.
A sea star can regrow a lost arm.
Sponges are simple animals with pores that filter food from water.
Coral polyps build reefs from calcium carbonate and get food from algae living inside them.
Squids and octopuses are mollusks with tentacles; octopuses squirt ink to escape predators.
Clams, oysters, mussels and snails are mollusks; many have shells.
Lobsters, crabs, shrimp and barnacles are crustaceans with an exoskeleton.
Krill are tiny crustaceans eaten by whales, penguins and fish.
Barnacles attach to rocks, ships and whales.
Lichens are a partnership between a fungus and algae.
Mosses are small plants without roots or tubes that grow in damp, shady places.
Ferns have roots, stems and leaves (fronds) and reproduce with spores.
Conifers such as pines, spruces and firs produce seeds in cones.
Flowering plants produce seeds inside fruits.
Venus flytraps catch insects to get nitrogen because they grow in poor soil.
Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly.
A platypus is a mammal that lays eggs.
A kangaroo is a marsupial that carries its young in a pouch.
Sharks have skeletons made of cartilage instead of bone.
Salmon swim from the ocean up rivers to lay eggs where they hatched.
Monarch butterflies migrate thousands of miles to Mexico for the winter.
Geese fly south in the fall to find food and warmer weather.
Bears eat large amounts of food in the fall and hibernate in winter.
Squirrels gather and store nuts in the fall for the winter.
Owls are nocturnal birds of prey with excellent night vision and hearing.
A woodpecker drills into tree bark with its strong pointed beak to find insects.
A chameleon catches insects with its long sticky tongue.
A snake smells with its tongue.
Frogs catch insects with a long sticky tongue.
Ants and bees live in colonies and divide work among different members.
Honeybees communicate the location of food with a dance.
Fireflies make light by a chemical reaction to attract mates.
Earthworms breathe through their moist skin.
A tadpole breathes with gills; an adult frog breathes with lungs and through its skin.
# elements and substances
Hydrogen is the lightest and most common element in the universe.
Helium is a light, unreactive gas used to fill balloons because it is less dense than air.
Oxygen is needed for burning and for cellular respiration.
Nitrogen makes up most of the air; plants need nitrogen compounds from the soil to grow.
Carbon is found in all living things and in fossil fuels; diamond and graphite are made of carbon.
Sodium is a soft, very reactive metal; chlorine is a poisonous green gas; together they form table salt.
Calcium is needed for strong bones and teeth.
Iron is a metal that is attracted to magnets and rusts; steel is mostly iron.
Iron is needed in the blood to carry oxygen.
Aluminum is a light metal used in cans, foil and airplanes; it is not magnetic.
Copper is a metal used in electrical wires and pipes.
Gold and silver are metals that conduct electricity well and do not rust.
Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature.
Lead is a heavy, poisonous metal.
Silicon is used to make computer chips and is found in sand and quartz.
Neon glows in signs when electricity passes through it.
Lithium is used in rechargeable batteries.
Magnesium burns with a bright white light.
Uranium is a radioactive element used as fuel in nuclear power plants.
Titanium is a strong, light metal used in airplanes and artificial joints.
Fluoride in toothpaste and water helps prevent tooth decay.
Chlorine is added to swimming pools and drinking water to kill bacteria.
Iodine is added to salt and is needed by the thyroid gland.
Ozone is a form of oxygen with three oxygen atoms.
Methane is natural gas; it is a greenhouse gas produced by cattle, landfills and wetlands.
Glucose is a sugar made by plants in photosynthesis; its formula is C6H12O6.
Water boils at a lower temperature at high altitude because air pressure is lower.
Salt water freezes at a lower temperature than fresh water.
Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon; brass is an alloy of copper and zinc; bronze is an alloy of copper and tin.
Glass is made by melting sand.
Plastic is made from petroleum (oil), a nonrenewable resource.
Paper is made from wood pulp from trees.
Cotton and wool are natural fibers; nylon and polyester are synthetic fibers made from petroleum.
Rubber comes from the sap of rubber trees or is made from petroleum.
Concrete is made from cement, sand, gravel and water.
Ceramics such as clay pots are made by heating clay to a high temperature.
# landforms, water and glaciers
A drumlin is an oval hill of sediment shaped by a glacier.
Outwash is sand and gravel deposited by meltwater streams in front of a glacier.
An esker is a long winding ridge of sand and gravel deposited by a stream under a glacier.
A kettle lake forms when a block of ice left by a glacier melts in a hole in the sediment.
A glacial erratic is a large boulder carried by a glacier and left far from where it came from.
Scratches (striations) on bedrock show the direction a glacier moved.
Glaciers once covered much of New York State and northern North America during the Ice Age.
The Finger Lakes of New York were carved by glaciers.
Long Island was formed from material deposited by glaciers.
Niagara Falls was formed as water eroded rock layers after the glaciers melted.
The Adirondack Mountains in New York are made of very old metamorphic rock.
Karst landscapes with caves and sinkholes form where groundwater dissolves limestone.
Stalactites hang from cave ceilings and stalagmites grow up from cave floors as dripping water deposits minerals.
A geyser shoots hot water and steam into the air because groundwater is heated by hot rock or magma underground.
Old Faithful in Yellowstone is a famous geyser above a hot spot.
A hot spot is a place where magma rises through the middle of a plate, forming volcanoes such as the Hawaiian Islands.
Mount St. Helens is a volcano in Washington State that erupted explosively in 1980.
Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where plates pull apart, and has many volcanoes.
The Andes and the Rocky Mountains formed where plates collided.
The Appalachian Mountains are old mountains that have been worn down by erosion.
Mount Everest in the Himalayas is the tallest mountain on Earth.
The Sahara is the largest hot desert on Earth.
The Amazon rain forest in South America has the greatest number of species of any land area.
The Mississippi River carries sediment and deposits it in a large delta at the Gulf of Mexico.
A meander is a curve in a river; an oxbow lake forms when a meander is cut off.
A floodplain is flat land beside a river that is covered with water and new sediment during floods.
A levee is a ridge along a river bank that helps prevent flooding.
A tributary is a smaller stream that flows into a larger river.
A watershed is all the land area that drains into a river or lake.
The headwaters are the source of a river, often in mountains.
An estuary is where fresh river water mixes with salt ocean water; it is a nursery for many fish.
A lagoon is shallow water separated from the ocean by a sandbar, reef or barrier island.
Barrier islands protect the coast from storm waves.
Jetties, groins and breakwaters are built to reduce beach erosion by waves.
A sandbar is a ridge of sand built by waves and currents.
A peninsula is land surrounded by water on three sides; an island is land surrounded by water.
A plateau is a high, flat area of land; a plain is a low, flat area.
A canyon is a deep valley with steep sides carved by a river.
A V-shaped valley is carved by a river; a U-shaped valley is carved by a glacier.
The continental shelf is the shallow ocean floor near the coast.
The Mariana Trench is the deepest place in the ocean.
# more specific facts
Stalactites hang down from the ceiling of a cave.
Stalagmites build up from the floor of a cave.
Birds with hooked beaks and sharp talons, such as hawks, eagles and owls, catch and eat small animals such as mice and rabbits.
A bird with a long thin beak, such as a hummingbird, drinks nectar from flowers.
A bird with a short thick beak, such as a finch or cardinal, cracks seeds.
A moth that looks like tree bark is using camouflage to hide from birds.
A walking stick insect looks like a twig, which is camouflage.
A hydroelectric power plant uses moving water flowing through a dam to turn turbines and generate electricity.
A nuclear power plant uses heat from nuclear fission of uranium to make steam and generate electricity.
Hydroponics lets people grow plants without soil.
Insects such as beetles, ants, bees and grasshoppers have an exoskeleton.
Bees pollinate the flowers of tomato, apple, squash and many other plants so that the plants can produce fruit.
