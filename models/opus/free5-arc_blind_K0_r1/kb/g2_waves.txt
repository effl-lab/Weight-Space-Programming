# waves, sound and light (second set)
A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter.
Mechanical waves, such as sound waves, water waves and seismic waves, need a medium, a solid, liquid or gas, to travel through.
Electromagnetic waves, such as light, radio waves and x rays, do not need a medium and can travel through empty space.
In a transverse wave the medium moves perpendicular, at right angles, to the direction the wave travels, like a wave on a rope or light.
In a longitudinal or compression wave the medium moves back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels, like sound.
The crest is the high point of a transverse wave and the trough is the low point.
Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest.
Amplitude is the height of a wave from its rest position; larger amplitude means more energy.
Frequency is the number of waves that pass a point each second, measured in hertz.
Higher frequency waves have shorter wavelengths; lower frequency waves have longer wavelengths.
Waves can be reflected, refracted, diffracted and absorbed.
Reflection is when a wave bounces off a surface.
Refraction is when a wave bends as it passes from one medium into another and changes speed.
Diffraction is when a wave bends around an obstacle or spreads out through an opening.
Sound is a wave of vibrations that travels through matter.
All sounds are made by vibrating objects, such as a guitar string, a drum head, vocal cords or a speaker cone.
Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases.
Sound travels about 343 meters per second in air, faster in water, and even faster in steel.
Sound cannot travel through a vacuum, such as outer space, because there are no particles to vibrate.
Pitch is how high or low a sound is; it depends on the frequency of the vibrations.
Faster vibrations make a higher pitch; slower vibrations make a lower pitch.
Short, thin, tight strings vibrate faster and make higher pitches; long, thick, loose strings make lower pitches.
A small drum or short air column makes a higher pitch than a large drum or long air column.
Loudness, or volume, depends on the amplitude of the sound wave; plucking or hitting harder makes a louder sound.
Loudness is measured in decibels; very loud sounds can damage hearing.
An echo is a sound reflected off a surface back to the listener.
Soft materials such as carpet, curtains, foam and cloth absorb sound; hard smooth surfaces such as walls, tile and glass reflect sound.
Bats and dolphins use echolocation, sending out sounds and listening for echoes to find food and objects.
Sonar uses reflected sound waves to find objects under water and measure depth.
Ultrasound is sound with a frequency too high for humans to hear; it is used to make images of the inside of the body.
Infrasound is sound with a frequency too low for humans to hear; elephants and whales use it to communicate.
Dogs can hear higher pitched sounds than humans can.
The Doppler effect is the change in pitch of a sound as its source moves toward or away from you, like a passing siren.
Light is a form of electromagnetic energy that travels as waves and can travel through empty space.
Light travels in straight lines until it hits something.
Light travels at about 300,000 kilometers per second in a vacuum, the fastest speed in the universe.
Light travels much faster than sound, which is why we see lightning before we hear thunder.
Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light; a shadow is larger when the object is closer to the light source.
Luminous objects such as the Sun, stars, fire and light bulbs give off their own light; we see other objects because they reflect light into our eyes.
We see an object when light reflected from it enters our eyes.
Light can be reflected, absorbed, transmitted or refracted when it hits an object.
The law of reflection: the angle at which light hits a mirror equals the angle at which it reflects.
Smooth shiny surfaces such as mirrors reflect light in one direction and form images; rough surfaces scatter light.
A plane mirror forms an image that is upright, the same size, and reversed left to right.
Concave mirrors curve inward and can magnify or focus light, as in flashlights, headlights and telescopes; convex mirrors curve outward and show a wider view, as in car side mirrors and store security mirrors.
Refraction makes a straw look bent in a glass of water and makes a pool look shallower than it is.
A lens is a curved piece of clear glass or plastic that refracts light.
A convex lens is thicker in the middle; it brings light rays together and can magnify objects, as in magnifying glasses, microscopes, cameras and the human eye.
A concave lens is thinner in the middle; it spreads light rays apart.
Eyeglasses use lenses to correct vision: nearsighted people use concave lenses and farsighted people use convex lenses.
White light is a mixture of all the colors of the visible spectrum: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet.
A prism separates white light into a spectrum of colors because each color bends a different amount.
A rainbow forms when sunlight is refracted and reflected by raindrops, separating it into colors; you see a rainbow with the Sun behind you.
Red light has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency of visible light; violet has the shortest wavelength and highest frequency.
An object looks a certain color because it reflects that color of light and absorbs the others; a red apple reflects red light.
White objects reflect all colors of light; black objects absorb all colors of light.
The sky looks blue because molecules in the air scatter blue light more than other colors.
Sunsets look red and orange because sunlight passes through more air, which scatters away the blue light.
The electromagnetic spectrum from longest to shortest wavelength is radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, x rays and gamma rays.
Radio waves carry radio, television and cell phone signals.
Microwaves are used in microwave ovens to heat food and in radar and communication.
Infrared radiation is felt as heat; night vision cameras and remote controls use infrared.
Ultraviolet light from the Sun causes sunburn and skin cancer and helps the skin make vitamin D; sunscreen blocks it.
X rays pass through soft tissue but not bone, so they are used to make images of bones.
Gamma rays have the most energy and are used to kill cancer cells.
Lasers produce a narrow, intense beam of light of one color.
Fiber optic cables carry information as pulses of light through thin glass fibers by total internal reflection.
Cell phones, radios, Wi Fi and GPS send information using electromagnetic radio waves; information can be sent as digital signals of ones and zeros.
Digital signals are more reliable than analog signals because they are less affected by noise.
