# weathering, erosion and deposition (second set)
Weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces; erosion carries the pieces away; deposition drops them in a new place.
Weathering and erosion change igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary rock into sediment.
Mechanical weathering, also called physical weathering, breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of.
Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock into new substances, for example when acid rain dissolves limestone or iron in rock rusts.
Ice wedging, or frost wedging, happens when water seeps into cracks in rock, freezes, expands and pushes the crack wider.
Repeated freezing and thawing of water in cracks breaks rocks apart; this is most common in places where temperatures go above and below freezing.
Plant roots growing into cracks in rocks can split the rocks apart; this is mechanical weathering by living things.
Lichens and mosses growing on rocks give off weak acids that slowly break down the rock; this is chemical weathering by living things.
Animals that burrow, such as earthworms, ants, moles and prairie dogs, mix soil and break up rock.
Abrasion is weathering caused when rock is worn away by other rocks, sand or sediment carried by wind, water or ice.
Rocks tumbled in a river become smooth and rounded because they hit and rub against each other.
Pebbles on a beach are smooth and rounded because waves roll them against each other and against sand.
Wind blowing sand against rocks in a desert wears the rocks down and can carve arches and odd shapes.
Rust forms when iron in rocks reacts with oxygen and water; rocks containing iron may turn reddish brown.
Oxidation is a chemical reaction with oxygen, such as rusting of iron.
Carbonic acid forms when carbon dioxide dissolves in rainwater; it slowly dissolves limestone and forms caves.
Stalactites hang from cave ceilings and stalagmites grow up from cave floors as dripping water deposits calcite.
Weathering is fastest in warm, wet climates and slowest in cold, dry climates.
Chemical weathering is fastest where the climate is hot and rainy.
Mechanical weathering by ice is most common in cold climates with freezing and thawing.
Rock with more surface area weathers faster; breaking a rock into small pieces speeds up weathering.
Softer rocks such as sandstone and limestone weather faster than harder rocks such as granite.
Old gravestones made of marble become hard to read because acid rain slowly dissolves the marble.
Erosion is the movement of weathered rock and soil from one place to another by water, wind, ice or gravity.
Running water is the main cause of erosion on Earth's surface.
Rivers and streams erode their banks and beds and carry sediment downstream.
Fast moving water can carry larger pieces of sediment than slow moving water.
When a river slows down, it drops the heaviest sediment such as gravel first and carries fine clay the farthest.
Sediment settles out of water in layers, with the largest and heaviest particles settling to the bottom first.
A river flowing down a steep slope moves faster and erodes more than a river on a gentle slope.
A V shaped valley is carved by a river; a U shaped valley is carved by a glacier.
Canyons are deep, steep sided valleys carved by rivers over millions of years, such as the Grand Canyon carved by the Colorado River.
A meander is a wide curve or bend in a slow moving river.
An oxbow lake forms when a meander of a river is cut off from the main channel.
A floodplain is the flat land next to a river that is covered with water and fresh sediment during floods.
Floods deposit rich sediment on floodplains, which makes floodplain soil good for farming.
A delta is a fan shaped deposit of sediment that forms where a river enters a lake or ocean and slows down.
An alluvial fan is a fan shaped deposit of sediment that forms where a stream flows out of mountains onto flat land.
Glaciers are huge masses of moving ice that erode valleys, carry boulders and deposit rock and soil.
A glacier picks up rocks and drags them along, scratching and grinding the bedrock beneath it.
Moraines are ridges of rock and soil deposited by glaciers.
Erratics are large boulders carried far from where they formed by glaciers and left behind when the ice melted.
Glacial till is unsorted rock and soil deposited directly by a melting glacier.
Many lakes, such as the Great Lakes, were carved by glaciers during the last ice age.
Long Island and Cape Cod were formed from material deposited by glaciers.
Scratches or grooves in bedrock are evidence that a glacier once moved over the area.
Wind erosion is most common in dry places with few plants, such as deserts and plowed fields.
Sand dunes are hills of sand deposited by wind.
Loess is fine wind blown dust deposited in thick layers.
During the Dust Bowl of the 1930s, drought and poor farming practices let wind blow away huge amounts of topsoil.
Waves erode coastlines, forming cliffs, sea caves, sea arches and sea stacks.
Waves and currents deposit sand along coasts, forming beaches, spits, sandbars and barrier islands.
Longshore currents move sand along a beach parallel to the shore.
Jetties and groins are built to reduce beach erosion by trapping sand.
Gravity causes mass movement, the downhill movement of rock and soil, such as landslides, mudflows, rockfalls, slumps and creep.
Mudflows happen when heavy rain soaks loose soil on a steep slope.
Landslides are most likely on steep slopes with loose soil, few plants, and lots of rain or an earthquake.
Creep is the very slow downhill movement of soil, which can make fences, poles and trees tilt.
Plant roots hold soil in place and reduce erosion; removing plants increases erosion.
Ways to reduce soil erosion include planting trees and grass, planting cover crops, contour plowing, terracing, building windbreaks and leaving crop stubble in fields.
Contour plowing means plowing across a slope instead of up and down it, so furrows slow running water.
Terraces are flat steps cut into a hillside that slow water running downhill.
Windbreaks are rows of trees planted to slow the wind and reduce wind erosion.
Cutting down forests, overgrazing by animals, and construction that leaves soil bare increase erosion.
Retaining walls, riprap rocks and plants on slopes help prevent erosion along roads and shores.
Erosion and deposition can happen quickly, as in a flood or landslide, or slowly over millions of years.
Most of Earth's landforms are shaped by a combination of building up forces such as volcanoes and mountain building and wearing down forces such as weathering and erosion.
Constructive forces build up land, such as volcanoes, deposition and crustal deformation; destructive forces wear land down, such as weathering and erosion.
# soil (second set)
Soil is a mixture of weathered rock particles, minerals, humus, water, air and living organisms.
Soil forms very slowly; it can take hundreds to thousands of years to form a few centimeters of soil.
Soil forms from the weathering of bedrock and the decay of plants and animals.
Parent material is the rock that a soil forms from.
Soil horizons are layers of soil: topsoil (A horizon), subsoil (B horizon) and weathered rock (C horizon) above bedrock.
Topsoil is dark because it contains the most humus, and it is best for plant growth.
Humus is the dark material in soil formed from decayed plants and animals; it adds nutrients and helps soil hold water.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and worms break down dead matter into humus.
Soil particles from largest to smallest are gravel, sand, silt and clay.
Sandy soil has large particles and large pore spaces, so water drains through it quickly and it holds little water.
Clay soil has very small particles, holds a lot of water, drains slowly and can become hard when dry.
Loam is a soil with a mix of sand, silt, clay and humus; loam is the best soil for growing most plants.
Permeability is how easily water flows through soil or rock; sand and gravel are permeable, clay is not very permeable.
Porosity is the amount of open space between particles in soil or rock.
Good soil for gardens holds water and air, drains well and contains nutrients and humus.
Composting turns food scraps, leaves and grass clippings into humus that adds nutrients to soil.
Farmers rotate crops, planting different crops in a field in different years, so the soil does not lose all of the same nutrients.
Legumes such as beans, peas, peanuts, clover and alfalfa have bacteria in their roots that add nitrogen to soil.
Growing the same crop in the same field year after year uses up the soil's nutrients.
Fertilizers add nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium to soil.
# the rock cycle (second set)
The rock cycle is the series of processes by which rocks change from one type to another over long periods of time.
In the rock cycle, any type of rock can change into any other type.
Igneous rock can be weathered into sediment, changed by heat and pressure into metamorphic rock, or melted into magma.
Sedimentary rock can be changed by heat and pressure into metamorphic rock or melted into magma.
Metamorphic rock can be melted into magma or weathered and eroded into sediment.
Magma that cools and crystallizes forms igneous rock.
Sediments are compacted by the weight of layers above them and cemented by dissolved minerals to form sedimentary rock.
Compaction squeezes sediments together; cementation glues sediment grains together with minerals such as calcite and quartz.
Heat and pressure deep in Earth change rocks into metamorphic rock without melting them.
Metamorphic rocks often have bands or layers of minerals, called foliation, caused by pressure.
Gneiss is a banded metamorphic rock that can form from granite.
Schist is a metamorphic rock with shiny flat layers of mica.
Quartzite is a hard metamorphic rock formed from sandstone.
Marble is a metamorphic rock formed from limestone; slate is a metamorphic rock formed from shale.
Anthracite is a hard coal formed when coal is changed by heat and pressure.
Rocks can be classified by how they formed, and by their texture, grain size, mineral content and color.
The size of crystals in igneous rock depends on how quickly the magma or lava cooled.
Intrusive igneous rocks cool slowly underground and have large crystals, like granite.
Extrusive igneous rocks cool quickly at or near the surface and have small crystals, like basalt, or no crystals, like obsidian.
Rocks with holes from gas bubbles, such as pumice and scoria, formed from lava full of gas.
Fossils are found almost only in sedimentary rock because the heat of magma and the heat and pressure of metamorphism destroy remains.
Coal, limestone, sandstone, shale and conglomerate are common sedimentary rocks.
Chalk is a soft white sedimentary rock made of the tiny shells of ocean organisms.
Rock salt and gypsum form when salty water evaporates and leaves minerals behind; they are chemical sedimentary rocks.
Organic sedimentary rocks such as coal and some limestone form from the remains of living things.
Breccia is a sedimentary rock made of sharp angular broken rock pieces cemented together.
# minerals (second set)
A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a crystal structure and a definite chemical composition.
Minerals are the building blocks of rocks; a rock is a mixture of minerals.
Ice is a mineral, but coal is not a mineral because it formed from living things.
Color is the easiest property to observe but is often the least reliable for identifying a mineral because many minerals come in several colors.
Streak, the color of a mineral's powder, is more reliable than color for identifying a mineral.
Hematite has a reddish brown streak; pyrite has a greenish black streak even though it looks gold.
Luster is how a mineral's surface reflects light; it is described as metallic or nonmetallic such as glassy, pearly, silky, waxy or dull.
The Mohs scale ranks ten minerals from softest to hardest: talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, feldspar, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond.
A fingernail has a hardness of about 2.5, a copper penny about 3.5, a steel nail or knife about 5.5 and glass about 5.5.
A mineral that scratches glass is harder than 5.5; a mineral that can be scratched by a fingernail is softer than 2.5.
Specific gravity or density compares how heavy a mineral is for its size; gold and galena are very dense.
Some minerals have special properties: magnetite is magnetic, calcite fizzes in acid, halite tastes salty, and some minerals glow under ultraviolet light.
Galena is a heavy, shiny gray mineral that is the main ore of lead and breaks into cubes.
An ore is a rock or mineral that contains a useful metal that can be mined at a profit, such as iron ore or copper ore.
Bauxite is the ore of aluminum; hematite and magnetite are ores of iron.
Gemstones such as diamonds, rubies, sapphires and emeralds are minerals valued for their beauty, hardness and rarity.
Crystals grow larger when they form slowly.
Geodes are hollow rocks lined with crystals that grew from mineral rich water.
Quartz is one of the most common minerals in Earth's crust and is the main mineral in sand.
Feldspar is the most common mineral group in Earth's crust.
Mica splits into thin, flexible, see through sheets.
Graphite is used as pencil lead and as a lubricant because it is soft and slippery.
Talc is used to make baby powder because it is very soft.
Diamonds are used in drill bits and saw blades for cutting because they are the hardest mineral.
