# cells
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function of all living things.
All living things are made of one or more cells, and new cells come from existing cells.
Both plant cells and animal cells have a nucleus, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes and vacuoles.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole that animal cells do not have.
Animal cells have no cell wall and no chloroplasts.
The cell wall gives a plant cell support and a rigid shape.
Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll and are where photosynthesis takes place.
Mitochondria release energy from food through cellular respiration.
The nucleus controls the cell's activities and contains DNA.
The cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
Ribosomes make proteins.
Vacuoles store water, food and wastes.
Cytoplasm is the jelly-like fluid inside the cell where the organelles are found.
Bacteria are single-celled organisms without a nucleus.
Bacteria and other one-celled organisms are too small to see without a microscope.
Viruses are not made of cells and can reproduce only inside the living cells of a host.
Viruses are much smaller than bacteria.
Antibiotics kill bacteria but do not work against viruses.
A cell placed in salty water loses water by osmosis and shrinks.
A cell placed in fresh water gains water by osmosis and swells.
Plants wilt when their cells lose water.
Cells divide by mitosis to make two identical cells for growth and repair.
Meiosis makes sex cells, such as eggs and sperm, with half the number of chromosomes.
A group of similar cells working together is a tissue; tissues form organs; organs form organ systems; organ systems form an organism.
Multicellular organisms have specialized cells that do different jobs.
Nerve cells are long and branched to carry messages; muscle cells contract to cause movement.
Red blood cells carry oxygen; white blood cells fight disease; platelets help blood clot.
Root hair cells have a large surface area to absorb water.
# human body
The circulatory system, made of the heart, blood and blood vessels, carries oxygen and nutrients to cells and removes wastes.
The heart pumps blood through arteries, veins and capillaries.
Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart.
During exercise the heart beats faster to deliver more oxygen and nutrients to the muscles.
During exercise breathing rate increases to take in more oxygen and remove carbon dioxide.
The respiratory system takes in oxygen and gets rid of carbon dioxide through the lungs.
In the alveoli of the lungs, oxygen passes into the blood and carbon dioxide passes out.
The diaphragm is a muscle that helps the lungs breathe.
The respiratory and circulatory systems work together to bring oxygen to the cells.
The digestive system breaks down food into nutrients that the body can absorb.
Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth chew food and saliva begins to break it down.
The stomach uses acid and enzymes to break down food.
Most nutrients are absorbed into the blood in the small intestine.
The large intestine absorbs water from undigested food.
The digestive and circulatory systems work together to carry nutrients to the cells.
The skeletal system supports the body, protects organs, makes blood cells and works with muscles to allow movement.
The skull protects the brain and the rib cage protects the heart and lungs.
Muscles pull on bones to move them; muscles work in pairs.
Tendons connect muscles to bones; ligaments connect bones to bones.
Joints are places where bones meet and allow movement.
The nervous system, made of the brain, spinal cord and nerves, controls the body and responds to stimuli.
The brain receives messages from the senses through nerves.
The cerebrum controls thinking, memory and the senses; the cerebellum controls balance and coordination.
The brain stem controls breathing and heartbeat.
A reflex, such as pulling a hand away from a hot stove, is an automatic response.
The excretory system, including the kidneys, bladder, skin and lungs, removes wastes from the body.
The kidneys filter wastes from the blood to make urine.
The skin protects the body from germs, helps control body temperature by sweating and senses touch.
The endocrine system makes hormones that control growth, development and other body processes.
Insulin is a hormone that controls the level of sugar in the blood.
The immune system defends the body against germs that cause disease.
White blood cells and antibodies attack germs.
A vaccine helps the body make antibodies so that it can fight a disease in the future.
The reproductive system produces offspring.
Germs spread through coughing, sneezing, touching surfaces and unwashed hands.
Washing hands with soap and water is one of the best ways to prevent the spread of germs and disease.
A cut should be cleaned with soap and water and covered with a clean bandage to prevent infection.
Covering the mouth when coughing or sneezing keeps germs from spreading.
A healthy diet includes fruits, vegetables, whole grains and protein.
Carbohydrates such as bread and pasta give the body energy.
Proteins from meat, beans and eggs build and repair body tissues and muscles.
Calcium from milk helps build strong bones and teeth.
Regular exercise strengthens the heart, lungs and muscles.
Smoking damages the lungs and can cause lung cancer and heart disease.
Getting enough sleep helps the body and brain stay healthy.
# plants
Plants need sunlight, water, air (carbon dioxide) and nutrients to grow.
Photosynthesis uses light energy, carbon dioxide and water to make sugar (glucose) and oxygen.
Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the leaves of a plant.
Leaves take in carbon dioxide through small openings called stomata.
Stomata let carbon dioxide, oxygen and water vapor move in and out of the leaf.
Chlorophyll is the green pigment that captures light energy for photosynthesis.
A plant kept in the dark stops making chlorophyll, turns pale or yellow and eventually dies.
Roots absorb water and minerals from the soil and anchor the plant.
The stem supports the plant and carries water and food between the roots and the leaves.
Xylem carries water and minerals up from the roots to the leaves.
Phloem carries sugar made in the leaves to other parts of the plant.
Transpiration is the loss of water vapor from the leaves of plants.
Flowers are the reproductive parts of flowering plants.
The stamen is the male part of a flower and makes pollen.
The pistil is the female part of a flower; it contains the ovary where seeds form.
Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the stamen to the pistil.
Bees, butterflies, birds and bats pollinate flowers as they drink nectar.
Brightly colored petals and sweet smells attract pollinators.
After pollination and fertilization, the ovary grows into a fruit that contains seeds.
A seed contains a young plant (embryo) and stored food.
Seeds need water, air and the right temperature to germinate; most seeds do not need light to sprout.
Seeds are spread by wind, water, animals and by being shot out of pods.
Seeds with fluffy parts or wings, such as dandelion and maple seeds, are spread by the wind.
Seeds with hooks or burrs stick to animal fur and are carried to new places.
Animals eat fruits and spread the seeds in their droppings.
Coconuts float and are spread by water.
Ferns and mosses reproduce with spores instead of seeds.
Some plants reproduce asexually from runners, bulbs, tubers or cuttings, such as potatoes and strawberries.
Plants grow toward light; this response is called phototropism.
Roots grow downward in response to gravity; this response is called gravitropism or geotropism.
Many trees lose their leaves in fall to save water and energy during the cold winter.
Deciduous trees lose their leaves in the fall; evergreen trees such as pines keep their needles all year.
Plants store extra sugar as starch in roots, stems and seeds.
Carrots, beets and potatoes store food for the plant.
Cactus plants have thick stems that store water, spines instead of leaves and a waxy coating to reduce water loss.
Water lilies have wide floating leaves to catch sunlight.
# animals
Vertebrates have a backbone; invertebrates do not.
Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are vertebrates.
Insects, spiders, worms, jellyfish, snails, clams, crabs and sea stars are invertebrates.
Mammals have hair or fur, breathe with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young milk.
Whales, dolphins, bats and humans are mammals.
Most mammals give birth to live young.
Birds have feathers, wings, beaks, hollow bones, are warm-blooded and lay eggs with hard shells.
Reptiles such as snakes, lizards, turtles and crocodiles have dry scaly skin, breathe with lungs and lay eggs with leathery shells.
Amphibians such as frogs, toads and salamanders live part of their lives in water and part on land and have moist skin.
Amphibians lay eggs in water; young tadpoles breathe with gills and adult frogs breathe with lungs.
Fish live in water, breathe with gills, have scales and fins and most lay eggs.
Most fish lay many eggs in the water, where the eggs are fertilized outside the female's body.
Insects have six legs, three body parts (head, thorax and abdomen) and usually two antennae and wings.
Spiders have eight legs and two body parts and are arachnids, not insects.
Arthropods such as insects, spiders and crabs have an exoskeleton and jointed legs.
An exoskeleton is a hard outer covering that protects and supports the body.
Earthworms make tunnels that loosen the soil and let air and water reach plant roots.
Earthworms break down dead plants and add nutrients to the soil.
Warm-blooded animals keep a constant body temperature; cold-blooded animals' body temperature changes with the environment.
Reptiles bask in the sun to warm their bodies because they are cold-blooded.
Complete metamorphosis has four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult, as in butterflies, beetles and flies.
Incomplete metamorphosis has three stages: egg, nymph and adult, as in grasshoppers and dragonflies.
A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly or moth.
A tadpole changes into a frog through metamorphosis.
# behavior and adaptation
An adaptation is a body part or behavior that helps an organism survive in its environment.
Camouflage is coloring or shape that helps an animal blend in with its surroundings to hide from predators or prey.
Mimicry is when a harmless animal looks like a harmful one, such as a harmless fly that looks like a bee.
Bright warning colors on frogs, snakes and insects warn predators that they are poisonous.
Some animals hibernate in winter to save energy when food is scarce.
During hibernation an animal's heart rate, breathing and body temperature drop.
Some birds, butterflies and whales migrate to warmer places in winter to find food.
Animals migrate to find food, warmer weather or places to reproduce.
Many animals store food for the winter, such as squirrels burying nuts.
Some animals grow thicker fur in winter to stay warm.
Instinct is a behavior an animal is born knowing, such as a spider spinning a web or a bird building a nest.
Learned behavior is behavior an animal gets from experience or by being taught, such as a dog doing tricks.
Animals that live in groups, such as wolves and ants, can hunt or defend themselves better.
A bird's beak shape is adapted to the food it eats: short thick beaks crack seeds, long thin beaks reach nectar, hooked beaks tear meat.
Sharp teeth and claws are adaptations of predators for catching and eating prey.
Flat teeth are adaptations of plant eaters for grinding.
Thick fur and a layer of fat (blubber) help animals survive in cold places.
Large ears help desert animals such as the fennec fox lose heat.
Nocturnal animals are active at night and often have large eyes or a good sense of smell or hearing.
Webbed feet help ducks, frogs and beavers swim.
Animals that live in the desert are often active at night to avoid the heat.
# genetics and evolution
Offspring inherit traits from their parents through genes.
Genes are found on chromosomes in the nucleus and are made of DNA.
Inherited traits include eye color, hair color, blood type, height potential and the shape of leaves.
Acquired traits, such as scars, a tan, muscles from exercise or learning to read, are not inherited.
A dominant trait shows up when an organism has at least one dominant allele.
A recessive trait shows up only when an organism has two recessive alleles.
In a cross between two heterozygous parents (Aa x Aa), about three fourths of the offspring show the dominant trait and one fourth show the recessive trait.
A Punnett square predicts the possible traits of offspring.
Sexual reproduction combines genes from two parents, so offspring are different from each parent.
Asexual reproduction needs only one parent and produces offspring identical to the parent.
Variation among individuals in a population allows some to survive changes in the environment.
Natural selection means individuals with helpful traits are more likely to survive and reproduce and pass those traits to their offspring.
Bacteria become resistant to antibiotics because resistant bacteria survive and reproduce.
Insects become resistant to pesticides when the few resistant insects survive and reproduce.
Over many generations natural selection can change a species.
A mutation is a change in DNA that can produce a new trait.
Selective breeding is when people choose plants or animals with desired traits to reproduce, such as breeding dogs or crops.
Fossils show that many organisms that lived long ago are now extinct.
Fossils show how organisms have changed over long periods of time.
Similar bone structures in different animals suggest they share a common ancestor.
A species becomes extinct when all of its members die.
Species can become extinct because of habitat loss, climate change, new predators, disease or overhunting.
# ecology
An ecosystem includes all the living and nonliving things in an area that interact.
Biotic factors are living things such as plants, animals and bacteria.
Abiotic factors are nonliving things such as sunlight, water, temperature, air, rocks and soil.
A population is all the members of one species living in an area.
A community is all the populations of different species living in an area.
A habitat is the place where an organism lives and gets its food, water and shelter.
A niche is the role an organism plays in its ecosystem.
Producers, such as plants and algae, make their own food using sunlight.
Consumers get energy by eating other organisms.
Herbivores eat only plants; carnivores eat only animals; omnivores eat both plants and animals.
Decomposers such as bacteria, fungi and mushrooms break down dead organisms and wastes and return nutrients to the soil.
Scavengers such as vultures eat dead animals.
A food chain shows how energy passes from producers to consumers: grass, rabbit, fox.
A food chain always begins with a producer that gets energy from the Sun.
A food web shows many connected food chains in an ecosystem.
The arrows in a food chain point from the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it.
Only about ten percent of the energy at one level of a food chain passes to the next level; most is lost as heat.
There are fewer organisms at the top of a food chain because less energy is available.
If a predator is removed, the population of its prey usually increases at first.
If the prey population decreases, the predator population decreases because there is less food.
If producers die, the herbivores that eat them lose their food and their population decreases.
When a population grows too large, food runs out and many individuals starve.
Organisms compete for limited resources such as food, water, space, light and mates.
Plants compete for sunlight, water, space and nutrients from the soil.
Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support.
Limiting factors such as food, water, space and predators keep populations from growing too large.
Mutualism is a relationship in which both organisms benefit, such as bees and flowers.
Commensalism is a relationship in which one organism benefits and the other is not helped or harmed, such as barnacles on a whale.
Parasitism is a relationship in which one organism benefits and the other is harmed, such as a tick on a dog or a tapeworm.
Predation is when one animal hunts and eats another.
Succession is the gradual change of the community in an area over time.
After a forest fire, grasses and small plants grow first, then shrubs and finally trees.
Lichens and mosses are the first organisms to grow on bare rock and help make soil.
An invasive species is a species brought into a new area where it has no natural predators and harms native species.
Biodiversity is the variety of different species in an area.
Ecosystems with high biodiversity are more stable and recover better from changes.
Cutting down forests destroys habitats and reduces the number of species.
Destroying habitat is the main cause of species becoming endangered or extinct.
A biome is a large region with a particular climate and plants and animals, such as a desert, tundra, rain forest or grassland.
Climate, especially temperature and rainfall, determines the type of plants that grow in a biome.
Tropical rain forests are hot and wet all year and have the greatest variety of species.
Deserts receive very little rain and have plants such as cacti that store water.
The tundra is cold and treeless, with permanently frozen ground called permafrost.
Grasslands have rich soil and grasses with few trees.
Deciduous forests have four seasons and trees that lose their leaves in fall.
Coniferous forests (taiga) have cold winters and evergreen trees with needles.
Wetlands filter water, prevent flooding and provide habitats for many organisms.
Coral reefs are ocean ecosystems with many species that need warm, clear, shallow water.
Plankton are tiny organisms that float in water and are food for many sea animals.
