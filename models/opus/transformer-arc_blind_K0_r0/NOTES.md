# A hand-wired association scorer for ARC

Nothing here is trained or fitted. `build.py` turns hand-written text files into every weight of the given
2-layer transformer (`arch.py`, unchanged):

* `kb/*.txt` holds 2,610 lines of grade-school science knowledge. Each line is a group of words that belong
  together ("conduction touching contact direct solid metal spoon pan handle heat transfer"). Files headed
  `!gloss` (272 lines) define new vocabulary from core words without changing the core words.
* `words.txt` holds the stopwords, negation words (`except`, `not`, `least`), lower pooling weights for question
  words that carry little meaning ("observed", "seconds"...), irregular forms, and stems that must not be merged.
* `vocab.txt` is generated by `python build.py`: the KB words plus their inflections and stopwords (61k entries).

## Word vectors (the embedding)

Each KB line gets a pseudo-random unit code in each of 4 "views" of 12 dims. The code is seeded by the line's
text, so it's a fixed function of what I wrote. A word is **defined by its first 4 lines** (core files first):
its vector is the per-view normalized sum of those codes. When a later line mentions an already-capped word,
it doesn't dilute that word. Instead, the other words on the line take in 2.5x the capped word's vector, so
they still relate to it. Without this cap, frequent words ("water" is in 163 lines) average into noise and new
lines dilute everything. Adding the cap raised my dev accuracy from about 58% to 62%. Inflections (plurals,
-ed, -ing, -y, -ness, ...) share their stem's vector.

## What the network computes

The residual stream is used as named features. They're coordinates in an orthonormal basis of the zero-sum
subspace of R^64, so LayerNorm's mean is 0. A constant feature of 40 dominates the norm, so LayerNorm is a
near-fixed rescaling, undone by LN weights C/8. Everything is then linear and can be read and written
exactly. Per-word features: content flag, per-view flags, negation flag, pooling weight, |Pv|^2.
Position features: option positions (128+) and a recency ramp over question positions.

* **Layer 0 (association), one head per view.** Every question word q attends over the option's content words
  with logit `5 cos_view(q, o) + 5 (1 - 0.35)`. Its sink is the question's own content words: q itself has
  logit 5, and their value is 0. The value is 1 on option words, so each head is a saturating soft count
  (0..1) of option words associated with q. Function words are masked. G = sum over the 4 views.
* **Layer 1.**
  * Head 0 has a constant query. It averages G over the question's content words (recency and word weight)
    into F1, which every token then carries.
  * Head 1 sets NN if the question has a negation word.
  * Head 3 is a sharp exact-repeat detector: logit `60 (|Pq|^2 - |Pq - Pk|^2)`, with P the sum of the views.
    It sets DUP on question words that the option merely repeats. A repeat is the strongest "association", but
    in ARC it's mostly a distractor's trick.
* **MLP 1** flips F1 when NN is on, with one gated GELU unit: F1 * (1 - 2 NN). "Which is NOT ..." becomes
  "which is least associated". On my 15 NOT/EXCEPT questions this gives 66% vs 10% without the flip.
* **Readout** (mean over tokens after the final LN): `10 F1 - 150 DUP + 5 * (option's share of the tokens)`.
  The last term is a mild preference for longer, more specific options.

## Why this design, and how it was checked

The binding constraint is about 48 numbers per word and a rank-16 bilinear form per head. An unlimited-dimension
version of the same scorer (exact line-overlap cosines) gets about 70% on my questions. Linear rank-48 embeddings
(SVD or random) get about 50%, because the KB has no low-rank structure. Thresholding inside each head's softmax
recovers most of that gap.

Choices were made by hand, comparing a few variants on about 330 questions I wrote myself (kept outside the
model folder), averaged over 6 code seeds. These variants were all worse: option words querying the question,
sparse one-view codes, 3 wider views with an in-layer repeat cancel, down- or up-weighting frequent words,
length-weighted lines, iterated or pure hub vectors, a hand-written definitions file, and reshaping G.
The final model gets 61% on all my sets and 54% on the last set, which I wrote after most tuning.
Real ARC is harder than my questions, especially the Challenge half, so expect less there.
