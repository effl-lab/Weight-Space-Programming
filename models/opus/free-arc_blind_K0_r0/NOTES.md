# A hand-written retrieval model for ARC science questions

Nothing here is trained or fitted, and nothing is pretrained. All the knowledge is in plain-text files I
wrote. `build.py` turns those files into sparse matrices using fixed formulas, and `arch.py` is a fixed
tensor program that scores each option against them.

## What the model knows (files I wrote)

- `kb/*.txt`: about 4,150 science facts, one per line, grouped in `#` sections. They cover earth science
  (rocks, weathering, plate tectonics, the water cycle, weather, climate), space, cells, plants, animals,
  ecology, the human body, genetics and evolution, matter and chemistry, energy, electricity and magnetism,
  forces and machines, waves, light and sound, natural resources and pollution, scientific inquiry, tools,
  units and lab safety, scientists, and materials and engineering design. The phrasing is aimed at how
  grade-school tests ask things: definitions ("glossary"), "examples of X", functions, causes and effects,
  advantages and disadvantages, directional relationships ("as X increases, Y ..."), everyday observations
  with their explanations, and short "crisp" restatements of the most-tested facts.
- `synonyms.txt`: irregular forms (grew→grow, leaves→leaf), concepts merged by hand (orbit=revolve,
  sunlight=sun), and forced keys (`=organism`) where the Porter stemmer would wrongly merge words
  (organism/organ, general/generate).
- `stopwords.txt`: function words and question-frame words ("which", "best", "describes").
- `isa.txt`: categories, e.g. steel → metal, conductor, solid; frog → amphibian.
- `vocab.txt`: generated by my script `devtools/make_vocab.py`. It holds every word in the files above plus
  inflected and possessive forms that reduce to the same concept, so "insect's" or "evaporates" still
  match. (The tokenizer keeps apostrophes, so possessives would otherwise be unknown words.)

## Weights (build.py)

- `concept[word id]`: the word's concept, which is its Porter stem after the synonym table (my own Porter
  implementation is in `textproc.py`). Stopwords map to the null concept 0.
- `idf[concept] = log(1 + N/df)`, counted over the facts.
- `F` (facts × concepts, sparse 0/1): one row per fact. If a clause contains "not", "never" and so on, the
  words after the negator are dropped, so "the Moon does not make its own light" can't support "the Moon
  makes light".
- `Fw`: windows of 4 consecutive content words of each fact, which rewards words that appear close together.
- `Fp`: paragraph rows, each fact together with its neighbours in the same section. This links "After a
  forest fire, grasses grow first…" to the neighbouring fact that names it *succession*.
- `F_norm`, `Fw_norm`, `Fp_norm` = (idf mass of the row)^-0.35, a mild penalty on long rows.
- `Et`: the isa expansion matrix. `FP`, `dir_of`: ordered pairs of "directional" concepts (energy forms and
  states of matter) for each fact, e.g. chemical→light for a flashlight.
- Small indicator vectors over word ids: negators, wh-words, "except", "least likely", absolute words
  ("only", "always", "never"), and transformation cue words.

## Forward pass (arch.py)

1. **Question bag.** Each question concept is weighted by its idf, and the last 14 tokens (the actual
   question) count double.
2. **Option bags.** Option concepts that also appear in the question are removed. Each option's idf mass
   `m` scales its support by `(m+1)^-0.6`.
3. **Discrimination.** A question word that links equally strongly to all four options (for example
   "simple machine" when every option is a machine) can't tell them apart. Each question word is
   re-weighted by how unevenly it links to the options, keeping a floor of 0.6.
4. **Support.** For every fact row:
   `support = (question idf matched) × (option idf matched) × row norm`.
   This product means a fact only counts if it shares words with both the question and the option.
   An option's score is its best fact, plus 0.15 × its next four facts, plus 0.5 × its best window, plus
   0.3 × its best paragraph. Category expansion through `isa.txt` is only used for options whose words
   are absent from the facts.
5. **Word order.** For energy-transformation and state-change questions (gated by cue words such as
   "change", "into", "from"), a fact's support is multiplied by `exp(0.7 × (agreeing − reversed ordered
   pairs))`. This separates "chemical → light" from "light → chemical", and evaporation from condensation.
6. **One hop.** The top 3 facts for the question alone are found. Their words are added to the question
   (minus all option words), and everything is scored again; this second pass is added to the first. It
   bridges steps such as "bright light in a thunderstorm" → lightning → static electricity.
7. **Priors.**
   - Options worded with "only", "always" or "never" lose 10%: on grade-school tests, absolute wording
     usually marks a distractor.
   - Negated questions flip the sign of the score, so the least-supported option wins. A question counts
     as negated when a wh-word shortly precedes "not", or when it contains "except" or "least likely".
     The wh-word condition is there so scenario text like "the box does not move" doesn't trigger it.
   - A 1e-4 × option length term breaks exact ties.

## Why this design

With no training, the only way to answer is to find stated knowledge that connects a question to an
option. A sparse retrieval model makes every number traceable to a sentence I wrote. Every refinement
above came from failure categories I saw on my own made-up questions:
- generic words dominating a rare discriminating word;
- answers that sit in a neighbouring sentence;
- needing one inference step;
- direction of change;
- negation;
- possessives and inflections.

## How I checked it

The only evaluation used is `devtools/myq.txt`: about 460 questions I made up, in eight batches. I wrote
most batches before adding the knowledge they need. On the full set the model scores about 85%. The
batches that were fresh when written scored 67–83% at that point.

Parameters were set by hand and checked against these questions. Accuracy is flat over reasonable
values, and every component helps on the later batches.

The real ARC set is harder than my own questions. Distractors there are worded to share words with the
right answer, and Challenge questions were selected because retrieval and word co-occurrence methods fail
on them. So I expect real accuracy to be clearly lower than on my questions, especially on Challenge.

## Rebuilding

After editing `kb/`, `synonyms.txt`, `stopwords.txt` or `isa.txt`, run
`python /srv/work/devtools/make_vocab.py` to regenerate `vocab.txt`. `build()` takes about 10 s, and the
forward pass takes about 8 ms per question on 4 cores with under 1 GB of memory.
