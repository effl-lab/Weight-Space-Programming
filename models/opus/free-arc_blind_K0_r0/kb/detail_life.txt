# plants in detail
Monocots such as grasses, corn, lilies and palms have one seed leaf, parallel leaf veins and flower parts in threes.
Dicots such as beans, oaks, roses and maples have two seed leaves, branching leaf veins and flower parts in fours or fives.
Cotyledons are seed leaves that store food for the young plant.
The epidermis is the outer protective layer of a leaf.
The palisade layer of a leaf has many chloroplasts and is where most photosynthesis occurs.
Stomata usually close at night and during hot dry weather to reduce water loss.
Transpiration pulls water up through the xylem from the roots to the leaves.
Plants lose most of their water through transpiration from their leaves.
The rate of photosynthesis increases with more light, more carbon dioxide and warmer temperatures, up to a limit.
Plants carry out respiration all the time, day and night, but photosynthesis only in the light.
During the day plants give off more oxygen than they use; at night they give off carbon dioxide.
Starch is the form in which plants store glucose; iodine turns starch blue black.
Plants use the sugar from photosynthesis for energy and to build cellulose for cell walls.
Wood is made of layers of xylem; each year a tree adds a new growth ring.
Bark protects a tree's trunk from insects, disease and injury.
Tree rings can be counted to find the age of a tree.
Plants absorb nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium as minerals dissolved in soil water.
Legumes such as peas, beans, clover and alfalfa have bacteria in root nodules that add nitrogen to the soil.
Farmers rotate crops with legumes to add nitrogen back to the soil.
Carnivorous plants like the Venus flytrap and pitcher plant trap insects to get nitrogen because they live in poor soil.
Some plants such as dandelions and maples spread seeds by wind; burdock and cocklebur seeds stick to fur.
Birds eat berries and drop the seeds in new places.
Water lilies, cattails and duckweed are plants adapted to living in water.
Epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on other plants to reach sunlight in rain forests.
Vines climb trees to reach sunlight.
Mangrove trees can grow in salt water along tropical coasts.
Tundra plants are small and grow close to the ground to avoid cold winds.
Seeds of some pine trees open only after a forest fire.
Pollen from one plant fertilizing another plant of the same species is cross pollination.
Self pollination happens when pollen fertilizes the same flower or plant.
Plants produce many seeds because most seeds do not survive to grow into new plants.
Fruits and vegetables are plant parts: lettuce and spinach are leaves, celery and asparagus are stems, carrots and radishes are roots, broccoli and cauliflower are flowers, and peas and corn kernels are seeds.
Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, pumpkins and squash are fruits because they contain seeds.
# animals in detail
Animals are multicellular consumers that cannot make their own food.
Most animals have body symmetry; sea stars have radial symmetry and humans have bilateral symmetry.
Mammals have a four chambered heart, as do birds.
Amphibians and most reptiles have a three chambered heart; fish have a two chambered heart.
Birds' feathers keep them warm, help them fly and can attract mates.
Birds have air sacs and hollow bones that make flying easier.
Birds lay eggs with hard shells; reptile eggs have leathery shells; amphibian and fish eggs are soft and laid in water.
A bird's egg has a shell, yolk, albumen or egg white, and an embryo.
Penguins use their wings as flippers to swim.
Ostriches cannot fly but run very fast.
Snakes shed their skin as they grow.
Turtles and tortoises have shells made of bone covered by scales.
Lizards can lose their tails to escape predators and later regrow them.
Frogs absorb water and oxygen through their thin moist skin.
Frogs are sensitive to pollution because they absorb chemicals through their skin, so they are indicators of environmental health.
Salmon hatch in fresh water streams, live in the ocean and return to fresh water to spawn.
Fish have a lateral line that senses vibrations in the water.
A swim bladder helps a fish control its depth in water.
Sharks have an excellent sense of smell and can sense electricity from prey.
Whales breathe air and must come to the surface to breathe through blowholes.
Baleen whales filter tiny animals called krill from the water.
Dolphins live in groups called pods and communicate with clicks and whistles.
Bats are nocturnal mammals; many eat insects and some eat fruit and pollinate flowers.
Elephants are the largest land animals; blue whales are the largest animals ever.
Cheetahs are the fastest land animals.
Hummingbirds have the fastest heartbeat of birds and hover by flapping their wings quickly.
Honeybees live in colonies with a queen, workers and drones; worker bees collect nectar and pollen.
Ants and termites live in colonies and divide work among members.
Spiders produce silk to make webs that catch insects.
Mosquito larvae live in standing water, so removing standing water reduces mosquitoes.
Insects have compound eyes made of many lenses.
Insects that eat crops, such as locusts and aphids, are called pests.
Ladybugs eat aphids and help farmers control pests.
Earthworms have no lungs and breathe through their skin, which must stay moist.
Octopuses have no bones and can squeeze through small openings; they squirt ink to escape predators.
Jellyfish have stinging cells to capture prey.
Coral reefs are built by colonies of tiny animals called coral polyps, which have algae living inside them.
Coral reefs are damaged by warmer ocean water, pollution and acidification, which causes coral bleaching.
Sea stars can regenerate lost arms.
Arctic animals such as polar bears, arctic foxes, walruses and seals have thick fur or blubber.
Desert animals such as camels, tortoises, rattlesnakes, scorpions and kangaroo rats conserve water.
Rain forest animals include monkeys, sloths, toucans, jaguars, parrots and tree frogs.
Grassland animals include bison, prairie dogs, zebras, lions, antelope and gazelles.
Forest animals in the eastern United States include deer, black bears, squirrels, raccoons, owls, foxes and woodpeckers.
Ocean animals include fish, whales, dolphins, sharks, jellyfish, octopuses, sea turtles and crabs.
Pond animals include frogs, turtles, ducks, fish, dragonflies and water striders.
# human body in detail
The heart pumps blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen and then pumps it to the rest of the body.
The right side of the heart pumps blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygen rich blood to the body.
Blood returning to the heart in veins carries carbon dioxide from the cells.
Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing on the walls of arteries.
Valves in the heart and veins keep blood flowing in one direction.
The average adult heart beats about 70 times per minute at rest.
The lungs contain millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli surrounded by capillaries.
When the diaphragm contracts and moves down, air is pulled into the lungs; when it relaxes, air is pushed out.
Breathing rate increases during exercise because muscles need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide.
The epiglottis covers the windpipe when you swallow so food goes into the esophagus.
Peristalsis is the wave of muscle contractions that moves food through the digestive tract.
Digestion changes large food molecules into small molecules that can be absorbed into the blood.
The pancreas makes digestive enzymes and the hormone insulin.
The gallbladder stores bile made by the liver.
The appendix is a small pouch attached to the large intestine.
Food takes about one to three days to pass through the digestive system.
The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain; it controls thinking, learning, memory and the senses.
The cerebellum coordinates balance and muscle movement.
The brain stem controls automatic functions such as breathing and heartbeat.
Sensory neurons carry messages from sense organs to the brain; motor neurons carry messages from the brain to muscles.
Hearing happens when sound waves make the eardrum vibrate, and the vibrations pass through tiny bones to the inner ear.
Taste buds on the tongue detect sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savory tastes.
The skin has nerve endings that sense touch, pressure, heat, cold and pain.
Cartilage is a flexible tissue found in the nose, ears and between bones in joints.
The femur, or thigh bone, is the longest bone in the human body.
Muscles can only pull, not push, so they work in pairs such as the biceps and triceps.
Voluntary muscles are controlled consciously; involuntary muscles such as the heart work automatically.
Exercise makes muscles stronger and larger.
The skin produces vitamin D when exposed to sunlight.
Melanin is the pigment that gives skin its color and protects against ultraviolet light.
The thyroid gland controls how fast the body uses energy, its metabolism.
The pituitary gland controls growth and other glands and is called the master gland.
The adrenal glands release adrenaline during stress or danger, making the heart beat faster.
Diabetes is a disease in which the body does not make or use insulin properly, so blood sugar is too high.
The immune system remembers germs it has fought, so a person usually does not get some diseases twice.
Vaccines contain weakened or dead germs or parts of germs that train the immune system.
Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites.
Colds and flu are caused by viruses and spread through coughs, sneezes and touching surfaces.
Athlete's foot and ringworm are caused by fungi.
Malaria is caused by a parasite spread by mosquitoes.
Heart disease is linked to smoking, poor diet, lack of exercise and high blood pressure.
Lung cancer and emphysema are linked to smoking.
Drinking alcohol and using drugs can damage the liver, brain and other organs.
A healthy diet, exercise, sleep and avoiding tobacco help prevent disease.
# genetics and reproduction in detail
Genes are made of DNA and code for proteins that determine traits.
Each body cell contains a complete copy of the organism's DNA.
DNA copies itself before a cell divides, so each new cell gets the same DNA.
A karyotype is a picture of all the chromosomes in a cell.
Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21.
Some genetic disorders such as cystic fibrosis and sickle cell anemia are caused by recessive alleles.
A carrier has one recessive allele for a trait but does not show the trait.
Two brown eyed parents can have a blue eyed child if both carry a recessive allele for blue eyes.
If both parents are heterozygous, there is a 25 percent chance their child shows the recessive trait.
If one parent is homozygous dominant, all offspring will show the dominant trait.
In incomplete dominance, offspring show a blend of traits, such as red and white flowers producing pink flowers.
In codominance, both alleles are expressed, such as a chicken with black and white feathers.
Many traits such as height and skin color are controlled by many genes.
The environment can affect how genes are expressed; a plant with tall genes stays short without enough light or nutrients.
Hydrangea flower color depends on the acidity of the soil.
Identical twins raised apart can differ in traits affected by environment.
Breeders use selective breeding to produce cows that give more milk and crops with bigger fruit.
Genetically modified crops can resist insects, drought or disease.
DNA fingerprinting is used to identify people and solve crimes.
Offspring from sexual reproduction get a combination of traits from both parents.
In animals, fertilization can be internal, inside the female's body, or external, outside the body in water.
Fish and frogs usually use external fertilization in water.
Mammals, birds and reptiles use internal fertilization.
Organisms that produce many offspring increase the chance that some will survive.
